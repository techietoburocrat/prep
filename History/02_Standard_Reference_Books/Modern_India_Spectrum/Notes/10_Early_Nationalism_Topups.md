# 🏛️ PART A — BEFORE THE CONGRESS

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Factors in the growth of modern nationalism — Spectrum's own eight

⭐ **Class 8 gives you the conclusion — "what is this country for" — Spectrum gives you the eight-point causal chain that a Mains answer can actually cite.**

| # | Factor |
|:--:|---|
| 1 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Political, administrative and economic unification of the country** — a bigger single state than the Mauryas or Mughals had ever ruled, with one civil service, one judiciary, one code of law |
| 2 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Western thought and education** — Milton, Shelley, J.S. Mill, Rousseau, Paine, Spencer, Voltaire; English as a common link-language across regions |
| 3 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Role of the press and literature** — **169 vernacular newspapers by 1877**, circulation ~1,00,000 |
| 4 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Rediscovery of India's past** — Max Müller, Monier Williams, R.G. Bhandarkar, Vivekananda; the "Indo-Aryan" theory gave educated Indians a psychological counter to colonial myths of servility |
| 5 | ⭐⭐ **The progressive character of socio-religious reform movements** *(cross-link: [Spectrum file 05](05_Reform_Movements.md))* |
| 6 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Rise of a middle-class intelligentsia** — Percival Spear's phrase: **"a well-integrated all-India class... a minority, but a DYNAMIC minority"** |
| 7 | ⭐⭐ **Impact of contemporary world movements** — the new South American nations, Greek and Italian and (especially) Irish liberation |
| 8 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Reactionary policies and racial arrogance of the rulers** — Lytton's **1876 cut in the ICS entry age from 21 to 19**, the Delhi Durbar of 1877 held amid famine, the **Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act (1878)**, and the **Ilbert Bill (1883)** |

> ⚠️⭐⭐⭐ **The ICS age-cut of 1876 is the one item on this list Class 8 never mentions at all** — its "three provocations" run Arms Act → Vernacular Press Act → Ilbert Bill only. **Spectrum adds a fourth, earlier provocation, and it is the one the Indian Association's first campaign (1877) was actually fought over.**

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Political associations before the Congress — the full roster

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This entire section is new. Class 8 names five associations with no dates or founders; Spectrum gives the complete regional picture.**

### Bengal strand

| Year | Association | Founder / detail |
|:--:|---|---|
| **1836** | **Bangabhasha Prakasika Sabha** | Associates of **Raja Rammohan Roy** |
| — | **Zamindari Association** *("Landholders' Society")* | To safeguard landlord interests — **marks the beginning of organised constitutional agitation** |
| **1843** | **Bengal British India Society** | *"Collection and dissemination of information... to secure the welfare... of all classes of our fellow subjects"* |
| **1851** | ⭐⭐⭐ **British Indian Association** | **Merger of the two above.** Petitioned for: (i) a popular separate legislature, (ii) separation of executive from judiciary, (iii) reduced official salaries, (iv) abolition of salt/abkari/stamp duties — **partially conceded** by the **Charter Act of 1853**, which added six members to the governor-general's council |
| **1866** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **East India Association** | **Dadabhai Naoroji**, in **London** — to lobby British public opinion; later opened Indian branches |
| **1875** | **Indian League** | **Sisir Kumar Ghosh** — *"stimulating the sense of nationalism"* |
| **1876** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Indian Association of Calcutta** *(Indian National Association)* | **Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose** — superseded the Indian League. **Fought the 1877 ICS age-limit cut**, low membership fee to attract poorer members, ran an **all-India conference (Calcutta, Dec 1883)** with 100+ delegates — *"a forerunner of the INC"*. **Merged into the INC in 1886** |

### Bombay and Madras strands

| Year | Association | Founder |
|:--:|---|---|
| **1867** | **Poona Sarvajanik Sabha** | **M.G. Ranade** and others |
| **1885** | **Bombay Presidency Association** | **Badruddin Tyabji, Pherozshah Mehta, K.T. Telang** |
| **1884** | **Madras Mahajan Sabha** | **M. Viraraghavachari, B. Subramaniya Aiyer, P. Anandacharlu** |

### Pre-Congress campaigns — eleven items

⭐⭐⭐ Import duty on cotton (1875) · Indianisation of government service (1878-79) · against Lytton's Afghan adventure · **against the Arms Act (1878)** · **against the Vernacular Press Act (1878)** · for the right to join volunteer corps · against plantation labour conditions and the Inland Emigration Act · in support of the **Ilbert Bill** · for an All-India Fund for Political Agitation · a campaign in Britain for a pro-India party · and **the Indian Civil Service age-limit agitation** — led by the Indian Association.

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Foundation of the Congress and its three theories

⭐ **Delta over Class 8:** the exact founding logistics, and — the genuinely new part — **the three competing theories of why Congress exists at all.**

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **First session** | **December 1885**, **Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay** — 72 delegates, presided by **Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee** |
| **Prelude** | Two sessions of the **Indian National Conference** (1883, 1885) — architects **Surendranath Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose** |
| **A.O. Hume's role** | A **retired English civil servant** who gave final shape to the idea and mobilised the leading intellectuals |
| **1890 milestone** | ⭐⭐ **Kadambini Ganguly**, the first woman graduate of Calcutta University, **addressed the Congress session** |

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Was it a "Safety Valve"? — the three theories

| Theory | Held by | The claim |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Safety Valve theory** | **Lala Lajpat Rai** *(an Extremist himself)* | Hume formed Congress, with Viceroy **Dufferin's** tacit cooperation, to **release growing Indian discontent before it turned into a popular uprising** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Conspiracy theory** | **R.P. Dutt** *(Marxist)* | A more extreme version — Congress was born **out of a deliberate conspiracy to abort a popular uprising**, with the bourgeois leaders complicit |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Lightning Conductor theory** | **Bipan Chandra**, citing the early leaders' own view | Modern historians dispute "safety valve": Indians could not have convened an all-India body on their own against official opposition, so early leaders **used Hume as a "lightning conductor"** — a catalyst and a shield, not a controller |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is a name-the-theory Prelims trap waiting to happen — three named theories, three named holders, and they are often confused with each other in options.**

### Aims and objectives of the Congress — eight points

⭐⭐⭐ Found a democratic nationalist movement · politically educate the people · establish a movement's headquarters · build friendly relations among nationalist workers across regions · develop an anti-colonial ideology · unify popular demands into a common programme · build **national unity across religion, caste and province** · nurture Indian nationhood.

# ⚖️ PART B — THE MODERATES' METHOD, AND THE INDIAN COUNCILS ACT 1892

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The Indian Councils Act 1892 — provisions and limitations

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is a genuine gap — Class 8 never names this Act at all, though it is the Moderates' one concrete institutional win before 1909.**

Legislative councils had **no real power till 1920**. The **Indian Councils Act (1861)** had created an Imperial Legislative Council that was, in Spectrum's words, *"an impotent body designed to disguise official measures as having been passed by a representative body"* — only **45 Indians nominated in thirty years (1862-92)**, mostly loyalist landed gentry (Syed Ahmed Khan, Kristodas Pal among the exceptions). From 1885-92 the nationalist demand was two-fold: **expansion** of councils and **reform** of councils (more power, especially over finance).

| | Indian Councils Act, 1892 |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Provisions** | Additional non-official members raised (Imperial council: 10-16, up from 6-10); **non-officials nominated by chambers of commerce, universities, municipalities, zamindars** — *the principle of representation introduced*; **budget could be discussed**; **questions could be asked** |
| ⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Limitations** | Officials **retained their majority**; the reformed council met on average **only 13 days a year**, with just **5 of 24 non-official Indian seats** typically present; **the budget could not be voted on or amended**; **no supplementary questions, no debate on answers** |

⭐⭐ By **1904-06**, Naoroji, Gokhale and Tilak in turn demanded **self-government on the lines of Canada or Australia** — the demand that would define the next phase.

> 🎯 **The verdict in one line:** the councils were meant to let nationalists **"let off political steam"** while remaining deaf to it — but the nationalists **turned them into forums for exposing bureaucratic defects anyway**, even without real power. **A Ranade quotation to Gokhale (1891) makes the point directly:** *"This work must be done for many years, without expecting any other results, because politics of this kind is altogether new in this land."*

⭐ **Moderate methods beyond the councils:** Indianisation of services (economic + political-drain + moral grounds), separation of judiciary from executive, criticism of aggressive frontier wars (Burma, Afghanistan), welfare/education spending, better treatment for Indian labour abroad, and defence of civil rights — the outrage at **Tilak's 1897 sedition arrest** and the **Natu brothers' deportation without trial** built the civil-liberties strand of Moderate politics.

⚠️ **Government hostility, regardless of moderation:** Curzon (1900) — *"The Congress is tottering to its fall, and one of my great ambitions while in India is to assist it to a peaceful demise."* Dufferin called it **"a factory of sedition."** The government's counter-move was the **United Indian Patriotic Association**, propped up under Syed Ahmed Khan and Raja Shiv Prasad Singh — an early instance of the **divide-and-rule** strategy that would recur through Morley-Minto.

# 🔥 PART C — WHY NATIONALISM TURNED MILITANT, AND THE SWADESHI YEARS

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The nine causes of militant nationalism — a DIFFERENT list from Class 8's WWI-effects

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Do not confuse this with Class 8's "five effects of WWI that made nationalism a MASS movement after 1919."** That is a different, later transformation. **This is the earlier one — why a section of the Congress itself turned militant between 1890 and 1905, well before the war.**

| # | Cause |
|:--:|---|
| 1 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Recognition of British rule's true, exploitative nature** — specific repressive steps: **1897 Natu deportation + Tilak's sedition arrest; 1898 IPC 124A tightened via new Section 156A; 1899 Indian seats cut in Calcutta Corporation; 1904 Official Secrets Act and Indian Universities Act** *(which openly called universities "factories producing political revolutionaries")* |
| 2 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Growth of confidence and self-respect** — Tilak, Aurobindo and B.C. Pal's repeated calls on self-reliance |
| 3 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Growth of education** — rising literacy alongside rising **educated unemployment**, sharpening economic discontent |
| 4 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **International influences** — **Japan's post-1868 rise**, Ethiopia's defeat of Italy (1896), the **Boer Wars (1899-1902)** exposing British vulnerability, and above all **Japan's 1905 victory over Russia** — *"demolished myths of European invincibility"* |
| 5 | ⭐⭐⭐ **Reaction to excessive westernisation** — Vivekananda, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Dayananda Saraswati recast India's past in confident colours |
| 6 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Dissatisfaction with the Moderates** — the **"Three P's"** (prayer, petition, protest) dismissed as **"political mendicancy"** |
| 7 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Curzon's reactionary rule** — *"letting off of gas"*; the Official Secrets Act, Indian Universities Act, Calcutta Corporation Act, and, above all, **Partition of Bengal** |
| 8 | ⭐⭐ **Existence of a militant school of thought** — Raj Narain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Datta, Aurobindo Ghosh, B.C. Pal *(Bengal)*; Chiplunkar, **Tilak** *(Maharashtra)*; **Lajpat Rai** *(Punjab)* |
| 9 | ⭐⭐ **Emergence of a trained leadership** ready to channel the released mass energy |

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Tilak's own line captures the movement's self-understanding:** *"The Extremists of today will be the Moderates of tomorrow, just as the Moderates of today were the Extremists of yesterday."*

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The Moderates vs the Extremists — the nine-point comparison

| | Moderates | Extremists |
|:--:|---|---|
| **1. Social base** | Zamindars, upper-middle classes in towns | Educated middle/lower-middle classes |
| **2. Inspiration** | Western liberal thought, European history | Indian history, cultural heritage, Hindu symbols |
| **3. On Britain** | Believed in Britain's **"providential mission"** | Rejected it as illusion |
| **4. On the imperial link** | Political connection served India's interests | It only perpetuated exploitation |
| **5. Loyalty** | Professed loyalty to the Crown | Crown **"unworthy of claiming Indian loyalty"** |
| **6. On the masses** | Not yet ready for political work | Immense faith in mass sacrifice |
| **7. Goal** | Constitutional reform, share in services | **Swaraj** as the panacea |
| **8. Methods** | Constitutional only | Boycott, passive resistance — extra-constitutional |
| **9. Self-view** | Patriots, no comprador role | Patriots who sacrificed for the country |

⚠️⭐⭐⭐ **A sharper, less comfortable point Spectrum makes and Class 8 doesn't:** the Extremists were **politically progressive but socially reactionary** — Tilak's **opposition to the Age of Consent Bill** *(raising the marriage age from 10 to 12; his stated objection was procedural — reform must come from self-rule, not an alien government — but the effect was obstruction)*, his Ganapati/Shivaji festivals, and B.C. Pal's and Aurobindo's talk of **"Hindu nation and Hindu interests"** all had the side effect of **alienating Muslims from the movement** — a genuine seed of what followed.

## 7️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Swadeshi's mass base, and its end — beyond Class 8's narrative

⭐ Class 8 tells the Swadeshi story well; Spectrum adds the **social breakdown of who actually joined**, and the movement's formal close.

| Group | Role |
|---|---|
| **Students** | Led picketing; faced disaffiliation, expulsion, scholarship bans |
| **Women** | *"traditionally home-centred... took active part in processions and picketing"* — a durable shift |
| **Muslims** | **Mostly stayed away** — the elite backed the partition (a Muslim-majority East Bengal), and Hindu festival-based mobilisation excluded them; the **All India Muslim League itself was "propped up" on 30 December 1905 partly as an anti-Congress front** |
| **Labour** | Scattered strikes — Burn Company (Howrah, 1905), East Indian Railway (1906, → a Railwaymen's Union), Bengal jute mills (1906-08), Tuticorin/Tirunelveli under Chidambaram Pillai and Subramania Siva, Rawalpindi under Lajpat Rai/Ajit Singh — **subsided by summer 1908 under state pressure** |

⭐⭐⭐ **Annulment, 1911:** the partition was reversed **mainly to defuse revolutionary terrorism** — Bihar/Orissa detached from Bengal, Assam made separate, and the **capital shifted to Delhi** as a sop to Muslims (who were, in the event, not pleased).

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why it fizzled by 1908:** severe repression · no disciplined party organisation ever emerged · leaderless after mass arrests/deportations and Aurobindo/B.C. Pal's retirement · the **Surat split (1907)** magnified internal squabbles · confined largely to the upper/middle classes, never reaching the peasantry · non-cooperation and passive resistance stayed **ideas, not executed programmes**.

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why it still mattered — "a leap forward":** it drew in hitherto untouched groups; it rehearsed **every technique later associated with Gandhi** — non-cooperation, passive resistance, filling British jails, constructive work — without yet giving them disciplined form; and its reach extended into art (Abanindranath Tagore, Nandalal Bose), science (J.C. Bose, P.C. Roy) and literature, not just politics.

# ⚔️ PART D — THE SURAT SPLIT, 1907

## 8️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The road to Surat, and government's three-pronged strategy

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Class 8 gives you one sentence: "the Congress split... after the split, the Congress came to be dominated by the Moderates." Spectrum gives you the actual mechanics — and they are a good example of how venue politics decides outcomes.**

| Session | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Benaras, Dec 1905** *(Gokhale president)* | Extremists wanted the boycott extended nationwide and to all forms of association (schools, courts, councils). A mild compromise resolution averted a split — **for now** |
| **Calcutta, Dec 1906** *(Dadabhai Naoroji president)* | A concession to the militants: Congress's goal formally defined as **"swarajya or self-government"** like the UK or the colonies — **the word swaraj used for the first time**, deliberately left undefined |
| **Surat, Dec 1907** | **The split.** Extremists wanted the session at **Nagpur**, with Tilak or Lajpat Rai as president. Moderates instead chose **Surat** — *specifically to invoke the convention that a leader from the host province could not preside*, since Surat lay in **Tilak's own Bombay Presidency*. They pushed **Rashbehari Ghosh** instead and sought to drop the swadeshi/boycott/national-education resolutions. Both sides held firm — **the split followed** |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The venue choice was itself the tactic — picking Surat was a deliberate procedural manoeuvre to exclude Tilak from the chair, not an accident of scheduling. This kind of granular "how" is exactly what a reference book adds over a textbook's summary.**

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The government's own three-pronged strategy**, per John Morley's own words ("rallying" the Moderates) — **repression → conciliation → suppression**: mildly repress the Extremists first (mainly to frighten the Moderates); then placate the Moderates with reform hints conditional on distancing from the Extremists; then, with the Moderates neutralised, **suppress the Extremists with full force**. *"Neither side understood the purpose behind the strategy... the Surat split suggested that the policy of carrot and stick had brought rich dividends."*

⭐⭐⭐ **Five repression laws, 1907-11:** Seditious Meetings Act (1907) · Indian Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act (1908) · Criminal Law Amendment Act (1908) · Indian Press Act (1910) — plus **Tilak's 1908 sedition trial**, over his *Kesari* commentary on the Muzaffarpur bomb (which killed two European women): *"there is also a limit to the patience of humanity."* **Six years' transportation to Mandalay, released 1914.**

# 🗳️ PART E — THE MORLEY-MINTO REFORMS OF 1909

## 9️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ A confirmed total gap — the Indian Councils Act, 1909, in full

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Class 8 mentions only the CONSEQUENCE — "separate electorates conceded, 1909" — never the Act's own architecture. This is the single largest confirmed gap in this file.**

### Build-up

**October 1906**, the **Simla Deputation** — a group of Muslim elites led by the **Agha Khan** — met Viceroy **Minto**, demanding separate electorates and representation **in excess of their numerical strength**, citing Muslim loyalty *"to the defence of the empire."* This same group soon took over the **Muslim League**, itself floated only weeks earlier (**30 December 1906**) by **Nawab Salimullah of Dacca**, along with Mohsin-ul-Mulk and Waqar-ul-Mulk — with the explicit aim of preaching loyalty and keeping Muslim opinion away from Congress. **Gokhale** separately lobbied Secretary of State **John Morley** in London for colonial-style self-government.

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The Reforms — Indian Councils Act, 1909

| Provision | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Elective principle** | Recognised for non-official council seats, for the first time — **but by class and community, not universal franchise** |
| ⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Separate electorates for Muslims** | Established for the central council — Spectrum calls this **"a most detrimental step for India"** |
| **Council size** | Elected members increased; provincial councils got a **non-official majority** — but since some non-officials were nominated, the **overall non-elected majority survived** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The numbers** *(Sumit Sarkar)* | Imperial Legislative Council, **69 total**: **37 officials**; of **32 non-officials**, 5 nominated + **27 elected** — of which **8 seats reserved for Muslims** (separate electorate), **4 for British capitalists**, **2 for landlords**, **13 general** |
| **Indirect election** | Local bodies elected an electoral college → provincial legislatures → central legislature |
| ⚠️ **Communal weighting** | Muslims given representation **beyond their population share**, and a **lower income qualification** for Muslim voters than Hindu voters |
| **Legislative powers** | Enlarged at centre and province — resolutions (non-binding), questions and supplementaries allowed, **separate budget items** votable — **but the budget as a whole still could not be voted on** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Executive council** | **One Indian appointed to the Viceroy's Executive Council — Satyendra Sinha, 1909, the first Indian ever to hold the post** |

### Evaluation

> **Morley himself, on the reforms' actual intent:** *"If it could be said that this chapter of reforms led directly or indirectly to the establishment of a parliamentary system in India, I, for one, would have nothing at all to do with it."*

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The verdict:** aimed at **dividing nationalist ranks** — confusing the Moderates with partial concessions while entrenching communal division through separate electorates, which in practice appeased only a **small Muslim elite**, not "the community" as officially claimed. Indirect, multi-tier election gave *"the impression of infiltration of legislators through a number of sieves."* **Nehru's own later verdict:** separate electorates created *"a political barrier... which grew [with] every extension of franchise like some canker."* Spectrum's closing line: what the reforms actually delivered was **"a shadow rather than substance"** — *"benevolent despotism,"* not self-government.

# 🏠 PART F — THE HOME RULE LEAGUE MOVEMENT

## 🔟 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ A confirmed total gap — the second-biggest addition in this file

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Class 8 never mentions the Home Rule League at all — the chapter jumps straight from WWI's effects to Gandhi's 1915 arrival. This entire movement, which bridges the Extremist-Moderate era and the Gandhian era, is a clean addition.**

**The idea:** an **Indian response to WWI**, less dramatic than the Ghadr Party's armed-uprising bid abroad but far more effective — a **year-round political alliance** (unlike Congress's annual sessions), modelled on the **Irish Home Rule League**, demanding self-government within the British Commonwealth. Key figures: **Tilak, Annie Besant, G.S. Khaparde, S. Subramania Iyer, Joseph Baptista** and, notably, **Mohammad Ali Jinnah**. In the end, **two separate leagues** launched rather than one — to avoid friction between the two leaders' respective followings, though the two **coordinated by dividing territory**.

### Factors behind it

⭐⭐⭐ Popular pressure seen as necessary to win concessions · **disillusionment with Morley-Minto** · wartime taxation and price rises · the war itself (fought among "civilised" imperial powers, mutually trading atrocity propaganda) undercutting the myth of white superiority · **Tilak's release (June 1914)** and conciliatory turn toward the Moderates · **Annie Besant**, an Irish theosophist in India since 1896, choosing to extend her activism into a Home Rule campaign.

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The two Leagues, compared

| | Tilak's League | Besant's League |
|---|---|---|
| **Founded** | **April 1916** | **September 1916** |
| **Headquarters** | Poona | Madras |
| **First meeting** | Belgaum | — |
| **Territory** | Maharashtra *(excl. Bombay city)*, Karnataka, Central Provinces, Berar | **Rest of India**, including Bombay city |
| **Branches** | **6** | **200** |
| **Organisation** | Tighter | **Looser** |
| **Key organisers** | Tilak himself | **George Arundale** *(secretary)*, B.W. Wadia, C.P. Ramaswamy Aiyar |
| **Programme emphasis** | Swarajya, **linguistic states**, vernacular education | Broad political education |

⭐⭐⭐ **Later joiners:** Motilal and Jawaharlal Nehru, Bhulabhai Desai, C.R. Das, K.M. Munshi, Saifuddin Kitchlew, Madan Mohan Malaviya, **Jinnah** (led the Bombay division), Tej Bahadur Sapru, Lajpat Rai — several from Gokhale's own Servants of India Society. ⚠️ **Anglo-Indians, most Muslims, and non-Brahmins of the South stayed away**, fearing Home Rule would mean **upper-caste Hindu majority rule**.

### Government repression, and why it faded

The government moved hard, especially in Madras (students barred from political meetings); a case against Tilak was thrown out by the High Court, but he was **barred from Punjab and Delhi**; **Annie Besant, Wadia and Arundale were arrested in June 1917**, provoking nationwide protest — **Sir S. Subramania Aiyar renounced his knighthood** in response, and Montagu himself quipped about the government's habit of multiplying its own troubles. Besant was **released in September 1917**.

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why it petered out by 1919 — seven reasons:** weak organisation · communal riots (1917-18) · Moderates pacified by **Montagu's August 1917 statement** and Besant's release · Extremist talk of passive resistance kept Moderates away from Sept. 1918 · Tilak abroad (Sept. 1918) fighting a libel case, leaving the movement leaderless · the **Montford reforms (announced July 1918)** further split nationalist opinion, with even Besant vacillating · and, decisively, **Gandhi's rising mass appeal displaced it**. *(In 1920 Gandhi briefly took over the League's presidency and renamed it the **Swarajya Sabha**; within a year it merged into the Congress.)*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Positive gains, despite the short life:** shifted emphasis from elite to mass politics · built the **first organisational town-country link**, crucial once the mass phase began · created a **generation of committed nationalists** · prepared the ground for **Gandhian-style politics** · directly influenced **Montagu's August 1917 declaration** and the reforms that followed · and the **Tilak-Besant push for Moderate-Extremist reunion fed straight into Lucknow, 1916.**

# 🤝 PART G — LUCKNOW, 1916: REUNION AND THE PACT

## 1️⃣1️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Beyond Class 8's one line — the readmission, the five reasons, and the actual demands

⚠️ **Class 8 gives you the outcome in a sentence: "the Congress and the Muslim League signed the historic Lucknow Pact... to work together for representative government." Spectrum gives the mechanics behind both halves of that sentence.**

**Readmission of the Extremists**, presided over by Moderate **Ambika Charan Majumdar**, was eased by: old controversies going stale · both sides realising the split had bred only inactivity · **Tilak and Besant's own reconciliation push** (Tilak now explicitly disavowing violence and endorsing administrative reform, not overthrow) · and the deaths of **Gokhale and Pherozshah Mehta**, the Moderates' sharpest anti-Extremist voices.

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why the Muslim League's own stance shifted toward Congress

| # | Reason |
|:--:|---|
| 1 | Britain's refusal to back the **Khalifa's Turkey** in the Balkan Wars (1912-13) and against Italy (1911) angered Muslims |
| 2 | **Annulment of Bengal's partition (1911)** annoyed the Muslim elite who had backed it |
| 3 | Refusal to grant Aligarh University all-India affiliating powers alienated more Muslims |
| 4 | The League's **younger, militant "Young Party"** was outgrowing the loyalist Aligarh school — its **1912 Calcutta session** already committed it to *"working with other groups for... self-government"* |
| 5 | Wartime internment of **Ali brothers, Maulana Azad, Hasrat Mohani**, and suppression of *Al Hilal* and *Comrade*, radicalised younger Muslim opinion |

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The Lucknow Pact's actual five joint demands

1. **Government to declare it would confer self-government at an early date**
2. **Expanded legislatures**, centre and province, with an **elected majority** and more powers
3. **Five-year legislative council terms**
4. **The Secretary of State's salary paid by the British treasury**, not Indian revenue
5. **Half the members of the Viceroy's and provincial Executive Councils to be Indian**

⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Criticism, on two axes:** *Constitutionally* — half an elected executive that the legislature still could not remove was a recipe for **deadlock**, since the budget still needed legislative approval. Spectrum's blunt summary: **"the Lucknow Pact demands were just a significantly expanded version of the Morley-Minto reforms."** *Communally* — Congress's acceptance of **separate electorates** (to continue until either community wanted joint ones) was, in Spectrum's own words, **"a major landmark in the evolution of the two-nation theory"** — the two organisations now met as **acknowledged separate political entities**, with mass reconciliation between the two communities never actually attempted, only elite-level agreement.

## 1️⃣2️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Montagu's August 1917 Declaration — the trigger for dyarchy

The government's own response, partly to placate the reunited nationalists: **Edwin Montagu's statement to the Commons, 20 August 1917** — *"increasing association of Indians in every branch of the administration, and the gradual development of self-governing institutions, with a view to the progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire."* For the first time, self-government became **stated British policy**, not sedition — a reversal of Morley's 1909 position. But **two Indian objections** stood out: **no timeframe was specified**, and the **pace and nature of the "progress" remained entirely Britain's own call**. The scheme built to satisfy this declaration — **dyarchy** — belongs to the **Government of India Act, 1919**, covered in full at [Spectrum file 08](08_From_Simon_to_Cabinet_Mission.md) *(in the context of its 1935 replacement)* — **this file stops at the trigger, not the mechanism.**

### 🔴 Prelims traps

| ⚠️ The claim that looks right | ✅ The truth |
|---|---|
| *The British Indian Association was the first political body in Bengal* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It was an 1851 MERGER of two earlier bodies — the Landholders' Society and the Bengal British India Society (1843)** |
| *The East India Association was founded in India* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Founded by Dadabhai Naoroji in LONDON, 1866, to lobby British opinion — Indian branches came later** |
| *The Indian National Congress's founding is uncontested history* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Three named, competing theories exist — Safety Valve (Lajpat Rai), Conspiracy (R.P. Dutt), Lightning Conductor (Bipan Chandra)** |
| *The Indian Councils Act 1892 let the legislature vote on the budget* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It could only be DISCUSSED — never voted upon or amended** |
| *Militant nationalism grew mainly because of World War I* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That is a SEPARATE, later cause (post-1919 mass mobilisation) — militant nationalism itself grew from the 1890s, peaking by 1905, driven by Curzon's policies and international events like Japan's 1905 win over Russia** |
| *The Surat split (1907) happened because Tilak refused to attend* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It happened because the Moderates deliberately chose Surat — in Tilak's own province — specifically to invoke the rule barring a host-province leader from presiding** |
| *The Morley-Minto Reforms granted colonial self-government* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Morley explicitly said he would have "nothing to do with" any reform leading toward a parliamentary system in India** |
| *Satyendra Sinha was the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Correct — appointed 1909, under the Morley-Minto Reforms** |
| *The Home Rule League was a single unified organisation* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **TWO separate leagues — Tilak's (April 1916, Poona, 6 branches) and Besant's (September 1916, Madras, 200 branches) — which divided territory between them** |
| *Jinnah opposed the Home Rule League movement* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **He JOINED it, leading its Bombay division** |
| *The Lucknow Pact (1916) rejected separate electorates* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The opposite — Congress ACCEPTED the principle of separate electorates for Muslims at Lucknow, a landmark step Nehru himself later called a seed of the two-nation theory** |
| *Montagu's August 1917 Declaration promised immediate self-government* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It promised only the "progressive realisation" of responsible government, with no timeframe — a key Indian objection at the time** |

## 🔁 Recap — the ten things to carry away

1. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **PRE-CONGRESS ASSOCIATIONS RAN THREE REGIONAL STRANDS — BENGAL (culminating in the British Indian Association, 1851), BOMBAY (Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, 1867), MADRAS (Mahajan Sabha, 1884)**
2. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THREE COMPETING THEORIES EXPLAIN CONGRESS'S 1885 FOUNDING — SAFETY VALVE (LAJPAT RAI), CONSPIRACY (R.P. DUTT), LIGHTNING CONDUCTOR (BIPAN CHANDRA)**
3. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE INDIAN COUNCILS ACT 1892 LET THE BUDGET BE DISCUSSED BUT NEVER VOTED ON — A CONCESSION WITH NO TEETH**
4. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **MILITANT NATIONALISM'S NINE CAUSES ARE DISTINCT FROM WWI'S FIVE MASS-MOBILISATION EFFECTS — DO NOT MERGE THE TWO LISTS**
5. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **TILAK'S OPPOSITION TO THE AGE OF CONSENT BILL SHOWS THE EXTREMISTS AS POLITICALLY PROGRESSIVE BUT SOCIALLY REACTIONARY**
6. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE SURAT SPLIT (1907) TURNED ON A VENUE CHOICE — SURAT WAS PICKED SPECIFICALLY TO BAR TILAK, A BOMBAY-PROVINCE LEADER, FROM THE CHAIR**
7. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE MORLEY-MINTO REFORMS (1909) INTRODUCED SEPARATE ELECTORATES FOR MUSLIMS WITH DISPROPORTIONATE REPRESENTATION — "A SHADOW RATHER THAN SUBSTANCE"**
8. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **TWO SEPARATE HOME RULE LEAGUES RAN IN PARALLEL — TILAK'S (POONA, 6 BRANCHES) AND BESANT'S (MADRAS, 200 BRANCHES) — AND JINNAH LED BESANT'S BOMBAY DIVISION**
9. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE LUCKNOW PACT'S FIVE JOINT DEMANDS CAME AT THE PRICE OF CONGRESS ACCEPTING SEPARATE ELECTORATES — A STEP SPECTRUM ITSELF CALLS A TWO-NATION-THEORY LANDMARK**
10. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **MONTAGU'S AUGUST 1917 DECLARATION FIRST MADE SELF-GOVERNMENT OFFICIAL BRITISH POLICY — BUT WITH NO TIMEFRAME, TRIGGERING THE GOI ACT 1919'S DYARCHY**
