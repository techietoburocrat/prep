# 📚 PART A — SOURCES FOR THE HISTORY OF MODERN INDIA

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The archive map

⭐ Spectrum's own frame: *"priority needs to be given to archives"* — unique, original records created **as part of an activity**, not written for a future reader. Four categories of public archives, plus private and foreign ones.

| Category | Key holdings |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Central Government** | **National Archives of India, New Delhi** — records split by East India Company branch (public, revenue, political, military, secret, commercial, judicial, education); **Survey of India records** *(from 1767, first Surveyor-General **James Rennell**)*; a dedicated **"Home Political" series started 1907** for political/communal matters; **Reforms Office records** for 1920-37 constitutional history |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **State Governments** | Former provinces, princely states, non-British foreign administrations. Named collections: the **Khalsa Darbar records** *(Lahore Kingdom, 1800-49)*; the **Peshwa Daftar, Pune** — *"the most valuable single source for Maratha history"* for nearly a century before the Peshwas fell; **Rajasthan State Archives, Bikaner** *(Jaipur, Bikaner, Jodhpur, Udaipur)*; Dogra-rule papers at **Jammu** *(from 1846)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The Three Presidencies** | **Bengal** — early Fort William records lost in the 1756 sack of Calcutta, complete only from post-Plassey · **Madras** — from **1670**, Fort St. George, rich on the Anglo-French struggle in the Deccan · **Bombay** — Maharashtra Secretariat Record Office, covers Maharashtra/Gujarat/Sindh/Kannada districts |
| **Other Europeans in India** | **Portuguese** *(Goa, 1700-1900)* · **Dutch** *(Cochin/Malabar records at Madras; Chinsura records in West Bengal)* · **French** *(Chandernagore/Pondicherry records taken to Paris)* · **Danish** *(Tranquebar, 1777-1845, mostly at Madras, rest at Copenhagen — sold to the English in 1845)* |
| **Judicial Records** | Earliest: **Mayor's Court, Fort St. George, from 1689** *(Madras Record Office)*; Calcutta's pre-1757 court records lost, but **1757-73 survive**, plus the **Supreme Court of Bengal (1774-1861)**; Bombay's Mayor's Court *(from 1728)* |
| **Published Archives** | **Parliamentary Papers**, Select Committee reports, Royal Commission reports *(education, civil reforms, famines)*, legislature proceedings, official gazettes |
| **Private Archives** | Leaders' papers and INC records at the **Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, New Delhi**; bank/business-house archives |
| ⭐⭐ **Foreign Repositories** | **India Office Records, London** + the **British Museum** *(Courts of Directors' minutes, Board of Control correspondence, viceroys' papers)* · **Archives Nationale, Paris** · Dutch records at **Rijksarchief, The Hague** · **West Pakistan Record Office, Lahore**, and Peshawar — for India's relations with Afghanistan/Iran too |

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Beyond the archive — five other source types

| Type | What's distinctive |
|---|---|
| **Biographies, memoirs, travel accounts** | Missionary writers *(Bishop Heber's Journal, Abbé Dubois's Hindu Manners and Customs)* aimed at "evangelising"; British travellers *(George Forster, the Burnes brothers, C.J.C. Davidson, John Butler)*; non-British travellers *(Victor Jacquemont, Baron Charles, William Moorcroft)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Newspapers and journals** | India's **first newspaper attempt failed** — **William Bolts**, a disgruntled Company employee, was blocked by the Court of Directors in 1776. **James Augustus Hickey's *Bengal Gazette* (1780)** was India's actual first — seized within two years for criticising officials. ⭐⭐ **A striking fact: nearly ONE-THIRD of the INC's 1885 founding fathers were journalists** |
| **Oral evidence** | *"Broadens the boundaries of the discipline"* but many historians remain **sceptical of its veracity** — cross-link the [Partition supplement](../../../01_NCERT/Class_12_Themes_in_Indian_History_III/Notes/13_Supplement_A_Understanding_Partition.md)'s fuller treatment |
| **Creative literature** | The **novel** as the major Indo-European literary import — **Bankim Chandra Chatterji's *Anandamath* (1882)**, source of *Vande Mataram*; later realist regional novels *(Thakazhi's *Chemmeen*, Basheer's *Balyakalasakhi*)* depicting marginalised lives |
| **Painting** | See §4 below |

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Painting as a historical source

| Style/moment | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **Company Painting** *("Patna Kalam")* | Emerged under EIC patronage — trades, festivals, dances, attire, popular **until photography arrived in the 1840s** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **1857 imagery** | British commemorative paintings meant to provoke specific emotions — **Thomas Jones Barker's *Relief of Lucknow* (1859)** celebrates British rescue; **Joseph Noel Paton's *In Memoriam*** shows English women and children awaiting *"dishonour, violence and death"* — evidence of **how the British WANTED the revolt remembered**, not a neutral record |
| **Kalighat painting** *(Calcutta, 19th c.)* | Mythological AND everyday figures — some pieces **satirised social evils and contemporary fashions** directly |
| **The Bengal School** | Led by **Abanindranath Tagore**, with **E.B. Havell** (Calcutta art school principal) and **Ananda Kentish Coomaraswamy** — a nationalist-stimulated art movement drawing on Indian mythology and heritage *(cross-link: Abanindranath's role is also noted in the Swadeshi cultural sphere at [Spectrum file 10](10_Early_Nationalism_Topups.md))* |

# 🏛️ PART B — THE EIGHT SCHOOLS OF HISTORIOGRAPHY

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Why historiography matters, in Spectrum's own words

> **Percival Spear:** *"The production of histories of India has become very frequent in recent years... The reason is two-fold: changes in the Indian scene requiring a reinterpretation of the facts, and changes in the attitudes of historians about the essential elements of Indian history."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The core idea to carry into any answer: history-writing is never neutral — WHO writes it, and WHEN, shapes WHAT gets asked and answered.** Spectrum names **eight schools** *(four "major," four "other")*, each with a distinct answer to the question **"what was the fundamental contradiction under colonial rule?"**

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The eight schools, side by side

| School | Core claim | Named figures |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Colonial / Imperialist** | India was a **stagnant society** needing British guidance ("White Man's burden"); the British brought **unity** and *Pax Britannica*; rests on **Social Darwinist** assumptions of English fitness to rule | **James Mill, Mountstuart Elphinstone, Vincent Smith** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Nationalist** | The freedom struggle was a **movement of the whole Indian people**, unified against colonial exploitation — developed **in direct confrontation with** the Colonial school | ⚠️ **Nationalist HISTORIANS of modern India post-date 1947** — before that, only nationalist LEADERS wrote (Pattabhi Sitaramayya, J.L. Nehru, A.C. Majumdar); the actual **economic critique** (drain theory) came earlier from Naoroji, Ranade, R.C. Dutt, Gokhale, Wacha; the school's true historians are **R.C. Majumdar and Tara Chand** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Marxist** | The **primary contradiction is colonial-master vs subject-people**, but — unlike the Nationalists — Marxists **also weigh internal class contradictions** within Indian society | **R.P. Dutt** *(India Today, 1940)*, **A.R. Desai** *(Social Background of Indian Nationalism, 1948, tracing the movement across five class-based phases)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Subaltern** | The real fault line is **elite vs subaltern (both Indian and foreign)**, not coloniser vs colonised. **Rejects the idea of a single unified national movement** — claims TWO separate streams: a genuine subaltern anti-imperialist struggle, and a **"bogus" elite Congress movement** that was really just an elite power struggle | **Ranajit Guha**, from the **early 1980s** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Communalist** | Views Hindus and Muslims as **permanently, mutually hostile** groups — reads all of medieval history as Hindu-Muslim conflict, and treats mutual hatred as the direct cause of Partition | Drew heavily on **colonial-era historiography and textbooks** |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Cambridge School** | The real contradiction was **among Indians themselves**, competing for British-given benefits — nationalism as a **quest for power and material gain**, not genuine anti-colonial struggle | ⚠️ Criticised by others as reducing nationalism to **"animal politics"** — strips out ideals/mind from human motivation |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Liberal / Neo-Liberal** | Colonial economic exploitation **did not even benefit Britain as a whole** — colonial markets and overseas capital investment (e.g. Indian railways) **discouraged Britain's own domestic industrial investment** | **Patrick O'Brien, Hopkins, Cain** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Feminist** | Grew from the **1970s women's movement**; broadened into gender history; analyses how **colonial legal structures** shaped women's lives, especially the **denial of ownership of productive resources** | Early landmark works: **Pandita Ramabai's *The High Caste Hindu Woman* (1887)**; **Katherine Mayo's *Mother India* (1927)** *(internationally attention-grabbing, though Mayo's book is itself often read as a colonial-apologist tract)* |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The single most citable internal debate: Sumit Sarkar's critique of R.P. Dutt.** Sarkar, himself a Marxist historian, called Dutt's approach a **"simplistic version of the Marxian class approach"** because Dutt treated the national movement as essentially a **bourgeois movement**, missing its genuinely "open-ended and all-class character." Sarkar instead reads nationalist leaders as an **intelligentsia acting as "a kind of proxy for as yet passive social forces with which it had little organic connection."** **This shows the Marxist school is not monolithic — it argues with itself.**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And Bipan Chandra's own verdict on the Subalterns, worth quoting directly in an answer:** *"A few historians have of late initiated a new trend, described by its proponents as subaltern, which dismisses all previous historical writing... as elite historiography, and claims to replace this old, 'blinkered' historiography with what it claims is a new people's or subaltern approach."* — **a pointedly sceptical framing from inside the profession, not a hostile outsider's dismissal.**

## ⚡ UPSC FOCUS

### 🔴 Prelims traps

| ⚠️ The claim that looks right | ✅ The truth |
|---|---|
| *India's first newspaper was published by a British official acting for the Company* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The opposite — James Augustus Hickey's *Bengal Gazette* (1780) was founded by a Company CRITIC, and an earlier attempt by William Bolts (1776) was actively blocked by the Court of Directors** |
| *The Peshwa Daftar is housed in Delhi's National Archives* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It is housed in the Alienation Office, PUNE** |
| *Nationalist historians wrote extensively on modern India well before independence* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Before 1947, only nationalist LEADERS (not historians) wrote on the national movement — R.C. Majumdar and Tara Chand's nationalist historiography of modern India is a post-1947 development** |
| *The Subaltern School and the Marxist School share the same reading of the national movement* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **They differ sharply — Subalternists REJECT the idea of a single national movement altogether, positing two separate streams; Marxists accept one movement but stress its internal class contradictions** |
| *Sumit Sarkar and R.P. Dutt represent the same strand of Marxist history* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Sarkar explicitly critiqued Dutt's approach as an oversimplified class analysis** |
| *The Cambridge School sees Indian nationalism as a genuine anti-colonial struggle* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **It views it as intra-Indian competition for British-granted benefits — critics call this reducing nationalism to "animal politics"** |
| *Liberal/Neo-Liberal historians argue colonialism benefited Britain's economy overall* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The opposite — O'Brien, Hopkins and Cain argue colonial markets/investment actually DISCOURAGED Britain's own domestic industrial development** |
| *Ghadar, Talwar, Bande Mataram and Indian Sociologist were all published from London* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Four different cities — London (Indian Sociologist), Paris (Bande Mataram), Berlin (Talwar), San Francisco (Ghadar)** |

## 🔑 Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HISTORIOGRAPHY** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The study of how history itself is written and interpreted — not the events, but the interpretive frameworks applied to them** |
| **SUBALTERN** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **A non-elite social group — the school's central claimed protagonist of "real" anti-imperialist struggle** |
| **PAX BRITANNICA** | ⭐⭐⭐ **The Colonial School's claim that British rule brought law, order and peace to a "bickering" Indian society** |
| **"TWO STREAMS" THESIS** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The Subalternist claim that there was no single Indian national movement — only a genuine subaltern struggle and a separate, self-interested elite one** |

## 🔁 Recap — the ten things to carry away

1. ⭐⭐⭐ **THE PESHWA DAFTAR (PUNE) IS THE SINGLE BEST SOURCE FOR MARATHA HISTORY BEFORE THE PESHWAS' FALL**
2. ⭐⭐⭐ **INDIA'S FIRST NEWSPAPER WAS HICKEY'S *BENGAL GAZETTE* (1780) — SEIZED WITHIN TWO YEARS FOR CRITICISING OFFICIALS**
3. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NEARLY ONE-THIRD OF THE INC'S 1885 FOUNDERS WERE JOURNALISTS**
4. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **FOUR EXPATRIATE REVOLUTIONARY PAPERS, FOUR CITIES — INDIAN SOCIOLOGIST (LONDON), BANDE MATARAM (PARIS), TALWAR (BERLIN), GHADAR (SAN FRANCISCO)**
5. ⭐⭐⭐ **1857-ERA BRITISH PAINTINGS WERE DELIBERATE PROPAGANDA, NOT NEUTRAL RECORD — BARKER'S RELIEF OF LUCKNOW AND PATON'S IN MEMORIAM BOTH STAGE BRITISH VICTIMHOOD/HEROISM**
6. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **EIGHT NAMED SCHOOLS OF HISTORIOGRAPHY EXIST — NOT SEVEN — COLONIAL, NATIONALIST, MARXIST, SUBALTERN, COMMUNALIST, CAMBRIDGE, LIBERAL/NEO-LIBERAL, FEMINIST**
7. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NATIONALIST HISTORIOGRAPHY OF MODERN INDIA IS A POST-1947 DEVELOPMENT — BEFORE THAT, ONLY LEADERS, NOT HISTORIANS, WROTE ON IT**
8. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE SUBALTERN SCHOOL REJECTS A SINGLE NATIONAL MOVEMENT ENTIRELY — TWO SEPARATE STREAMS, GENUINE SUBALTERN VS "BOGUS" ELITE**
9. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **SUMIT SARKAR CRITIQUED FELLOW MARXIST R.P. DUTT AS TOO SIMPLISTIC A CLASS READING — THE SCHOOL ARGUES WITH ITSELF**
10. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THE LIBERAL/NEO-LIBERAL SCHOOL ARGUES COLONIALISM ACTUALLY HURT BRITAIN'S OWN DOMESTIC INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT**
