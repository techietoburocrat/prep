## 🎬 Start Here: The Claim That Frames Everything

We describe people by the language they speak — a Tamil, an Oriya — and associate each region with **distinctive food, clothes, poetry, dance, music and painting.**

> ⭐ **"Sometimes we take these identities for granted and assume that they have existed FROM TIME IMMEMORIAL. However, the frontiers separating regions have EVOLVED OVER TIME (and in fact are STILL CHANGING). Also, what we understand as regional cultures today are often the PRODUCT OF COMPLEX PROCESSES OF INTERMIXING of local traditions with ideas from other parts of the subcontinent."**

```
   THREE PATTERNS NCERT IDENTIFIES

   [1] Some practices are DISTINCTIVE to particular regions
   [2] Others seem SIMILAR ACROSS regions
   [3] Others derive from OLDER PRACTICES in a particular area,
       but take a NEW FORM in other regions
```

> 🎯 **This is a genuinely important corrective and prime Essay material.** Regional identity feels primordial and is in fact **recent and constructed**. Kathak became "classical" only **after independence**; Bengali reached a common literary form only in the **15th century**; Malayalam entered official records in the **9th**.
>
> 💻 These are **forks that diverged, exchanged patches, and were later declared canonical** — the "ancient tradition" is a snapshot of a still-running process, retrospectively frozen and labelled.

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐ Language and Region: The Cheras and Malayalam

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The kingdom** | The **Chera kingdom of MAHODAYAPURAM**, established in the ⭐ **NINTH century** in the south-western part of the peninsula — part of present-day **Kerala** |
| ⭐⭐ **The innovation** | The rulers **introduced the MALAYALAM language and script in their INSCRIPTIONS** — **"one of the earliest examples of the use of a REGIONAL LANGUAGE in OFFICIAL RECORDS in the subcontinent"** |
| **But also Sanskritic** | The Cheras **also drew upon Sanskritic traditions**. The **temple theatre of Kerala**, traced to this period, **borrowed stories from the Sanskrit epics** |
| **First literary works** | In Malayalam, dated to about the **twelfth century**, and **directly indebted to Sanskrit** |
| ⭐⭐ ***MANIPRAVALAM*** | A **fourteenth-century text, the *LILATILAKAM*, dealing with GRAMMAR and POETICS**, was composed in **Manipravalam** — literally ⭐ **"DIAMONDS AND CORALS"**, referring to the two languages, **Sanskrit and the regional language** |

> ⭐ ***Manipravalam*** is a favourite Prelims item and a beautiful metaphor: **two precious materials strung on one thread**, neither dissolving into the other. It names exactly what this chapter is about — **regional culture as a deliberate blend**, not a replacement.
>
> 🧠 **Remember: *mani* = gem/diamond, *pravalam* = coral. Sanskrit + Malayalam. Text = *Lilatilakam*, 14th century.**

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ Religion and Region: The Jagannatha Cult at Puri

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The name** | **Jagannatha** literally = ⭐ **"lord of the world"**, a name for **VISHNU** |
| **Where** | **PURI, Orissa (Odisha)** |
| ⭐⭐ **The tribal origin** | **"To date, the LOCAL TRIBAL PEOPLE make the WOODEN IMAGE of the deity"** — which suggests the deity **was originally a local tribal deity**, later **identified with Vishnu** |
| **12th century** | **ANANTAVARMAN**, one of the most important rulers of the **GANGA dynasty**, decided to **erect a temple** for **Purushottama Jagannatha** at Puri |
| ⭐ **1230** | King **ANANGABHIMA III dedicated his kingdom to the deity and proclaimed himself the "DEPUTY" of the god** |
| ⭐⭐ **The consequence** | As the temple gained importance as a **centre of pilgrimage**, **its authority in SOCIAL and POLITICAL matters also increased** |
| ⭐⭐ **Why conquerors wanted it** | **All those who conquered Orissa — the MUGHALS, the MARATHAS and the ENGLISH EAST INDIA COMPANY — attempted to gain control over the temple.** They felt this would **"make their rule ACCEPTABLE TO THE LOCAL PEOPLE"** |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is the chapter's best single case study, and it answers two exam questions at once.**
>
> **First, the absorption mechanism (Ch.6 again):** a **tribal wooden deity** becomes **Vishnu** — but the tribal people **keep the right to carve the image**, to this day. The local is not erased; it is **incorporated with its role preserved**. That is why the tradition held.
>
> **Second, religion as political infrastructure:** by declaring himself the god's **deputy**, Anangabhima III made **the deity the sovereign and himself the administrator**. Any later conqueror therefore had to capture **the temple**, not merely the territory — because legitimacy resided there. **Three successive imperial powers, including the British, understood this and acted on it.**
>
> 🎯 **NCERT's own question — "why did conquerors try to control the temple of Jagannatha?" — is a standard exam question with exactly this answer.**

## 3️⃣ ⭐ The Rajputs and Traditions of Heroism

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The name** | In the **nineteenth century** the British called the region that is most of present-day Rajasthan ⭐ **"RAJPUTANA"** |
| ⚠️ **The caveat** | This is **only partly true** — several groups identify as Rajputs across **northern and central India**, and **many peoples other than Rajputs live in Rajasthan** |
| **Rule** | From about the **eighth century**, most of present-day Rajasthan was ruled by various Rajput families — **Prithviraj** (Ch.2) was one such ruler |
| ⭐ **The heroic ideal** | The hero **"fought valiantly, often choosing DEATH ON THE BATTLEFIELD RATHER THAN FACE DEFEAT"** |
| ⭐ **The medium** | Stories recorded in **poems and songs**, recited by ⭐ **specially trained MINSTRELS** |
| **Their purpose** | To **preserve the memories of heroes** and **inspire others to follow their example** |
| **Their popular appeal** | Ordinary people were attracted by **dramatic situations** and a range of **strong emotions — loyalty, friendship, love, valour, anger** |

### ⚠️ ⭐ Women in these stories

> Women are sometimes depicted as **following their heroic husbands in both life and death** — there are stories about the practice of ***SATI***, **the immolation of widows on the funeral pyre of their husbands.**
>
> ⭐ **"So those who followed the heroic ideal often had to pay for it with THEIR LIVES."**

> 🎯 **NCERT's placement of that sentence is deliberate and worth noticing.** The heroic ideal is not presented as simple glory — it is presented as **a code that killed the people who lived by it, and killed women who had not chosen it at all.**
>
> ⭐ **Strong GS1 material on gender:** the celebration of *jauhar* and *sati* in heroic literature is how a coercive practice was rendered as honour. Connect forward to **Raja Ram Mohan Roy and the abolition of sati (1829)** — Class 8, Ch.7, *Women, Caste and Reform*.
>
> 🔗 Also connect back: the **first epigraphic record of sati** is the **Eran inscription, 510 CE** (Class 6 Ch.9).

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Beyond Regional Frontiers: The Story of Kathak

**The single most-asked item in this chapter.**

```
   THE EVOLUTION OF KATHAK

   ORIGIN
   "KATHAK" derives from KATHA -- a word used in Sanskrit
   and other languages for STORY.
   The KATHAKS were originally a CASTE OF STORY-TELLERS in
   TEMPLES of north India, who embellished their performances
   with GESTURES and SONGS.
        |
        v
   15th - 16th CENTURIES : THE BHAKTI TURN
   Kathak began evolving into a DISTINCT MODE OF DANCE with
   the spread of the BHAKTI MOVEMENT.
   Legends of RADHA-KRISHNA were enacted in folk plays called
   *** RASA LILA ***, which combined FOLK DANCE with the basic
   GESTURES of the story-tellers.
        |
        v
   MUGHAL PERIOD : THE COURT TURN
   Under the MUGHAL EMPERORS AND THEIR NOBLES, Kathak was
   performed IN THE COURT, where it ACQUIRED ITS PRESENT
   FEATURES and developed into a distinctive style.
        |
        v
   TWO GHARANAS (traditions)
     [1] the courts of RAJASTHAN (JAIPUR)
     [2] LUCKNOW -- under the patronage of *** WAJID ALI SHAH ***,
         the LAST NAWAB OF AWADH, it grew into a major art form
        |
        v
   BY THE 3rd QUARTER OF THE 19th CENTURY
   Firmly entrenched not only in those two regions but in
   PUNJAB, HARYANA, JAMMU AND KASHMIR, BIHAR and MADHYA PRADESH.
   Emphasis on INTRICATE AND RAPID FOOTWORK, ELABORATE COSTUMES,
   and the ENACTMENT OF STORIES.
        |
        v
   COLONIAL DISFAVOUR
   Viewed with DISFAVOUR by most BRITISH ADMINISTRATORS in the
   19th and 20th centuries. It SURVIVED and continued to be
   performed by *** COURTESANS ***...
        |
        v
   ...and was recognised as ONE OF THE SIX "CLASSICAL" FORMS
   OF DANCE *** AFTER INDEPENDENCE ***.
```

> ⭐⭐ **Three facts from that chain get asked constantly:**
> 1. **Kathak came from temple story-tellers** (*katha* = story), **not** from the court
> 2. **Two gharanas: Jaipur and Lucknow**, the latter under **Wajid Ali Shah**
> 3. It was **preserved by courtesans** through colonial disapproval and **classicised only after 1947**
>
> 🎯 **That third point is genuinely important for a Mains answer on cultural nationalism:** the "classical" status of Indian dance forms is a **post-independence act of national canon-formation**, and the women who actually kept the tradition alive — courtesans — were **stigmatised** in the process. **A tradition was rescued and its custodians were written out.**

### 💃 ⭐⭐ The six classical dance forms

| Dance | State |
|---|---|
| **KATHAK** | North India (Jaipur & Lucknow gharanas) |
| **BHARATANATYAM** | **Tamil Nadu** |
| **KATHAKALI** | **Kerala** |
| **ODISSI** | **Odisha** |
| **KUCHIPUDI** | **Andhra Pradesh** |
| **MANIPURI** | **Manipur** |

> 🧠 **Memory hook: "KA-BHA-KA-O-KU-MA."** *(Note: the officially recognised list today includes **Sattriya** from Assam and **Mohiniyattam** from Kerala, making eight — but NCERT names these six. If a question specifies "as per NCERT", use six.)*

### ⭐ NCERT's critique of the word "classical" — quotable

> *"The question of defining any art form as 'classical' is often quite complicated. Do we define something as classical if it deals with a **religious theme**? Or because it appears to require **a great deal of skill acquired through long years of training**? Or because it is **performed according to rules that are laid down, and variations are not encouraged**?... Many dance forms that are classified as 'folk' also **share several of the characteristics considered typical of 'classical' forms**. So, while the use of the term 'classical' may suggest that these forms are **superior, this need not always be literally true.**"*

> 🎯 **Excellent for Essay and for GS1 on cultural hierarchy.** The classical/folk boundary is **a judgement about status dressed up as a description of form.** Deploy it in any answer about whose culture gets preserved and valorised.

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Painting for Patrons: The Tradition of Miniatures

| Basics | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What** | **Small-sized paintings**, generally in ⭐ **WATER COLOUR on CLOTH or PAPER** |
| **Earliest** | On ⭐ **PALM LEAVES or WOOD** |
| ⭐ **Earliest famous group** | Found in **WESTERN INDIA**, used to illustrate ⭐ **JAINA TEXTS** |

### 👑 The Mughal miniature

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Patrons** | ⭐ **AKBAR, JAHANGIR and SHAH JAHAN** |
| **What they painted** | Primarily **illustrated manuscripts containing HISTORICAL ACCOUNTS and POETRY** |
| **Style and subject** | **Brilliant colours**; **court scenes, scenes of battle or hunting, and other aspects of social life** |
| ⭐ **Their circulation** | **Often exchanged as GIFTS** and **viewed only by an EXCLUSIVE FEW — the emperor and his close associates** |

> 🎯 **That last row matters.** Mughal miniatures were **not public art**. They were **private, elite, diplomatic objects** — which is precisely why they survived in such good condition (kept in palaces) and why they tell us about the court and not about ordinary life.

### 🏔️ ⭐⭐ The dispersal — and the birth of the Pahari schools

```
   With the DECLINE OF THE MUGHAL EMPIRE, many painters
   MOVED OUT to the courts of the emerging REGIONAL STATES (Ch.8)
                          |
        +-----------------+------------------+
        v                                    v
   The DECCAN courts               The RAJPUT courts of RAJASTHAN
                                            |
   Mughal artistic tastes INFLUENCED them,  |
   but they RETAINED AND DEVELOPED their    |
   DISTINCTIVE CHARACTERISTICS               |
                                            v
                        Portraits of rulers and court scenes
                        followed the Mughal example; themes from
                        MYTHOLOGY AND POETRY were depicted at
                        *** MEWAR, JODHPUR, BUNDI, KOTA and
                            KISHANGARH ***
```

**And in the Himalayan foothills (modern Himachal Pradesh):**

| School | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **BASOHLI** | By the **late seventeenth century** this region developed a ⭐ **BOLD AND INTENSE** style. The most popular text painted here was ⭐ **BHANUDATTA'S *RASAMANJARI*** |
| ⭐⭐ **KANGRA** | ⭐ **Nadir Shah's invasion and the conquest of Delhi in 1739** caused the **migration of Mughal artists to the hills** to escape the uncertainties of the plains. There they found ready patrons → the founding of the **Kangra school**. By the ⭐ **mid-eighteenth century** Kangra artists developed a style that "breathed a new spirit into miniature painting". Inspired by ⭐ **VAISHNAVITE traditions**; distinguished by ⭐ **SOFT COLOURS INCLUDING COOL BLUES AND GREENS, and a LYRICAL TREATMENT of themes** |

> 🧠 **The contrast to remember: BASOHLI = BOLD AND INTENSE. KANGRA = SOFT, COOL BLUES AND GREENS, LYRICAL.** That pairing is a classic Prelims match, and the trigger for Kangra is **1739, Nadir Shah**.
>
> 🎯 **Note the causal chain, which is a good Mains point:** *political collapse → artists lose patronage → migration → new regional schools.* **The decline of the Mughal empire did not end Indian painting; it decentralised and diversified it.** Cultural flourishing does not require political unity — the same argument you met in Class 6 Ch.9.

### ⭐⭐ The line NCERT insists on — do not skip it

> **"Remember that ORDINARY WOMEN AND MEN PAINTED AS WELL — on POTS, WALLS OF HOUSES, FLOORS, CLOTH — works of art that have ONLY OCCASIONALLY SURVIVED, unlike the miniatures that were CAREFULLY PRESERVED IN PALACES FOR CENTURIES."**

> ⭐⭐ **This is the "silence of the sources" lesson (Class 6 Ch.1) applied to art history**, and it's a superb one-line answer to NCERT's question *"why do we know much more about the cultural practices of rulers than about those of ordinary people?"*
>
> **The survival of art is a function of patronage and storage, not of quality.** A palace preserves; a mud wall is replastered every year. Folk art traditions — Madhubani, Warli, alpana, rangoli — are **as old and as skilled**, and are simply **not collectible**.

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐ A Closer Look: BENGAL

### 📜 How Bengali emerged

```
   MID-1st MILLENNIUM BCE
   Early Sanskrit texts suggest the people of Bengal
   *** DID NOT SPEAK SANSKRITIC LANGUAGES ***
        |
        v
   4th - 3rd CENTURIES BCE
   COMMERCIAL TIES develop between BENGAL and MAGADHA
   (south Bihar) -- may have led to growing Sanskrit influence
        |
        v
   4th CENTURY CE
   The GUPTA rulers establish political control over NORTH BENGAL
   and begin to *** SETTLE BRAHMANAS *** there
   -> linguistic influence of the mid-Ganga valley grows stronger
        |
        v
   7th CENTURY
   The Chinese traveller *** XUAN ZANG *** observed that
   LANGUAGES RELATED TO SANSKRIT were in use ALL OVER BENGAL
        |
        v
   8th CENTURY onward : the PALAS
   14th - 16th CENTURIES : SULTANS independent of Delhi
   1586 : *** AKBAR conquers Bengal *** -> nucleus of the BENGAL SUBA
        |
        v
   *** While PERSIAN was the language of ADMINISTRATION,
       BENGALI developed as a REGIONAL LANGUAGE ***
        |
        v
   By the 15th CENTURY the Bengali group of DIALECTS was united by
   a COMMON LITERARY LANGUAGE based on the spoken language of the
   WESTERN part of the region (now West Bengal)
```

> ⭐ **And the crucial nuance:** although Bengali is **derived from Sanskrit**, it **passed through several stages of evolution**, and a wide range of **NON-Sanskrit words — from TRIBAL LANGUAGES, PERSIAN and EUROPEAN LANGUAGES — became part of modern Bengali.**
>
> 🎯 **A language is a layered deposit, not a pure descent.** Excellent for Essay on language, identity and purity claims.

### 📚 ⭐⭐ Two categories of early Bengali literature

| | **CATEGORY 1: indebted to Sanskrit** | **CATEGORY 2: independent of Sanskrit** |
|---|---|---|
| **Contents** | **Translations of the Sanskrit epics**; the ⭐ ***MANGALAKAVYAS*** (literally **"auspicious poems"**, dealing with **local deities**); **bhakti literature** such as biographies of ⭐ **CHAITANYADEVA**, leader of the Vaishnava bhakti movement (Ch.6) | ⭐ **NATH literature** — the songs of **MAYNAMATI and GOPICHANDRA**; stories concerning the worship of ⭐ **DHARMA THAKUR**; **fairy tales, folk tales and ballads** |
| **Dating** | ⭐ **Easier to date** — several manuscripts have been found | ⭐ **Circulated ORALLY and CANNOT BE PRECISELY DATED** |
| **Where popular** | — | ⭐ **Particularly popular in EASTERN BENGAL, where the influence of BRAHMANAS was relatively WEAK** |

**Background:** the **Naths** were **ascetics who engaged in a variety of yogic practices** (Ch.6). The song of **Maynamati** — a queen who **encouraged her son Gopichandra to adopt the path of asceticism** — was often **enacted**. **Dharma Thakur** is a **popular regional deity, often worshipped in the form of a STONE or a PIECE OF WOOD.**

> 🎯 **The correlation is the point:** the **oral, non-Sanskritic** literature was strongest **exactly where Brahmana influence was weakest.** Textual culture and social power map onto each other — and it explains NCERT's question *"why do you think the second category of texts was not written down?"*

### 🕌 ⭐⭐ *Pirs* and the peopling of eastern Bengal

```
   FROM THE 16th CENTURY
   People migrate in large numbers from the LESS FERTILE
   WESTERN Bengal to the FORESTED AND MARSHY areas of
   SOUTH-EASTERN Bengal
        |
        |  They CLEAR FORESTS and bring land under RICE cultivation
        v
   Local communities of FISHERFOLK and SHIFTING CULTIVATORS,
   OFTEN TRIBALS, MERGE with the new communities of PEASANTS
        |
        |  This COINCIDES with MUGHAL control over Bengal,
        |  capital in the heart of the eastern delta.
        |  Officials received land and often set up MOSQUES
        |  that served as centres for religious transformation.
        v
   The early settlers sought ORDER AND ASSURANCE in unstable
   new settlements. This was provided by COMMUNITY LEADERS who
   also functioned as TEACHERS and ADJUDICATORS, and were
   sometimes ascribed with SUPERNATURAL POWERS.
        |
        v
   People referred to them with affection and respect as *** PIRS ***
```

**⭐⭐ And note how capacious the term was:**

> The term ***pir*** included **saints or SUFIS and other religious personalities, DARING COLONISERS and DEIFIED SOLDIERS, various HINDU and BUDDHIST DEITIES, and even ANIMISTIC SPIRITS.** ⭐ **The cult of pirs became very popular and their shrines can be found everywhere in Bengal.**

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is one of the most striking passages in the whole book.** A single honorific covered **Sufi saints, pioneer settlers, dead soldiers, Hindu and Buddhist gods, and nature spirits** — all at once.
>
> 🎯 **It is the best available illustration of how religious categories actually behaved on a frontier**: not as sealed systems but as **a shared vocabulary of reverence** that people applied to whoever gave them order and assurance. **Superb for GS1 on composite culture and syncretism** — and it pairs with the *dargah* in Ch.6.

### 🛕 ⭐⭐ The Bengali temple style

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **When** | A **temple-building spree from the late fifteenth century, culminating in the nineteenth** |
| **Why** | As in Ch.2 — built by **individuals or groups who were becoming powerful**, to **demonstrate their power and proclaim their piety** |
| ⭐⭐ **Who built them** | Many modest **BRICK and TERRACOTTA** temples were built with the support of several ⭐ **"LOW" SOCIAL GROUPS — the KOLU (OIL PRESSERS) and the KANSARI (BELL METAL WORKERS)** |
| ⭐ **How they could afford it** | ⭐ **The coming of the EUROPEAN TRADING COMPANIES created new economic opportunities**; many families of these groups availed of them, and **as their social and economic position improved, they proclaimed their status through the construction of temples** |
| ⭐⭐ **The form** | When **local deities**, once worshipped in **THATCHED HUTS**, gained the recognition of the **Brahmanas**, their images were **housed in temples** — and **the temples began to COPY THE THATCHED HUT**: the **double-roofed (*DOCHALA*)** or **four-roofed (*CHAUCHALA*)** structure |
| **The four-roofed form** | **Four triangular roofs on four walls move up to converge on a curved line or a point**; usually built on a **square platform** |
| **Decoration** | The **interior was relatively plain**, but **outer walls were decorated with paintings, ornamental tiles or TERRACOTTA TABLETS** |
| ⭐ **The finest** | **VISHNUPUR**, in the **BANKURA district of West Bengal** |

> ⭐⭐ **Two ideas here are exam-grade and worth stating explicitly:**
> 1. **Architecture copied vernacular housing.** The temple imitates **the village hut** — a religious form built from a domestic one. ***Dochala* = two roofs, *chauchala* = four roofs.** Learn both terms
> 2. **Social mobility built the temples.** Oil pressers and bell-metal workers **got rich through European trade** and **converted economic gain into ritual status** by building temples. **A concrete, datable instance of caste status being negotiated through wealth** — outstanding for GS1
>
> 🔗 It also quietly foreshadows Class 8: **the European companies are already reshaping Bengali society before they rule it.**

### 🐟 ⭐⭐ Fish as food — the exemption

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Why** | Bengal is a **riverine plain** producing plenty of **RICE and FISH** — the staple of even poor Bengalis. **Fishing has always been an important occupation** |
| **Evidence** | Bengali literature contains several references to fish; **terracotta plaques on the walls of temples and *viharas* (Buddhist monasteries) depict scenes of FISH BEING DRESSED AND TAKEN TO MARKET IN BASKETS** |
| ⭐⭐ **The rule and its relaxation** | **Brahmanas were not allowed to eat non-vegetarian food**, **but the popularity of fish in the local diet made the Brahmanical authorities RELAX THIS PROHIBITION for the Bengal Brahmanas** |
| ⭐ **The text** | The ***BRIHADDHARMA PURANA***, a ⭐ **thirteenth-century Sanskrit text from Bengal**, **permitted the local Brahmanas to eat certain varieties of fish** |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is the perfect closing example for the chapter, and a genuinely excellent exam point.**
>
> A **pan-Indian textual rule** (Brahmanas do not eat non-vegetarian food) met a **regional ecological reality** (a riverine plain where fish is the staple) — **and the text gave way.** A *Purana* was written to license the local practice.
>
> 🎯 **The direction of causation is what matters: local practice changed the textual rule, not the other way round.** That single fact demolishes any picture of a uniform, top-down Brahmanical order and is deployable in GS1 answers on the diversity of Indian society, on regional variation in caste norms, and in Essay on the relationship between text and lived practice.

| **Jagannatha** ⭐⭐⭐ | "Lord of the world", **Vishnu**, **Puri**; image still carved by **tribal people**; temple by **Anantavarman** (**Ganga** dynasty, 12th c.); **Anangabhima III, 1230**, made himself the god's **"deputy"**; **Mughals, Marathas, EIC** all sought control |
| **Rajputana** ⭐ | British name, **19th century** |
| **Rajput heroic ideal** ⭐ | Death rather than defeat; recited by **minstrels**; women and ***sati*** |
| **Kathak** ⭐⭐⭐ | From ***katha*** = story; **caste of temple story-tellers**; **rasa lila** in the 15th–16th c. with **bhakti**; court form under the **Mughals**; **two gharanas — Jaipur and Lucknow**; **Wajid Ali Shah**, last Nawab of Awadh; preserved by **courtesans**; **classical only after independence** |
| **Six classical dances** ⭐⭐ | Kathak, **Bharatanatyam** (TN), **Kathakali** (Kerala), **Odissi** (Odisha), **Kuchipudi** (AP), **Manipuri** (Manipur) |
| **Miniatures** ⭐⭐ | **Water colour on cloth/paper**; earliest on **palm leaf/wood**; **western India, Jaina texts**; Mughal patrons **Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan**; seen by **an exclusive few** |
| **Rajput centres** ⭐ | **Mewar, Jodhpur, Bundi, Kota, Kishangarh** |
| **Basohli** ⭐⭐ | **Late 17th c.**, Himalayan foothills, **bold and intense**; **Bhanudatta's *Rasamanjari*** |
| **Kangra** ⭐⭐ | Triggered by ⭐ **Nadir Shah, 1739**; mid-18th c.; **Vaishnavite** inspiration; **soft cool blues and greens**, lyrical |
| **Bengali language** ⭐⭐ | Guptas settled **Brahmanas** in north Bengal (4th c.); **Xuan Zang** (7th c.); **Akbar conquers 1586** → **Bengal suba**; **Persian for administration, Bengali as regional language** |
| **Bengali literature** ⭐⭐ | Cat. 1 — epics, ***Mangalakavyas***, **Chaitanyadeva** biographies. Cat. 2 — **Nath literature**, **Maynamati & Gopichandra**, **Dharma Thakur**, folk tales; **oral, undatable, eastern Bengal** |
| ***Pir*** ⭐⭐ | Covered **Sufis, colonisers, deified soldiers, Hindu and Buddhist deities, animistic spirits** |
| **Bengali temples** ⭐⭐ | ***Dochala*** (double-roofed) / ***chauchala*** (four-roofed), copying **thatched huts**; **brick and terracotta**; built by **Kolu (oil pressers)** and **Kansari (bell metal workers)**; finest at ⭐ **VISHNUPUR, Bankura** |
| **Fish** ⭐⭐ | ***Brihaddharma Purana***, **13th century**, permitted Bengal Brahmanas to eat certain fish |

**Traps:**
- **"Kathak originated in the Mughal court"** → **FALSE.** It originated with **temple story-tellers**; the court **refined** it
- **"Kathak has gharanas at Jaipur and Varanasi"** → **NCERT names Jaipur and LUCKNOW** (a Banaras gharana exists but is not in this text)
- **"Kangra painting is bold and intense"** → **FALSE.** That's **Basohli**; Kangra is **soft, cool, lyrical**
- **"Manipravalam means a mixture of Tamil and Malayalam"** → **FALSE.** **Sanskrit + the regional language**
- **"The Jagannatha image is carved by Brahmana priests"** → **FALSE.** By **local tribal people**, to this day
- **"Bengali temples were built only by wealthy Brahmanas"** → **FALSE.** Many by **"low" social groups** enriched by European trade
- **"Brahmanas everywhere were forbidden fish"** → In **Bengal the prohibition was relaxed**, licensed by the *Brihaddharma Purana*

## 🔁 2-MINUTE RECAP

1. ⭐ **Regional cultures were MADE, not inherited** — frontiers evolved, and cultures are products of **intermixing local traditions with outside ideas**.
2. **Cheras of Mahodayapuram (9th c.)** put **Malayalam in their inscriptions** — among the earliest official uses of a regional language. ***Manipravalam*** ("diamonds and corals") = **Sanskrit + regional language**; the ***Lilatilakam*** (14th c.).
3. **Jagannatha at Puri** was probably a **tribal deity** — **tribal people still carve the image** — later identified with **Vishnu**. **Anantavarman** built the temple; **Anangabhima III (1230)** made himself the god's **deputy**. **Mughals, Marathas and the EIC** all tried to control it, because **legitimacy lived there**.
4. **Rajput heroism**: death before defeat, sung by **minstrels**; women were drawn in through ***sati*** — "those who followed the heroic ideal often had to pay for it with their lives."
5. **Kathak**: from ***katha*** (story) and temple **story-tellers** → **rasa lila** under **bhakti** (15th–16th c.) → **Mughal court** → **two gharanas, Jaipur and Lucknow** (**Wajid Ali Shah**) → disfavoured by the British, **kept alive by courtesans** → **classical only after independence**.
6. **Six classical dances:** Kathak, Bharatanatyam (TN), Kathakali (Kerala), Odissi (Odisha), Kuchipudi (AP), Manipuri (Manipur). ⭐ NCERT questions whether "classical" implies superiority.
7. **Miniatures**: water colour on cloth/paper; earliest illustrated **Jaina texts** in **western India**; Mughal patrons **Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan**; seen by **an exclusive few**.
8. **Mughal decline dispersed the painters** → Deccan and Rajput courts (**Mewar, Jodhpur, Bundi, Kota, Kishangarh**); **Basohli** (late 17th c., **bold and intense**, *Rasamanjari*); **Kangra** (after **Nadir Shah, 1739**; **soft cool blues and greens**, Vaishnavite).
9. ⭐ **Ordinary people painted on pots, walls, floors and cloth** — it simply did not survive, because **palaces preserve and mud walls do not**.
10. **Bengali** emerged over a millennium — Gupta **Brahmana settlement**, **Xuan Zang's** observation, the **Palas**, independent **Sultans**, **Akbar's conquest (1586)**; **Persian administered, Bengali flourished**; a common literary form by the **15th century**; loanwords from **tribal, Persian and European** languages.
11. **Two literatures:** Sanskrit-indebted (epics, ***Mangalakavyas***, **Chaitanyadeva**) — datable; and **independent** (**Nath** songs, **Maynamati and Gopichandra**, **Dharma Thakur**, folk tales) — **oral, undatable, strongest in eastern Bengal where Brahmana influence was weak**.
12. ***Pirs*** — a single term covering **Sufis, settlers, deified soldiers, Hindu and Buddhist deities and animistic spirits**; their shrines are everywhere in Bengal.
13. **Bengali temples** copied the **thatched hut** — ***dochala*** and ***chauchala*** — in **brick and terracotta**, often funded by **oil pressers and bell-metal workers enriched by European trade**; finest at **Vishnupur, Bankura**.
14. ⭐ **The *Brihaddharma Purana* (13th c.) permitted Bengal Brahmanas to eat fish** — **local practice bending the textual rule**, not the reverse.
