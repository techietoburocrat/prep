## 1️⃣ Words That Moved — The Core of This Chapter

### 🗺️ "HINDUSTAN" ⭐⭐⭐

**This is the single most-tested idea in the chapter.**

| Who / When | What they meant by it |
|---|---|
| **Minhaj-i Siraj**, 13th century (chronicler) | A **political** term: the lands of **Punjab, Haryana, and between the Ganga and Yamuna** — i.e. the areas within the **Delhi Sultanate's** control. ⭐ **It never included South India** |
| **Babur**, early 16th century | A **geographical and cultural** term: the **geography, fauna and culture of the inhabitants** of the subcontinent |
| **Amir Khusrau**, 14th century | Used **"Hind"** in a broadly similar cultural sense |
| **Today** | "India" — the modern **nation-state**, with political and national meaning |

> ⭐ **The exam point:** the idea of India as a geographical and cultural entity **did exist**, but **"Hindustan" did not carry the political-national meaning we now attach to it.** Reading modern nationhood back into a 13th-century word is precisely the error the chapter warns against.

### 🚶 "FOREIGNER" ⭐⭐

| Then | Now |
|---|---|
| **Any stranger** who appeared in a village — someone not part of *that* society or culture | Someone who is not an Indian citizen |

- Hindi: ***pardesi*** · Persian: ***ajnabi***
- A **city-dweller** might regard a **forest-dweller** as a foreigner
- But **two peasants of the same village were not foreigners to each other** — *even if they had different religions or castes* ⭐

> 🎯 **That last line is doing heavy lifting**, and it's excellent Mains/Essay material. Medieval identity was organised primarily around **locality and community**, not religion. The idea that religion was the master identity is largely a **later, colonial-era projection** — which sets up the periodisation critique at the end of the chapter.

## 2️⃣ Sources — Continuity and Discontinuity

| Continuity with Class 6 | Discontinuity (what's new) |
|---|---|
| Still coins, inscriptions, architecture, textual records | **Textual records increase dramatically** in number and variety, and slowly displace other sources |

### 📄 Paper changes everything

**Paper became progressively cheaper and more widely available.** NCERT makes the point with a comparison worth remembering:

```
  MID-13th CENTURY                    A CENTURY LATER (14th)
  ----------------                    -----------------------
  A scholar wants to copy a book.     You buy food in the market and
  He has no paper. So he WASHES       the shopkeeper might WRAP IT
  THE WRITING OFF an unwanted         FOR YOU IN PAPER.
  manuscript, dries it, and reuses
  the sheet.
        |                                        |
        +--------------------+-------------------+
                             v
              Paper was EXPENSIVE in the 13th century
              and CHEAP by the 14th.
```

**What paper was used for:** holy texts, **chronicles of rulers**, letters, teachings of saints, petitions, judicial records, **registers of accounts and taxes**.

Manuscripts were collected by wealthy people, rulers, monasteries and temples, and kept in **libraries and archives**.

### ✍️ The scribe problem ⭐⭐

There was **no printing press**. Scribes copied by hand — and **introduced small changes**, a word here, a sentence there.

```
   ORIGINAL  ->  copy  ->  copy  ->  copy  ->  copy  ->  what survives
                  |         |         |         |
                 drift     drift     drift     drift
                  |         |         |         |
                  +---------+---------+---------+
                            v
   After centuries, manuscripts of the SAME TEXT become
   SUBSTANTIALLY DIFFERENT from one another.

   And we RARELY have the author's original -- we are almost
   entirely dependent on later scribal copies.

   => Historians must read SEVERAL manuscript versions and
      GUESS what the author originally wrote.
```

> 💻 **This is an unversioned fork chain with silent mutation and no origin commit.** You have a dozen divergent copies, no upstream, and no diff against the original. The historian's job is essentially **reconstructing the ancestor from the descendants** — which is exactly what textual criticism does, and it is remarkably similar to inferring a common ancestor in phylogenetics.

**The complication on top:** authors themselves **revised** their work. The 14th-century chronicler **Ziyauddin Barani** wrote a chronicle and then **another version two years later** — the two differ, and historians didn't know about the second for a long time because it lay unnoticed in large library collections.

> 🎯 **Prelims-relevant:** Barani is the standard example of an author with two divergent versions. Also note **miniature paintings** — small illustrations (the one shown is 10.5 cm × 7.1 cm) used to illustrate manuscripts, so beautiful that **later collectors tore manuscripts apart to sell the miniatures alone.** That's a nice, quotable fact about how art markets destroy context.

## 3️⃣ New Social and Political Groups

The period 700–1750 is hard to summarise precisely because **so much changed at once.**

### ⚙️ New technologies and crops ⭐⭐

| New technologies | New crops |
|---|---|
| The **Persian wheel** (irrigation) | **Potatoes** |
| The **spinning wheel** (weaving) | **Corn (maize)** |
| **Firearms** in combat | **Chillies** |
| | **Tea** |
| | **Coffee** |

> ⚠️ **Prelims trap:** potatoes, corn, chillies, tea and coffee are **not indigenous** — they arrive in this period. Chillies and potatoes especially surprise people, since they feel utterly Indian today. (Chillies and corn come from the Americas via the Columbian Exchange; the point that "Indian food" is historically layered is good Essay material.)

> 🔑 **NCERT's framing is worth keeping:** these innovations **came along with people, who brought other ideas with them as well.** Technology never travels alone.

### 🐎 The Rajputs and other risers ⭐⭐

This was a period of **great mobility** — people travelled long distances in search of opportunity, and the subcontinent held wealth enough for fortunes to be made.

| Group | Detail |
|---|---|
| **RAJPUTS** ⭐⭐ | From ***"Rajaputra" = son of a ruler***. Between the **8th and 14th centuries** the term broadened to a group of **warriors claiming Kshatriya status** — including not just rulers and chieftains but **soldiers and commanders** serving in armies across the subcontinent. Their poets and bards attributed to them a **chivalric code**: extreme valour and a great sense of loyalty |
| **Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Ahoms** | Also used the opportunities of the age to become politically important |
| **Kayasthas** ⭐ | A **caste of scribes and secretaries** — rose through literacy and administration, not arms |

> 🧠 **The pattern:** in a period of constant state formation, **military skill and administrative literacy were both routes upward.** Being *born* a Kshatriya mattered less than being *accepted* as one — a point Ch.2 makes explicit with the *hiranya-garbha* ritual.

### 🌾 Forest clearing and the making of peasants

```
   Gradual CLEARING OF FORESTS + extension of agriculture
                    |
        +-----------+-----------+
        v                       v
   Forest-dwellers          Others start TILLING
   FORCED TO MIGRATE        the land -> become PEASANTS
   (habitat destroyed)                |
                                      v
                      Absorbed into large, complex societies:
                      required to PAY TAXES and offer goods
                      and services to local lords, chieftains,
                      priests, monasteries and temples
                                      |
                                      v
                      Significant ECONOMIC and SOCIAL
                      DIFFERENCES emerge among peasants
```

Some peasants kept cattle; some combined **artisanal work with agriculture in the lean season**.

### 🏷️ Jati ⭐⭐

As society became more differentiated, people were grouped into ***jatis*** (sub-castes), ranked by **birth and occupation**.

| Key facts about jati |
|---|
| ⭐ **Ranks were not fixed** — the status of the same jati **could vary from area to area** |
| Jatis **framed their own rules and regulations** to manage members' conduct |
| These were enforced by an **assembly of elders** — in some areas the ***jati panchayat*** ⭐ |
| But jatis were **also required to follow the rules of their villages** |
| A village was governed by a chieftain; jatis were **one small unit of a state** |

> 🔗 **Cross-link:** Class 6 Ch.4 gave you **varna** (four categories, from the Purusha Sukta). **Here you get *jati*** — the thousands of birth-based groups that are the actual lived structure of caste. **Varna ≠ jati**, and this chapter is where jati properly enters the story. Essential for GS1 Indian Society.

## 4️⃣ Region and Empire

By **700**, many regions already had **distinct geographical dimensions, their own language and cultural characteristics.**

### 🗣️ Amir Khusrau on language, 1318 ⭐⭐

The poet listed a **different language in every region**:

| Language | Region |
|---|---|
| **Sindhi** | Sind |
| **Lahori** | Lahore region |
| **Kashmiri** | Kashmir |
| **Dvarsamudri** | Southern Karnataka |
| **Telangani** | Andhra Pradesh |
| **Gujari** | Gujarat |
| **Ma'bari** | Tamil Nadu |
| **Gauri** | Bengal |
| **Awadhi** | Eastern Uttar Pradesh |
| **Hindawi** | The area around Delhi |

> ⭐ **And his contrast:** unlike these, **Sanskrit did not belong to any region.** It was an old language, and *"common people do not know it, only the Brahmanas do."*
>
> 🎯 **That observation is gold for Mains.** It captures in one line the difference between a **regional vernacular** (spoken, local, alive) and a **classical prestige language** (unregional, learned, restricted). It is exactly why the Bhakti and Sufi saints of Ch.6 chose the vernacular — and it echoes Class 6 Ch.6, where the Buddha chose Prakrit over Sanskrit for the same reason. **Same strategic choice, 1,800 years apart.**

### 👑 Pan-regional empires and exaggerated claims

Rulers made sweeping claims. A **Chola prashasti** describes its king as ruler of an empire stretching from **Gauda (Bengal)** in the east to **Gajjana (Ghazni, Afghanistan)** in the west, including all of **Dravida (south India)**.

**Historians regard these as exaggerated.** The interesting question NCERT poses is *why rulers kept making them* — the claim itself was a political act, asserting a status they aspired to.

**Pan-regional empires** — spanning diverse regions — were built by the **Cholas, Khaljis, Tughluqs and Mughals**. ⭐ **Not all were equally stable or successful.**

> 🗺️ **Note the source detail:** the map of Delhi Sultanate provinces under **Muhammad Tughluq** comes from an **Egyptian** source — the ***Masalik al-absar fi mamalik al-amsar*** of **Shihabuddin al-Umari**. A foreign observer documenting Indian administration — the Chapter 1 lesson of Class 6 recurring.

**When the Mughal Empire declined in the 18th century, regional states re-emerged** (Ch.8) — but they carried the **legacies** of the empires that had ruled them, visible in governance, economic management, elite cultures and language.

> 🔑 **NCERT's formulation, worth memorising:** *"These regions felt the impact of larger pan-regional forces of integration without ever quite losing their distinctiveness."* That sentence is the thesis of **Chapter 7** and a ready-made Mains opening.

## 5️⃣ Old and New Religions

### 🕉️ Changes within Hinduism

| Development |
|---|
| **Worship of new deities** |
| **Construction of temples by royalty** ⭐ |
| **Growing importance of Brahmanas** as a dominant group |
| Their position **consolidated by the support of patrons** — new rulers searching for prestige |

> 🔑 **The mechanism to notice:** rulers needed legitimacy; Brahmanas could confer it; rulers gave land and temples; Brahmanas grew powerful. **A mutually reinforcing bargain** — precisely the same bargain you saw between kings and priests in Class 6 Ch.5 (the Ashvamedha).

**A *patron*** = a wealthy individual who supports another person — an artiste, a craftsperson, a learned man, or a noble.

### 🙏 Bhakti emerges

> **One of the major developments of this period:** the idea of ***bhakti*** — **a loving, personal deity that devotees could reach WITHOUT the aid of priests or elaborate rituals.** (Full treatment in Ch.6.)

### ☪️ The arrival of Islam

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **How** | **Merchants and migrants** first brought Islam to India in the **seventh century** ⭐ |
| **Belief** | Muslims regard the **Quran** as their holy book and accept the sovereignty of the one God, **Allah**, whose love, mercy and bounty embrace all who believe in Him — ⭐ **without regard to social background** |
| **Patronage** | Many rulers were patrons of Islam and of learned theologians and jurists |
| **Diversity** ⭐ | Like Hinduism, **Islam was interpreted in a variety of ways by its followers** |

**The main division:**

| **SHIA** | **SUNNI** |
|---|---|
| Held that the Prophet Muhammad's son-in-law, **Ali**, was the legitimate leader of the Muslim community | Accepted the authority of the **early leaders (Khalifas)** of the community and the succeeding Khalifas |

**Also important:** other differences of theology and law, and **mystic traditions — the Sufis** (Ch.6).

> 🎯 **Note the emphasis NCERT chooses:** that Islam's appeal included acceptance **without regard to social background**, and that both Hinduism and Islam were internally **diverse**. Both points push against treating either as a monolith — which is the setup for the periodisation critique next.

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Thinking About Time and Historical Periods

**The most important section in the chapter for Mains and for general intellectual hygiene.**

### What historians mean by "time"

Not just hours, days or years on a clock. Time also reflects **changes in social and economic organisation**, and **the persistence and transformation of ideas and beliefs.** Dividing the past into **periods** — large segments with shared characteristics — makes study easier.

### ❌ The colonial periodisation

```
   In the MIDDLE OF THE 19th CENTURY, British historians divided
   Indian history into THREE periods:

        +-----------+     +-----------+     +-----------+
        |  "HINDU"  | --> | "MUSLIM"  | --> | "BRITISH" |
        +-----------+     +-----------+     +-----------+

   THE ASSUMPTION BUILT INTO IT:
   that the RELIGION OF RULERS was the ONLY important
   historical change -- and that there was nothing else
   worth noticing in the rich diversity of the subcontinent.
```

> ⚠️ **Associated above all with James Mill**, whose *History of British India* (1817) set out this scheme. NCERT attributes it to "British historians" of the mid-19th century.

### Why historians reject it — say this in an answer

1. It makes **the ruler's religion the master variable**, when economy, society, technology and culture changed independently of it
2. It implies medieval India was defined by religious conflict, **flattening the diversity** the chapter has just documented (recall: two peasants of different religions in one village were *not* foreigners to each other)
3. It treats "Hindu" and "Muslim" as **internally uniform**, when the chapter has shown both were **interpreted in many ways**
4. It laid intellectual groundwork for **communal readings of Indian history** — a live political issue

**What historians do instead:** *"Most look to **economic and social factors** to characterise the major elements of different moments of the past."*

### ⚠️ And the honest self-criticism

NCERT then turns the critique on its own book — which is intellectually admirable and very quotable:

| The problem with "medieval" |
|---|
| The **16th and 18th centuries were quite different** from the 8th or 11th. Describing the whole span as **one historical unit is not without its problems** |
| "Medieval" is often contrasted with "modern". **"Modernity" carries a sense of material progress and intellectual advancement** — which implies the medieval period **lacked any change whatsoever** |
| **But that was not the case.** Societies were transformed often, and economies in several regions reached a prosperity that **attracted European trading companies** |

> 🎯 **This is a gift for Essay and GS1.** The idea that *"medieval" is a label that smuggles in a judgement* — stagnation, backwardness, a gap between two bright ages — is exactly the kind of critical observation that lifts an answer. **Periodisation is never neutral; it encodes what the person doing the dividing thought mattered.**

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Two maps** (Al-Idrisi 1154, French 1720s) show that sources are shaped by **who made them and why**.
2. **"Hindustan" moved:** political and northern for **Minhaj-i Siraj** (13th c., **excluding the south**), geographical and cultural for **Babur** (16th c.), national for us.
3. **"Foreigner"** meant any stranger to a locality — *pardesi*, *ajnabi*. **Two peasants of different religions in one village were not foreigners to each other.**
4. **Paper got cheap** between the 13th and 14th centuries, and textual records multiplied.
5. **Scribal copying introduced drift**; we rarely have originals; **Barani** even wrote two versions himself.
6. **New technologies:** Persian wheel, spinning wheel, firearms. **New crops:** potatoes, corn, chillies, tea, coffee.
7. **New groups rose** — **Rajputs** (from *Rajaputra*), Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Ahoms, and the scribal **Kayasthas**.
8. **Forests were cleared**, forest-dwellers displaced, peasants absorbed into taxpaying society, and **jatis** emerged — **ranked differently in different regions**, governed by **jati panchayats**.
9. **Amir Khusrau (1318)** listed the regional languages and noted **Sanskrit belonged to no region**.
10. **Religion changed:** temple Hinduism and Brahmana dominance grew under royal patronage; **bhakti** emerged; **Islam arrived in the 7th century** with merchants and migrants and was **interpreted in many ways** (Shia/Sunni/Sufi).
11. ⭐ **The "Hindu–Muslim–British" periodisation** (mid-19th-c. British historians, above all **James Mill**) assumed the ruler's religion was the only thing that mattered. **Historians reject it** and use economic and social criteria instead.
12. **Even "medieval" is problematic** — it implies a stagnant gap before "modernity", which the evidence contradicts.
