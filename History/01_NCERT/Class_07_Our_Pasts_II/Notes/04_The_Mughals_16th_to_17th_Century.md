## 🎬 Start Here: Why the Red Fort Still Matters

> **"Today the Prime Minister of India addresses the nation on Independence Day from the ramparts of the Red Fort in Delhi, the residence of the Mughal emperors."**

NCERT opens with this, and it's a deliberate point. The Mughals **"imposed structures of administration and ideas of governance that outlasted their rule, leaving a political legacy that succeeding rulers of the subcontinent could not ignore."**

```
   THE ACHIEVEMENT, STATED PRECISELY

   Ruling a territory as large as the subcontinent,
   with such a DIVERSITY of people and cultures,
   was an extremely difficult task.

   Quite IN CONTRAST TO THEIR PREDECESSORS, the Mughals
   created an empire and accomplished what had hitherto
   seemed possible only for SHORT PERIODS of time.

   From the latter half of the 16th century they expanded
   from AGRA and DELHI, until in the 17th century they
   controlled NEARLY ALL of the subcontinent.
```

> 🔑 **The word doing the work is "diversity".** Many empires have been large. The Mughal problem was governing **many religions, languages, castes and regional elites at once** — and the answer they arrived at (*sulh-i kul* plus a deliberately mixed nobility) is what makes them interesting rather than merely big.

## 1️⃣ Who Were the Mughals? ⭐⭐

### Double descent

```
        MOTHER'S SIDE                    FATHER'S SIDE
              |                                |
        GENGHIS KHAN                        TIMUR
        (died 1227)                       (died 1404)
        Mongol ruler over parts        Ruler of Iran, Iraq and
        of China and Central Asia      modern-day Turkey
              |                                |
              +----------------+---------------+
                               |
                          THE MUGHALS
```

### ⭐ Why they hated the word "Mughal"

| Reason |
|---|
| **Genghis Khan's memory was associated with the massacre of innumerable people** |
| It was also **linked with the Uzbegs — their Mongol competitors** (and the very people who had driven Babur from his ancestral throne) |

**By contrast, they were PROUD of their TIMURID ancestry** — not least because **their great ancestor had captured Delhi** (Timur's invasion of 1398).

> ⭐ **A lovely detail worth remembering:** *"They celebrated their genealogy pictorially, **each ruler getting a picture made of Timur and himself**."* Dynastic legitimacy asserted through **painting** — a reminder that Mughal art was political instrument as much as decoration.
>
> 🎯 **Exercise question straight from the book:** *"Why did the Mughals emphasise their Timurid and not their Mongol descent?"* You now have the two-part answer.

### 🐎 Babur

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Became ruler at** | **12 years old** |
| **Lost his throne** | Forced to leave his ancestral throne (Ferghana) due to **invasion by another Mongol group, the UZBEGS** ⭐ |
| **Then** | After **years of wandering**, he **seized KABUL in 1504** ⭐ |
| **1526** ⭐⭐ | Defeated **Ibrahim Lodi** at **Panipat** and **captured Delhi and Agra** |

> 🔗 **1526 is the hinge date of the book** — the Delhi Sultanate (Ch.3) ends and the Mughal Empire begins. **Cannons were an important addition to sixteenth-century warfare, and Babur used them effectively.**

### 👑 The emperors — the sequence to know

| Ruler | Reign |
|---|---|
| **Babur** | 1526 – 1530 |
| **Humayun** | 1530 – 1540, and again 1555 – 1556 |
| *(**Sher Shah Suri** interregnum)* | *1540 – 1555 (Ch.3)* |
| ⭐ **AKBAR** | **1556 – 1605** |
| **Jahangir** | 1605 – 1627 |
| **Shah Jahan** | 1627 – 1658 |
| ⭐ **Aurangzeb** | **1658 – 1707** |

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ Traditions of Succession

| System | Meaning | Did the Mughals use it? |
|---|---|---|
| **PRIMOGENITURE** | The **eldest son** inherits his father's estate | ❌ **NO** |
| **COPARCENARY INHERITANCE** ⭐ | **Division of the inheritance amongst ALL the sons** — the Mughal and Timurid custom | ✅ **YES** |

> 🎯 **The consequence, which NCERT leaves you to work out:** if every son has a claim, **every succession is a potential civil war.** And that is exactly what happened — Aurangzeb reached the throne by defeating and killing his brothers and imprisoning Shah Jahan.
>
> 💻 **Compare with Chapter 3's instability.** The Sultanate's problem was that *loyalty didn't transfer across reigns*; the Mughal problem was that *the succession rule guaranteed multiple valid claimants*. **Two different empires, two different unsolved continuity problems.** That comparison makes a strong Mains paragraph.
>
> NCERT's own question is worth answering: *which is fairer, primogeniture or coparcenary?* Coparcenary is more equitable **between sons** but destabilising **for the state**.

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐ Mughal Relations with Other Rulers

The Mughals **campaigned constantly against rulers who refused to accept their authority.** But as they became powerful, **many rulers also joined them voluntarily.**

### The Rajputs — the model case

| Pattern |
|---|
| **Many married their daughters into Mughal families and received high positions** |
| **But many resisted as well** |

**⭐ The Sisodiya Rajputs of Mewar** refused to accept Mughal authority for a long time. **Once defeated, however:**

```
   They were HONOURABLY TREATED by the Mughals,
   given their lands back as assignments (WATAN JAGIR).

              |
              v
   "The careful balance between DEFEATING but NOT
    HUMILIATING their opponents enabled the Mughals
    to extend their influence over many kings and
    chieftains."
```

> ⭐⭐ **"Defeating but not humiliating" is the single most quotable phrase in this chapter.** It is a genuine statecraft principle: a defeated enemy who keeps his lands, honour and a place in your system becomes an **asset**; one who is humiliated becomes a **permanent insurgency**.
>
> 🎯 **Deploy it in GS4 (conflict resolution, magnanimity in victory), in GS2 (federalism and accommodation of regional elites), and in Essay.** It is also the direct contrast with Aurangzeb's later, harsher approach — which is part of the explanation for Mughal decline in Ch.8.

**⭐ Marriage alliances — the two examples NCERT gives:**

| Emperor | His mother |
|---|---|
| **JAHANGIR** | A **KACHHWAHA** princess, daughter of the Rajput ruler of **AMBER** (modern-day **Jaipur**) |
| **SHAH JAHAN** | A **RATHOR** princess, daughter of the Rajput ruler of **MARWAR** (**Jodhpur**) |

> 🧠 **Learn the pairings:** Kachhwaha–Amber/Jaipur; Rathor–Marwar/Jodhpur; **Sisodiya–Mewar** (the resisters). These appear as match-the-following in the textbook itself, which is a strong hint about how they get tested.

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Mansabdars and Jagirdars — The Core Machinery

### A deliberately mixed nobility

```
   From a SMALL NUCLEUS of Turkish nobles (TURANIS)
   the Mughals expanded recruitment to include:

     IRANIANS  |  INDIAN MUSLIMS  |  AFGHANS
     RAJPUTS   |  MARATHAS        |  and other groups

   Those who joined Mughal service were enrolled
   as MANSABDARS.
```

> 🎯 **NCERT's exercise question asks why the Mughals recruited from diverse backgrounds rather than just Turanis and Iranis.** The answer: an empire of that diversity could only be held by **co-opting regional elites into the imperial service**, giving them a stake in its survival. **A nobility drawn from one ethnic group would have had no reach into Rajasthan, the Deccan or Bengal.** This is *sulh-i kul* expressed as personnel policy.

### The terms — learn these precisely ⭐⭐⭐

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ***MANSAB*** | A **position or rank** |
| ***MANSABDAR*** | **An individual who holds a mansab** |
| ***ZAT*** ⭐ | The **numerical value** determining **rank and salary**. **The higher the zat, the more prestigious the position and the larger the salary** |
| ***SAWAR*** ⭐ | The **number of cavalrymen** a mansabdar was required to maintain |
| ***JAGIR*** ⭐ | The **revenue assignment** given to a mansabdar as salary |
| ***JAGIRDAR*** | The **holder of a jagir** |

**⭐ Zat in practice:**
- Nobles with a **zat of 5,000** ranked higher than those of **1,000**
- In **Akbar's reign there were 29 mansabdars with a rank of 5,000 zat**
- **By Aurangzeb's reign the number had increased to 79** ⭐ *(NCERT asks: would this have meant more expenditure for the state? — **yes**, and it's the seed of the crisis below)*

### 🐎 The military obligation

```
   The MANSABDAR must maintain a specified number of SAWAR
   (cavalrymen). The process:

     1. He BRINGS his cavalrymen FOR REVIEW
     2. They are REGISTERED
     3. Their HORSES ARE BRANDED
     4. He then RECEIVES MONEY to pay them as salary
```

> 💻 **Steps 2 and 3 are an anti-fraud mechanism, and a good one.** Registering the men and **branding the horses** prevents a mansabdar from borrowing the same troopers (or horses) to pass multiple inspections, or from claiming pay for soldiers who don't exist. **It is verification of a claimed resource before disbursing payment against it** — the *dagh* (branding) and *chehra* (descriptive roll) system, and it dates back to Alauddin Khalji.

### ⭐ Jagirdar vs Muqti — a distinction UPSC likes

| | ***MUQTI*** (Ch.3, Sultanate) | ***JAGIRDAR*** (Mughal) |
|---|---|---|
| Held | An **iqta** | A **jagir** |
| Residence | **Resided in and administered** his iqta | ⭐ **Most did NOT reside in or administer their jagir** |
| Revenue | Collected it himself | **Collected by his servants**, while he **served in some other part of the country** |
| Rights | Governance + revenue | ⭐ **Only rights to the revenue** |

> ⭐ **This is a real conceptual advance.** By separating **where you are paid from** and **where you serve**, the Mughals broke the link between an officer and a territorial power base. A jagirdar could not build a regional following in a district he never visited. **It is the *iqta* idea taken one step further toward a salaried bureaucracy.**

### 📉 ⭐⭐⭐ The Jagirdari Crisis

This is the chapter's most important causal chain, and it feeds directly into Chapter 8.

```
   AKBAR'S REIGN                    AURANGZEB'S REIGN
   -------------                    -----------------
   Jagirs CAREFULLY ASSESSED        Actual revenue collected was
   so their revenues were           OFTEN LESS than the granted sum
   ROUGHLY EQUAL to the                        |
   mansabdar's salary                          |
                                    HUGE INCREASE in the NUMBER
                                    of mansabdars
                                                |
                                                v
                                    -> A LONG WAIT before a
                                       mansabdar received a jagir
                                    -> A SHORTAGE of jagirs
                                                |
                                                v
                    Many jagirdars tried to EXTRACT AS MUCH
                    REVENUE AS POSSIBLE while they had a jagir
                                                |
                                                v
                    Aurangzeb was UNABLE TO CONTROL these
                    developments in the last years of his reign
                                                |
                                                v
                    "THE PEASANTRY THEREFORE SUFFERED
                     TREMENDOUSLY."
```

> 🎯 **Why this is such good exam material:** it is a **structural** explanation of imperial decline, not a moral one. Nobody had to be wicked. **More nobles + less revenue per jagir + short tenure = rational short-term extraction.** The incentive to strip an asset you will hold briefly is exactly what you'd predict.
>
> 💻 It's a **tragedy-of-the-commons with a rotation policy attached** — the rotation that protected the state from regional power bases (above) simultaneously **removed any incentive to preserve the productive capacity of the land**. **The same design feature that solved one problem created another.** Say that in a Mains answer and it lands.

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐ Zabt and Zamindars

### The revenue base

> **The main source of income available to Mughal rulers was tax on the produce of the peasantry.**

In most places, peasants paid taxes **through the rural elites** — the **headman or the local chieftain**.

**⭐ *ZAMINDAR*** — the Mughals used **one term** to describe **all intermediaries**, whether **local headmen of villages** or **powerful chieftains**.

> 🎯 **The flattening of a hugely varied reality into one administrative category is itself significant** — a village headman and a regional chieftain were very different people with very different power, but the Mughal state dealt with both through a single legal-fiscal label. **This is a state simplifying society in order to govern it**, and it's a strong point for an answer on state capacity.

### ⭐⭐ The *zabt* system

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Who** | **TODAR MAL**, Akbar's **revenue minister** ⭐ |
| **What he did** | Carried out a **careful survey of crop yields, prices and areas cultivated** for a **10-year period** |
| **The unit** | Each **province was divided into revenue circles**, each with **its own schedule of revenue rates for individual crops** ⭐ |
| **The name** | This revenue system was known as ***ZABT*** |
| ⭐ **Where it worked** | Only where **Mughal administrators could survey the land and keep very careful accounts** |
| ⭐ **Where it did NOT** | **Provinces such as GUJARAT and BENGAL** |

> ⭐ **That last row is the most-missed fact in the chapter and a favourite trap.** *Zabt* was **not** empire-wide. It required survey capacity, and where the state lacked it, other systems were used. **Never write "the Mughals used zabt throughout the empire."**
>
> 🔗 **Note the lineage again:** careful survey and measurement → **Sher Shah** → **Todar Mal under Akbar**. Ch.3's closing point, delivered.

### ⚔️ Zamindars and revolt

> In some areas **the zamindars exercised a great deal of power.** Exploitation by Mughal administrators could drive them into rebellion — and when **zamindars and peasants of the same caste allied** in rebelling against Mughal authority, the result was serious.

**⭐ "These peasant revolts challenged the stability of the Mughal Empire from the end of the seventeenth century."**

> 🎯 **Note what makes the alliance dangerous: shared CASTE.** Caste solidarity cut across the fiscal hierarchy that the state relied on — the intermediary and the taxpayer made common cause. **This is a key mechanism behind the 18th-century collapse (Ch.8)**, and it connects to the Jat and Maratha risings.

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐ The Sources: *Akbar Nama* and *Ain-i Akbari*

Akbar ordered **ABUL FAZL**, one of his close friends and courtiers, to write a history of his reign.

| Volume | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Volume 1** | **Akbar's ancestors** |
| **Volume 2** | **The events of Akbar's reign** |
| **Volume 3 = *AIN-I AKBARI*** ⭐ | **Akbar's administration, household, army, the revenues and the geography of his empire** — plus **rich details about the traditions and culture of the people living in India** |

**⭐ What makes the *Ain-i Akbari* remarkable:**

> *"The most interesting aspect about the Ain-i Akbari is its rich **STATISTICAL DETAILS** about things as diverse as **crops, yields, prices, wages and revenues**."*

> 💻 **This is essentially an imperial dataset** — a systematic, quantified account of an empire's economy, compiled in the 1590s. For historians it is extraordinary, because it allows **quantitative** economic history of a pre-modern Indian state, which almost no other source permits.
>
> ⚠️ **But apply Chapter 1's caution:** Abul Fazl was Akbar's **close friend and courtier**, writing an official history. The statistics are invaluable; the **evaluations of Akbar are not neutral**. Same rule as the *tawarikh* and the *prashastis*.

## 7️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ *SULH-I KUL* — "Universal Peace"

**The most important idea in the chapter, and possibly in the book.**

Akbar's nobles included **Turanis, Iranis, Indian Muslims, Afghans, Rajputs and Marathas** — men of many faiths. He held **debates with religious scholars** of different traditions, and with **Abul Fazl's** help framed the idea of ***sulh-i kul*** so that he could **govern a society composed of many religions, cultures and castes.**

**⭐⭐ Jahangir's description of his father's policy — worth knowing closely:**

> *"As in the wide expanse of the divine compassion there is room for all classes and the followers of all creeds, so ... in his Imperial dominions, which on all sides were limited only by the sea, **there was room for the professors of opposite religions, and for beliefs, good and bad, and the road to intolerance was closed.** **Sunnis and Shias met in one mosque and Christians and Jews in one church to pray.** He consistently followed the principle of 'universal peace' (**sulh-i kul**)."*

> 🎯 **Read the two underlined moves carefully, because they are what make this quotable:**
> 1. **"beliefs, good and bad"** — tolerance is extended **even to beliefs the ruler considers wrong**. That is a far stronger claim than tolerating what you approve of. It is close to the modern liberal position.
> 2. **"the road to intolerance was closed"** — intolerance is framed as **the thing the state acts against**, rather than a private attitude the state ignores.
>
> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is the single best pre-modern Indian citation for:**
> - **GS2** — the indigenous antecedents of Indian secularism (alongside Ashoka's **Rock Edict XII**, Class 6 Ch.7)
> - **GS4 Ethics** — tolerance, pluralism, the ethics of governing diversity
> - **GS1** — Mughal cultural policy
> - **Essay** — unity in diversity, composite culture
>
> 🔗 **Pair it with Ashoka's RE XII.** Ashoka argued that attacking another sect **harms your own**; Akbar's court argued that the state must **close the road to intolerance**. Two emperors, 1,900 years apart, reaching compatible conclusions about governing a plural society. **That pairing, deployed in an introduction, is genuinely strong.**

## 8️⃣ ⭐ Nur Jahan

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Name** | **MEHRUNNISA** |
| **Married** | Emperor **JAHANGIR in 1611** |
| **Title received** | **NUR JAHAN** |
| ⭐ **Coins** | As a mark of honour, **Jahangir struck SILVER COINS** bearing **his own titles on one side** and on the other the inscription **"struck in the name of the Queen Begum, Nur Jahan"** |
| ⭐ ***Farman*** | She issued **farmans** (royal orders) under her own seals |
| **Square seal** | *"Command of her most Sublime and Elevated Majesty **Nur Jahan Padshah Begum**"* |
| **Round seal** | *"by the sun of Shah Jahangir she became as brilliant as the moon; may Nur Jahan Padshah be the lady of the age"* |

> 🎯 **Why the coins and the *farman* matter as evidence:** minting coinage and issuing *farmans* are **sovereign acts**. That Nur Jahan's name appeared on both is **hard, material proof of real political authority**, not court flattery.
>
> 🔗 **Compare with Chapter 3's queens.** Raziyya put her **father's** name on her coins to borrow legitimacy; **Nur Jahan's own name and titles appear on hers.** Rudramadevi had to pose as a man. **Three centuries, three positions — and a genuinely comparative answer for a GS1 gender question.**

## 9️⃣ ⭐⭐ The Empire in the 17th Century — Wealth and Its Distribution

### The paradox international travellers reported

> The administrative and military efficiency of the empire led to **great economic and commercial prosperity**. International travellers described it as **"the fabled land of wealth"**.
>
> **But these same visitors were also appalled at the state of poverty that existed side by side with the greatest opulence. THE INEQUALITIES WERE GLARING.**

### ⭐⭐ The statistic you should memorise

**Documents from the twentieth year of Shah Jahan's reign:**

```
   The HIGHEST-RANKING mansabdars were only

              445  out of a total of  8,000

                   = about 5.6% of all mansabdars

   ...and this small group received

              61.5%  OF THE TOTAL ESTIMATED REVENUE
                     OF THE EMPIRE

   as salaries for themselves and their troopers.
```

> ⭐ **"5.6% of the nobility took 61.5% of imperial revenue" is one of the most powerful single facts in the whole NCERT History series.** It is precise, sourced, and immediately usable in an Economy, Society or Essay answer on inequality.

### 💸 The economic consequence — a genuinely sophisticated argument

```
   Emperors and mansabdars spent a great deal of income on
   SALARIES and GOODS
              |
              v
   This BENEFITED the artisans and peasantry who SUPPLIED
   them with goods and produce
              |
              v
   BUT: "the scale of revenue collection left VERY LITTLE FOR
   INVESTMENT in the hands of the PRIMARY PRODUCERS --
   the peasant and the artisan."
              |
              v
   The POOREST lived HAND TO MOUTH and could hardly consider
   investing in additional TOOLS AND SUPPLIES to
   INCREASE PRODUCTIVITY
              |
              v
   Only the WEALTHIER PEASANTRY, artisanal groups, merchants
   and bankers PROFITED in this economic world.
```

> 🎯 **This is an argument about why growth did not compound**, and it is unusually sophisticated for a Class 7 book. **Extraction at that scale prevented capital formation at the bottom.** Demand existed (the elite bought a great deal), but the producers could never accumulate enough surplus to invest in productivity.
>
> ⭐ **Superb material for GS3 (economy) and Essay:** it is a pre-modern illustration of the argument that **high inequality suppresses productive investment** — and a direct answer to the "but the Mughal empire was fabulously rich" line. **Rich empire, poor producers, no compounding.**

### 📉 And then the centre weakens

> The enormous wealth and resources commanded by the Mughal elite made them an **extremely powerful group** in the late seventeenth century. As the authority of the Mughal emperor slowly declined, **his servants emerged as powerful centres of power in the regions.** They **constituted new dynasties** and held the command of provinces like **HYDERABAD and AWADH**. Although they **continued to recognise the Mughal emperor in Delhi as their master**, by the eighteenth century the provinces **had consolidated their independent political identities.**

> 🔗 **That is Chapter 8 in a paragraph** — and note the mechanism: the successor states were founded **not by rebels but by the empire's own officers**. **The empire was not overthrown; it was inherited from the inside.**

| **Inequality statistic** ⭐⭐ | **445 of 8,000** mansabdars (**5.6%**) took **61.5%** of estimated revenue (**20th year of Shah Jahan**) |
| **Successor provinces** | **Hyderabad** and **Awadh** |

**Traps:**
- **"The Mughals followed primogeniture"** → **FALSE.** **Coparcenary**
- **"Jagirdars administered their jagirs like muqtis"** → **FALSE.** Most **never resided there**; they held **only revenue rights**
- **"Zabt was applied throughout the empire"** → **FALSE.** Not in **Gujarat and Bengal**
- **"Zat indicated the number of cavalrymen"** → **FALSE.** That's ***sawar***; **zat** fixed rank and salary
- **"The Mughals proudly called themselves Mongols"** → **FALSE.** They **disliked** it
- **"Nur Jahan's influence is known only from later legend"** → **FALSE.** **Coins and farmans** are contemporary material evidence

### Mains

- **GS2 / GS4 / Essay ⭐⭐⭐:** ***sulh-i kul*** — quote Jahangir; pair with **Ashoka's Rock Edict XII**; the indigenous roots of Indian pluralism
- **Administration ⭐⭐:** mansabdari as an attempt at a **non-territorial, salaried nobility**; contrast *jagirdar* with *muqti*
- **Decline ⭐⭐⭐:** the **jagirdari crisis** as a structural, incentive-driven explanation — far better than "weak successors"
- **Economy/GS3 ⭐⭐:** the 61.5% statistic and the **investment-suppression argument** — wealth without capital formation
- **Gender (GS1) ⭐:** Nur Jahan's coins and farmans vs Raziyya's and Rudramadevi's strategies (Ch.3)
- **Statecraft/GS4:** *"defeating but not humiliating"* as a principle of conflict resolution

## 🔁 2-MINUTE RECAP

1. **Descent:** **Genghis Khan** (mother's side), **Timur** (father's side). They **rejected "Mughal"** (massacre, Uzbeg association) and **celebrated Timurid** ancestry — in paintings pairing each ruler with Timur.
2. **Babur:** ruler at 12, driven out by **Uzbegs**, took **Kabul 1504**, won **Delhi and Agra in 1526** using cannon.
3. **Succession was coparcenary, not primogeniture** — every son a claimant, so every succession a war.
4. **Rajput policy:** marriage alliances (**Kachhwaha–Amber**, **Rathor–Marwar**) and, for the resisting **Sisodiyas of Mewar**, **defeat without humiliation** and lands returned as ***watan jagir***.
5. **Nobility deliberately mixed** — Turanis, Iranis, Indian Muslims, Afghans, Rajputs, Marathas — all enrolled as **mansabdars**.
6. ***Zat*** = rank and salary; ***sawar*** = cavalry obligation, with men **registered** and horses **branded**; ***jagir*** = revenue assignment.
7. **Jagirdars ≠ muqtis** — they **did not live in or govern** their jagirs, holding **only revenue rights** while serving elsewhere.
8. **The jagirdari crisis:** more mansabdars (**29 → 79** at 5,000 zat), shrinking real revenue, long waits and short tenures → **maximum extraction** → **"the peasantry suffered tremendously."**
9. ***Zabt*** — **Todar Mal's** 10-year survey, revenue circles, crop-wise rates — **but not in Gujarat and Bengal**.
10. ***Zamindar*** covered **all intermediaries**; when zamindars and peasants **of the same caste** allied, revolts **challenged the empire from the end of the 17th century**.
11. ***Akbar Nama*** by **Abul Fazl**, three volumes; **Ain-i Akbari** is volume 3 and is exceptional for its **statistics**.
12. ***Sulh-i kul*** — "universal peace"; room even for **"beliefs, good and bad"**; **"the road to intolerance was closed."**
13. **Nur Jahan (Mehrunnisa)** married Jahangir in **1611**; **silver coins** struck in her name; she issued **farmans** under her own seals.
14. **445 of 8,000 mansabdars took 61.5% of imperial revenue** — wealth coexisting with glaring poverty, and **too little left with peasants and artisans to invest in productivity**.
15. **The empire's own officers** became the rulers of **Hyderabad** and **Awadh** — Chapter 8 begins here.
