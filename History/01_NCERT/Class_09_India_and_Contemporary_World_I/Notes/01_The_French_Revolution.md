# Chapter 1 — The French Revolution

> **The one-line point:** A **bankrupt king** called a meeting he had not called in **175 years**, to make the only group already paying tax pay more. That meeting asked a question nobody could put back in the box — **who does the nation actually belong to?** The words we now use for politics — *citizen, nation, rights, liberty, equality* — were forged in the five years that followed.

## 🎬 Start Here: 14 July 1789

Paris wakes up frightened. The king has ordered troops into the city and the rumour is that he means to fire on his own people.

```
   MORNING   7,000 men and women gather at the town hall.
             They decide to form a people's MILITIA.
             They break into government buildings looking for arms.
                            |
   AFTERNOON Several hundred march east to the BASTILLE,
             the fortress-prison, hoping for hoarded ammunition.
                            |
             The commander is killed. The prisoners are released.
             There are SEVEN of them.
                            |
   AFTER     The fortress is demolished. Its stones are sold
             in the markets as souvenirs.
```

⭐ **Read that "seven" carefully — it is the whole point.** The Bastille held almost no one. It was stormed because *"it stood for the despotic power of the king."* This is the first thing to understand about the Revolution: **it was fought over symbols and meanings as much as over bread and taxes.**

> ⚠️ **And NCERT's honest caveat, which is very quotable:** historians later saw 14 July as the beginning of a chain of events ending in the king's execution — **"though most people at the time did not anticipate this outcome."** Nobody storming the Bastille was planning to behead Louis XVI. Revolutions are read backwards; they are not lived that way.

## 1️⃣ French Society Before 1789 — The Old Regime

### 🏛️ The society of estates

```
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  1st ESTATE — THE CLERGY                             │
   │  privileged by BIRTH · pays NO tax to the state      │
   │  COLLECTS the tithe (1/10 of agricultural produce)   │
   ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  2nd ESTATE — THE NOBILITY                           │
   │  privileged by BIRTH · pays NO tax to the state      │
   │  COLLECTS feudal dues + forced labour from peasants  │
   ├══════════════════════════════════════════════════════┤
   │  3rd ESTATE — EVERYBODY ELSE                         │
   │                                                      │
   │    RICH:  big businessmen, merchants, court          │
   │           officials, lawyers                         │
   │    POOR:  peasants and artisans                      │
   │  POOREST: small peasants, landless labour, servants  │
   │                                                      │
   │  PAYS: the tithe (to Church)                         │
   │        feudal dues + labour service (to nobles)      │
   │        the TAILLE — direct tax (to the state)        │
   │        indirect taxes on salt, tobacco (to the state)│
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

⭐⭐⭐ **The single sentence to remember:** *"The burden of financing activities of the state through taxes was borne by the third estate alone."*

**The numbers that make it worse:**

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| Peasants as share of population | **about 90%** |
| Land actually owned by peasants | only a **small number** owned what they cultivated |
| Land owned by nobles + Church + rich third estate | **about 60%** |
| Population 1715 → 1789 | **23 million → 28 million** |

> **The word to know:** *Old Regime* (*Ancien Régime*) = **the society and institutions of France before 1789.** The society of estates was part of the **feudal system dating back to the middle ages**.

**NCERT's own image for the relationship** is an anonymous etching called *The Spider and the Fly*: *"The nobleman is the spider, the peasant the fly."* The peasant brings grain, fruit, money, salad; the fat lord *"does not even care to grace him with a look."*

### 🍞 1.1 The struggle to survive — a subsistence crisis

This is a **chain**, and NCERT explicitly asks you to fill in the chain, so learn it as a chain:

```
        BAD HARVEST
             |
             v
      SCARCITY OF GRAIN
             |
             v
      RISING FOOD PRICES
             |
             v
   THE POOREST CAN NO LONGER BUY BREAD  ────>  FOOD RIOTS
             |
             v
       WEAKER BODIES
             |
             v
      DISEASE EPIDEMICS
             |
             v
   INCREASED NUMBER OF DEATHS
```

**Why it kept happening under the Old Regime:**
- Population rose **23 → 28 million** ⇒ demand for foodgrain rose.
- Grain **production could not keep pace** ⇒ price of **bread**, the staple diet, rose rapidly.
- **Wages were fixed by workshop owners** and did **not** keep pace with prices.
- ⇒ *"the gap between the poor and the rich widened"*, and any drought or hail tipped it into crisis.

> **Definition to memorise:** ⭐ **Subsistence crisis — an extreme situation where the basic means of livelihood are endangered.**

### 💡 1.3 The middle class asks a new question

Peasants and workers had revolted before — over taxes, over food. **They had always failed.** NCERT's explanation is precise and worth quoting:

> *"They lacked the means and programmes to carry out full-scale measures that would bring about a change in the social and economic order."*

⭐⭐ **That is the analytical point: anger is not a programme.** What changed in the eighteenth century was the arrival of a group that had **both** grievance **and** a programme — the **middle class**.

**Who they were and where their money came from:**
- Merchants and manufacturers grown rich on **expanding overseas trade** and on **manufacture of woollen and silk textiles**.
- Plus professions — **lawyers, administrative officials**.
- All **educated**.

**Their one belief, which is the seed of everything that follows:**

> ⭐⭐⭐ *"No group in society should be privileged by birth. Rather, a person's social position must depend on his **merit**."*

### 🧠 The four philosophers — learn one line each

| Thinker | Work | The idea |
|---|---|---|
| **John Locke** | *Two Treatises of Government* | **Refuted the doctrine of the divine and absolute right of the monarch** |
| **Jean Jacques Rousseau** | *The Social Contract* | Government based on a **social contract between people and their representatives**; **one person, one vote** |
| **Montesquieu** | *The Spirit of the Laws* | **Division of power** between **legislature, executive, judiciary** |
| *(the working example)* | **The American Constitution** | Montesquieu's model **put into force in the USA** after the thirteen colonies' independence; its **guarantee of individual rights** was an important example for French thinkers |

⭐⭐⭐ **Note the direct line to your Polity syllabus.** Separation of powers, natural rights, popular sovereignty, social contract — you will meet all four again in Laxmikanth. They enter modern politics *here*.

### ☕ How ideas travelled without mass literacy

```
   SALONS  +  COFFEE-HOUSES   ->  intensive discussion
             |
   BOOKS   +  NEWSPAPERS      ->  and crucially,
             |
   READ ALOUD IN GROUPS       ->  "for the benefit of those who
                                   could not read and write"
```

**Remember this mechanism.** It answers the obvious objection — *how does political philosophy reach people who cannot read?* You will see the same mechanism again in the Indian national movement.

### 🗣️ The lived grievance — Danton's letter (Source A)

Georges Danton, later a revolutionary, on finishing his studies:

> *"The choice of a career in the army was not open to me **as I was not a noble by birth**, nor did I have a patron. The church too could not offer me a refuge. I could not buy an office as I did not possess a sou… **the system had provided us with an education without however offering a field where our talents could be utilised.**"*

⭐⭐ This is the **educated-and-blocked** grievance, and it is the most dangerous kind a society can generate. Keep it — it is directly transferable to essays and to the Indian middle class of the 1880s in **Class 8 Ch.8**.

## 2️⃣ The Outbreak — 1789 in Four Dates

### 🏠 A way to see the voting dispute clearly

Picture a housing society with **three blocks**. Block A has 300 flats, Block B has 300, Block C has 600. **Only Block C pays maintenance.** The society's rule is **one vote per block** — so A and B, who pay nothing, can always outvote C two-to-one and raise C's maintenance again.

Now C says: *stop counting blocks, count flats.* That single procedural demand would flip every future decision. **That is the Estates-General of 1789**, and it is why a dispute over voting procedure — not a battle — is the actual hinge of the Revolution.

### 📅 The sequence

```
   1614 ─────────────────────────────────────── last meeting of
                                                the Estates-General
              (175 YEARS of not asking anyone)

   5 MAY 1789    ESTATES-GENERAL convened at Versailles
                 1st estate: 300 reps  ┐  seated in rows
                 2nd estate: 300 reps  ┘  facing each other
                 3rd estate: 600 reps  ->  MADE TO STAND AT THE BACK
                 Peasants, artisans and WOMEN: denied entry
                 (but 40,000 letters of grievances were carried in)
                        |
                 DISPUTE: one vote per ESTATE (king)
                          vs one vote per MEMBER (third estate)
                        |
                 King refuses. Third estate WALKS OUT.
                        v
   20 JUNE 1789  THE TENNIS COURT OATH
                 They assemble in an indoor tennis court at Versailles,
                 declare themselves a NATIONAL ASSEMBLY, and swear
                 not to disperse till they have drafted a CONSTITUTION
                 limiting the powers of the monarch.
                 Led by MIRABEAU and ABBE SIEYES.
                        v
   14 JULY 1789  THE STORMING OF THE BASTILLE
                 (bad harvest + bakers hoarding + women storming
                  bakeries + troops ordered into Paris)
                        v
   JULY-AUG      THE GREAT FEAR in the countryside
                 Rumour: lords have hired brigands to destroy the
                 ripe crops. Peasants seize hoes and pitchforks,
                 attack CHATEAUX, loot hoarded grain, and BURN THE
                 DOCUMENTS RECORDING MANORIAL DUES.
                 Nobles flee abroad.
                        v
   4 AUGUST 1789 (night) THE ASSEMBLY'S DECREE
                 - feudal system of obligations and taxes ABOLISHED
                 - clergy forced to give up privileges
                 - TITHES abolished
                 - CHURCH LANDS CONFISCATED
                   -> government gains assets worth 2 BILLION LIVRES
```

⭐⭐⭐ **Two people worth knowing by name:**
- **Mirabeau** — *born into a noble family* but convinced feudal privilege must go; brought out a journal and gave powerful speeches to the Versailles crowds.
- **Abbé Sieyès** — *originally a priest*; wrote the pamphlet **"What is the Third Estate?"**

> 🎯 **The exam-friendly irony:** the two men who led the third estate's revolt came **from the first and second estates**. Privilege was not overthrown only from below.

### 💸 Why the king had no choice

| Cause | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Inherited an empty treasury** | Louis XVI acceded in **1774**, aged **20**, married to the Austrian princess **Marie Antoinette** |
| **Long years of war** | had already drained French finances |
| **The court at Versailles** | maintaining an *"extravagant court at the immense palace"* |
| **The American War** | France helped the **thirteen American colonies** against **Britain** — added **over 1 billion livres** to a debt already above **2 billion livres** |
| **The debt trap** | lenders now charged **10% interest**; an *"increasing percentage of its budget on interest payments alone"* |
| **The only fix available** | raise taxes — *"Yet even this measure would not have sufficed"* |

⭐⭐ **The deep structural point for essays:** France's monarchy was destroyed partly by the **debt it took on to help create the American republic**. Empires that fund other people's freedom often cannot afford their own arrangements afterwards.

## 3️⃣ The Constitution of 1791 — and Its Fine Print

### ⚙️ How the new machine worked

```
                        THE CONSTITUTION OF 1791
                       (France = CONSTITUTIONAL MONARCHY)

   JUDICIARY        EXECUTIVE  ──CONTROL──>   LEGISLATURE
    Judges        King+Ministers <──VETO──   NATIONAL ASSEMBLY
       ^                  ^                     (745 members)
       |                  |                          ^
       └──── elected ─────┘                          │ chosen by
                                                                │
                                         ELECTORS (50,000 men)
                                                     ^
                                                     │ voted by
                                                                │
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┴──────────┐
   │  ACTIVE CITIZENS  — entitled to vote                       │
   │  about 4 MILLION of a population of 28 MILLION             │
   │  qualification: MEN over 25 paying tax equal to at least   │
   │                 3 DAYS of a labourer's wage                │
   ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  PASSIVE CITIZENS — NO voting rights                       │
   │  about 3 MILLION men                                       │
   ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  WOMEN, CHILDREN, and all YOUTH BELOW 25 — no rights       │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

   And to BE an elector or an Assembly member, a man had to belong
   to the HIGHEST BRACKET OF TAXPAYERS.
```

⭐⭐⭐ **This diagram is the single most examinable thing in the chapter.** Everything that happens next — the Jacobins, the Terror, the women's clubs — is a fight over **who is inside that top box**.

> ⚠️ **Note the sleight of hand.** The Assembly was **indirectly elected**: citizens voted for **electors**, who chose the Assembly. Only 4 million of 28 million could vote at all, and only the richest could *be* elected. A revolution against privilege of **birth** immediately installed privilege of **wealth**.

### 📜 The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen

The Constitution **began** with it. Rights such as **the right to life, freedom of speech, freedom of opinion, equality before law** were declared **"natural and inalienable"** — *they belonged to each human being by birth and could not be taken away*, and **it was the duty of the state to protect them.**

**The articles worth being able to quote:**

| Art. | Text (compressed) |
|---|---|
| **1** | *"Men are born and remain **free and equal in rights**."* |
| **2** | The aim of every political association is the preservation of natural and inalienable rights — **liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression** |
| **3** | ⭐⭐⭐ *"The source of all **sovereignty resides in the nation**; no group or individual may exercise authority that does not come from the people."* |
| **4** | *"**Liberty** consists of the power to do whatever is **not injurious to others**."* |
| **6** | *"**Law is the expression of the general will.**"* All citizens may participate in its formation; **all are equal before it** |
| **7** | No one may be accused, arrested or detained **except in cases determined by law** |
| **11** | Every citizen may **speak, write and print freely** — taking responsibility for abuse |
| **12** | A common tax is indispensable; it must be **assessed equally on all citizens in proportion to their means** |
| **17** | ⭐ *"**Property is a sacred and inviolable right**"* — no one may be deprived of it unless legally established public necessity requires it, **with just compensation given in advance** |

> 🔗 **Polity link worth making now:** Article 4's definition of liberty (*"whatever is not injurious to others"*) is the ancestor of **reasonable restrictions** under **Art. 19(2)** of the Indian Constitution. Article 3 is the ancestor of *"We, the People."* Article 17 is the ancestor of the **right to property** fight in India — and note that **India eventually demoted property from a fundamental right, while France declared it "sacred and inviolable."**

### 🎨 Box 1 — Reading political symbols

Most people could not read, so **images did the work of arguments**. Learn these; they turn up in Class 10 as well.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Broken chain** | Chains fettered slaves — a broken chain = **the act of becoming free** |
| **Bundle of rods / *fasces*** | One rod breaks easily, a bundle does not — **strength lies in unity** (**fraternity**) |
| **The eye within a triangle radiating light** | The all-seeing eye = **knowledge**; the sun's rays drive away **the clouds of ignorance** |
| **Sceptre** | **Royal power** |
| **Snake biting its tail (a ring)** | **Eternity** — a ring has neither beginning nor end |
| **Red Phrygian cap** | The cap worn by **a slave upon becoming free** — **liberty** |
| **Blue–white–red** | The **national colours** of France |
| **The winged woman** | Personification of **the law** |
| **The Law Tablet** | The law is the **same for all**; all are **equal** before it |

> ⚠️ **Gender detail that is easy to miss and often asked:** *sans-culottes* men wore the red liberty cap. **"Women however were not allowed to do so."** The symbol of liberty itself was gendered.

### 🗞️ Marat's warning (Source B)

The journalist **Jean-Paul Marat**, in *L'Ami du peuple* (*The Friend of the People*), on the 1791 Constitution:

> *"The task of representing the people has been given to the rich… the lot of the poor and oppressed will never be improved by peaceful means alone… when they have managed to cast off the yoke of the aristocrats, **they will do the same to the other owners of wealth.**"*

⭐⭐⭐ **Marat is predicting Chapter 2 of this book.** The demand that political equality be followed by **economic** equality is exactly where socialism begins. Mark this passage — it is the bridge between the two chapters.

## 4️⃣ From Monarchy to Republic — 1792

### ⚔️ The war that radicalised everything

```
   Louis XVI signs the Constitution ... and secretly negotiates
   with the KING OF PRUSSIA.
                 |
   Neighbouring rulers plan to send troops to crush the revolution.
                 |
   APRIL 1792 — the National Assembly strikes FIRST:
                declares war on PRUSSIA and AUSTRIA.
                 |
   Volunteers throng from the provinces. They see it as
   "a war of the PEOPLE against KINGS and ARISTOCRACIES
    all over Europe."
                 |
   They sing the MARSEILLAISE, composed by ROGET DE L'ISLE,
   first sung by volunteers from MARSEILLES marching into Paris.
   -> now the NATIONAL ANTHEM OF FRANCE.
```

**What the war did at home:** losses and economic hardship; **men away at the front, women left to earn a living and run families**; and a growing conviction that *"the revolution had to be carried further, as the Constitution of 1791 gave political rights only to the richer sections of society."*

### 🧢 Jacobins and *sans-culottes*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Name from** | the former **convent of St Jacob** in Paris |
| **Who** | *"the less prosperous sections"* — small shopkeepers; artisans such as **shoemakers, pastry cooks, watch-makers, printers**; **servants and daily-wage workers** |
| **Leader** | **Maximilian Robespierre** |
| **The trousers** | They wore **long striped trousers** like dock workers, to set themselves apart from nobles who wore **knee breeches** |
| ⭐ **The name** | ***sans-culottes* = "those without knee breeches"** |
| **Plus** | the men wore the **red cap** symbolising liberty (**women were not allowed to**) |

> 🎯 **Prelims-style trap:** *sans-culottes* does **not** mean "without trousers." It means **without knee breeches** — a deliberate class statement made through clothing. NCERT devotes a whole later chapter (*Clothing: A Social History*, now removed) to exactly this idea.

### 🏛️ The Republic

```
   SUMMER 1792     Jacobins plan an insurrection of Parisians
                   angered by short supplies and high food prices.
                        |
   10 AUGUST 1792  They storm the PALACE OF THE TUILERIES,
                   massacre the king's guards, hold the king
                   hostage for several hours.
                   The Assembly votes to imprison the royal family.
                        |
                   ELECTIONS HELD — and now ALL MEN OF 21 AND ABOVE,
                   REGARDLESS OF WEALTH, GET THE VOTE.
                   (the property qualification is gone)
                        |
   21 SEPT 1792    The new assembly — the CONVENTION —
                   ABOLISHES THE MONARCHY and declares
                   FRANCE A REPUBLIC.
                        |
   21 JAN 1793     Louis XVI, sentenced to death for TREASON,
                   is executed publicly at the PLACE DE LA CONCORDE.
                   Marie Antoinette meets the same fate shortly after.
```

> **Definitions:** **Republic** = a form of government where **the people elect the government including the head of government; there is no hereditary monarchy.** **Treason** = betrayal of one's country or government.

## 5️⃣ The Reign of Terror, 1793–94

### 🔪 What Robespierre did

| Domain | Measure |
|---|---|
| **Politics** | *"A policy of severe control and punishment."* Anyone he saw as an **enemy of the republic** — **ex-nobles and clergy, members of other political parties, even members of his own party who disagreed with his methods** — was **arrested, imprisoned, tried by a revolutionary tribunal**, and if 'guilty', **guillotined** |
| **The instrument** | The **guillotine** — two poles and a blade — **named after Dr Guillotin who invented it** |
| **Wages and prices** | Laws placing a **maximum ceiling on wages and prices** |
| **Food** | **Meat and bread rationed**; peasants **forced to transport grain to the cities** and sell at **government-fixed prices** |
| **Bread itself** | **White flour forbidden**; all citizens required to eat ***pain d'égalité*** (**equality bread**), a loaf of **wholewheat** |
| **Speech** | *Monsieur* and *Madame* abolished — everyone became ***Citoyen*** and ***Citoyenne*** (Citizen) |
| **Religion** | **Churches shut down**, buildings converted into **barracks or offices** |
| **The end** | He pursued his policies *"so relentlessly that even his supporters began to demand moderation."* **Convicted July 1794, arrested, guillotined the next day.** |

### ⚖️ The debate you should be able to argue both sides of

This is **Ethics and Essay material of the highest quality**. Learn both quotations.

> **ROBESPIERRE**, to the Convention, 7 February 1794:
> *"To establish and consolidate democracy… we must first finish the war of liberty against tyranny… We must **annihilate the enemies of the republic** at home and abroad, or else we shall perish. **In time of Revolution a democratic government may rely on terror. Terror is nothing but justice, swift, severe and inflexible.**"*

> **CAMILLE DESMOULINS**, 1793 — *executed shortly after, during the Terror*:
> *"Some people believe that Liberty is like a child, which needs to go through a phase of being disciplined before it attains maturity. **Quite the opposite. Liberty is Happiness, Reason, Equality, Justice, it is the Declaration of Rights**… Would it be possible to bring a single person to the scaffold **without making ten more enemies among his relations and friends?**"*

```
   ROBESPIERRE                     DESMOULINS
   Liberty is a DESTINATION.       Liberty is the ROAD ITSELF.
   Get there by any means,         The means ARE the end; violence
   including terror.               only manufactures more enemies.
   Terror = justice.               Terror = the opposite of the
                                   Declaration of Rights.
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The killer detail for an essay:** Desmoulins was **guillotined for saying this.** The argument was not settled by reason; it was settled by the blade — which is itself Desmoulins' argument proved.

### 🏢 3.2 The Directory

```
   FALL OF THE JACOBINS (July 1794)
            |
   The WEALTHIER MIDDLE CLASSES seize power.
            |
   NEW CONSTITUTION: denies the vote to NON-PROPERTIED sections.
            |            (the 1792 gain is rolled back)
            |
   TWO elected legislative councils
            |
   They appoint a DIRECTORY — an executive of FIVE members
   -> deliberately designed as "a safeguard against the
      concentration of power in a ONE-MAN EXECUTIVE
      as under the Jacobins"
            |
   BUT the Directors kept clashing with the councils,
   who then sought to dismiss them.
            |
            v
   POLITICAL INSTABILITY  ->  paved the way for a MILITARY
                              DICTATOR: NAPOLEON BONAPARTE
```

⭐⭐⭐ **This is the answer to "How would you explain the rise of Napoleon?"** — the Directory was built to prevent a strongman and **produced one**, because a system designed only to stop concentration of power could not actually govern. Institutional deadlock is an invitation to a general.

> **And NCERT's balancing sentence, which you should quote at the end of any such answer:** *"Through all these changes in the form of government, the ideals of **freedom, of equality before the law and of fraternity** remained inspiring ideals that motivated political movements in France and the rest of Europe during the following century."*

## 6️⃣ Did Women Have a Revolution?

**NCERT asks this as a question, not a statement.** Answer it as one — with a ledger.

### ➖ What women started from

- Most third-estate women **had to work for a living**: seamstresses, laundresses, sellers of flowers/fruit/vegetables, **domestic servants**.
- Most had **no access to education or job training**. Only **daughters of nobles or wealthier third-estate families** could study at a convent — after which **their families arranged a marriage**.
- They also had to **cook, fetch water, queue up for bread, look after children**.
- ⭐ **Their wages were lower than those of men.**

### ✊ What they did

| Action | Detail |
|---|---|
| **5 October 1789** | Parisian women **marched to Versailles** and **brought the king back with them to Paris** |
| **Clubs** | About **sixty women's clubs** across French cities |
| **The famous one** | ⭐ **The Society of Revolutionary and Republican Women** |
| **Newspapers** | They started their own |
| **The demand** | **The same political rights as men** — the right **to vote**, **to be elected to the Assembly**, and **to hold political office** |
| **The grievance** | The **Constitution of 1791 reduced them to passive citizens** |

### ➕ What they actually won (the early years)

- **State schools created; schooling made compulsory for all girls.**
- **Fathers could no longer force them into marriage against their will.**
- **Marriage made a contract** entered into **freely** and **registered under civil law**.
- ⭐ **Divorce made legal — and could be applied for by both women and men.**
- **Women could train for jobs, become artists, or run small businesses.**

### ➖ What was then taken back

> ⭐⭐⭐ *"During the **Reign of Terror**, the new government issued laws ordering **closure of women's clubs** and **banning their political activities**. Many prominent women were **arrested** and a number of them **executed**."*

### 👤 Olympe de Gouges (1748–1793)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who** | *"One of the most important of the politically active women in revolutionary France"* |
| **Her protest** | Against the Constitution **and** the Declaration of Rights of Man and Citizen, because **they excluded women from basic rights that each human being was entitled to** |
| **Her document, 1791** | ⭐⭐⭐ **The *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and Citizen***, addressed to **the Queen and the members of the National Assembly**, demanding that they act on it |
| **1793** | She **criticised the Jacobin government for forcibly closing women's clubs** |
| **The end** | Tried by the **National Convention**, charged with **treason**, and **executed** |

**Her rewriting of the Declaration — the method is the message:**

> 1. *"**Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights.**"*
> 3. *"The source of all sovereignty resides in the nation, which is nothing but **the union of woman and man**."*
> 4. *"All **female and male** citizens should have a say… **All female and male citizens are equally entitled to all honours and public employment according to their abilities** and without any other distinction than that of their talents."*
> 5. *"**No woman is an exception**… Women, like men, obey this rigorous law."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why this is such a powerful exam example:** she did not write a new manifesto. She took the revolution's *own* sacred text and **inserted women into it**, sentence by sentence. That is the sharpest possible way to expose an exclusion — you show that the principle was already universal and the practice was not.

### 🗯️ The opposing argument — Chaumette (Source G), 1793

The Jacobin politician justifying the closure of women's clubs:

> *"Has Nature entrusted domestic duties to men? Has she given us breasts to nurture babies? **No.** She said to Man: Be a man. **Hunting, agriculture, political duties… that is your kingdom.** She said to Woman: Be a woman… **the things of the household, the sweet duties of motherhood** — those are your tasks. **Shameless are those women, who wish to become men.**"*

⭐⭐ **Note the structure of the argument: it appeals to *nature* to settle a question of *rights*.** That move — dressing a social arrangement as a biological fact — is the standard form of every argument against extending rights, and you will meet it again in **Class 8 Ch.7** on widow remarriage and women's education in India.

### ⏳ The long tail

- Women's movements for **voting rights and equal wages** continued **for the next two hundred years** in many countries.
- The fight for the vote ran through **an international suffrage movement** in the **late 19th and early 20th centuries**.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **Women in France finally won the right to vote in 1946** — **157 years after 1789.**

> 🎯 **How to answer "Did women have a revolution?"** — *Partly, and temporarily.* They **made** the revolution (October 1789, the clubs, the newspapers), they **won real civil gains** (divorce, schooling, marriage as contract, training for jobs), they **were denied political rights throughout** (passive citizens in 1791; still excluded when the vote was widened to all men in 1792), and they were **actively suppressed** in 1793. The revolution gave women a **language** for their claim long before it gave them the claim.

## 7️⃣ The Abolition of Slavery

### 🚢 The triangular trade

```
   EUROPE                          The trade began in the 17th century.
     |
     |  French merchants sail from
     |  BORDEAUX or NANTES
     v
   AFRICAN COAST
     |  slaves bought from LOCAL CHIEFTAINS
     |  branded and shackled, packed tightly into ships
     |  THREE-MONTH voyage across the Atlantic
     v
   THE CARIBBEAN — Martinique, Guadeloupe, San Domingo
     |  sold to plantation owners
     |  produce: TOBACCO, INDIGO, SUGAR, COFFEE
     v
   back to EUROPE
        -> "Port cities like Bordeaux and Nantes owed their
            economic prosperity to the flourishing slave trade."
```

**Why slavery existed at all, in NCERT's own causal terms:** Europeans were **reluctant to go and work in distant and unfamiliar lands** ⇒ **shortage of labour on the plantations** ⇒ met by the **triangular slave trade**. The exploitation of slave labour *"made it possible to meet the growing demand in European markets for sugar, coffee and indigo."*

### ⛓️ The sequence — and the u-turn

```
   18th c.   "Throughout the eighteenth century there was
              LITTLE CRITICISM OF SLAVERY IN FRANCE."
                     |
   1789-93   The National Assembly holds LONG DEBATES on whether
             the rights of man extend to all French subjects
             INCLUDING THOSE IN THE COLONIES.
             It passes NO LAW — "fearing opposition from
             businessmen whose incomes depended on the slave trade."
                     |
   1794 ⭐   THE CONVENTION legislates to FREE ALL SLAVES in the
             French overseas possessions.
             (NCERT: "one of the most revolutionary social reforms
              of the JACOBIN regime")
                     |
   1804 ⚠️   NAPOLEON REINTRODUCES SLAVERY.
             "Plantation owners understood their freedom as including
              the RIGHT TO ENSLAVE African Negroes in pursuit of
              their economic interests."
                     |
   1848 ⭐   Slavery FINALLY abolished in French colonies.
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The sentence to carry into an essay:** *plantation owners understood **their** freedom as including the right to enslave others.* That is the contradiction of universal rights in one line — **freedom claimed as a property right over other people**. If you write one thing about this chapter, write this.

> **Word to know:** **Emancipation** — the act of freeing.

## 8️⃣ The Revolution in Everyday Life

**NCERT's framing question:** *"Can politics change the clothes people wear, the language they speak or the books they read?"*

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Abolition of censorship**, summer 1789 | Under the Old Regime **all** written material and cultural activity — **books, newspapers, plays** — could be published or performed **only after approval by the censors of the king**. The Declaration made **freedom of speech and expression a natural right** |
| **The flood of print** | Newspapers, pamphlets, books and printed pictures **flooded the towns and travelled rapidly into the countryside**, describing and discussing events |
| ⭐⭐ **Freedom of the press cut both ways** | It *"also meant that **opposing views** of events could be expressed. **Each side sought to convince the others of its position through the medium of print.**"* |
| **For the non-reading majority** | **Plays, songs and festive processions** — *"one way they could grasp and identify with ideas such as liberty or justice that political philosophers wrote about at length in texts which only a handful of educated people could read"* |
| **Festivals** | Staged deliberately to **mobilise loyalty**, using symbols from **ancient Greece and Rome** to convey *"the aura of a hallowed history"* |
| **Forms of address** | *Monsieur/Madame* → ***Citoyen/Citoyenne*** |
| **Clothing** | knee breeches → the *sans-culotte*'s long striped trousers and red cap |
| **Bread** | white flour → ***pain d'égalité*** |

⭐⭐ **The transferable insight:** a revolution that only changes the government changes very little. This one went after **the calendar of daily life** — what you eat, what you wear, what you call your neighbour. **Culture is where a political change is either made permanent or lost.**

## 9️⃣ Napoleon and the Legacy

### 👑 Napoleon

| | |
|---|---|
| **1804** | **Crowns himself Emperor of France** |
| **What he did** | Set out to **conquer neighbouring European countries**, **dispossessing dynasties** and **creating kingdoms where he placed members of his family** |
| **His self-image** | *"Napoleon saw his role as **a moderniser of Europe**."* |
| **His laws** | **Protection of private property**; a **uniform system of weights and measures** provided by the **decimal system** |
| **The turn** | *"Initially, many saw Napoleon as a **liberator**… But soon the Napoleonic armies came to be viewed everywhere as an **invading force**."* |
| **1815** | **Defeated at Waterloo** |
| ⭐ **The verdict** | *"Many of his measures that carried the **revolutionary ideas of liberty and modern laws** to other parts of Europe had an **impact on people long after Napoleon had left**."* |

### 🌍 The legacy — this is the highest-yield paragraph in the chapter

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**The ideas of liberty and democratic rights were the most important legacy of the French Revolution.** These spread from France to the rest of Europe during the nineteenth century, where **feudal systems were abolished**. **Colonised peoples reworked the idea of freedom from bondage into their movements to create a sovereign nation state.**"*

**And the two Indians NCERT names:**

| Person | Response |
|---|---|
| **Tipu Sultan** | Named as one who **responded to the ideas coming from revolutionary France** *(recall from **Class 8 Ch.2** — he had a close relationship with the French in India)* |
| **Raja Rammohan Roy** | *"He could think and talk of nothing else when he heard of the **July Revolution in France in 1830**. On his way to England at Cape Town **he insisted on visiting frigates flying the revolutionary tri-colour flag though he had been temporarily lamed by an accident.**"* — Susobhan Sarkar, *Notes on the Bengal Renaissance* |

⭐⭐⭐ **This is your bridge sentence between World History and Indian History**, and it is worth memorising the Rammohan Roy image because it is vivid enough to be quoted: a lamed man limping to see a flag.

### 🔴 Prelims traps

|---|---|
| *"Sans-culottes = without trousers"* | **Without KNEE BREECHES** — long striped trousers instead |
| *"The Bastille was stormed to free hundreds of political prisoners"* | It held **seven** prisoners. It was stormed as a **symbol of despotic royal power** |
| *"The Estates-General had not met since 1714"* | **1614** — a gap of **175 years** |

| *"The 1791 Constitution gave universal male suffrage"* | Only **active citizens** — men **over 25** paying tax equal to **3 days' wages** — about **4 million of 28 million**. Universal *male* suffrage (21+) came only in **1792** |
| *"France became a republic in 1789"* | **21 September 1792**. 1789 = Bastille; 1791 = constitutional **monarchy** |
| *"Louis XVI was executed in 1792"* | **21 January 1793**, at the **Place de la Concorde** |
| *"Slavery was abolished once, in 1794"* | **1794 abolished → Napoleon reintroduced it (1804) → finally abolished 1848** |
| *"Olympe de Gouges wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man"* | She wrote the ***Declaration of the Rights of **Woman** and Citizen***, **1791** |
| *"French women got the vote after the Revolution"* | **1946** |
| *"Rousseau proposed separation of powers"* | **Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*). Rousseau = **social contract**; Locke = **against divine right** |
| *"The Marseillaise was written in Marseilles"* | Composed by **Roget de L'Isle**; named because **volunteers from Marseilles** first sang it marching into Paris |
| *"The guillotine was named after its first victim"* | After **Dr Guillotin**, **who invented it** |

### 🟢 Mains angles

- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"The message of universal rights was beset with contradictions." Examine.** — The single best question in the chapter. Your four contradictions: **(i)** rights declared universal, but the 1791 Constitution created **active vs passive citizens** on a property test; **(ii)** *"Men are born and remain free and equal"* — while **women were passive citizens**, their clubs closed and leaders executed; **(iii)** the National Assembly **debated but would not legislate** on colonial slavery *"fearing opposition from businessmen"*, and when abolition came in 1794 it was **reversed within ten years**; **(iv)** **Article 17 made property "sacred and inviolable"**, which is precisely what plantation owners invoked to keep human beings as property.
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Terror is nothing but justice, swift, severe and inflexible." Critically examine revolutionary violence.** — Robespierre vs Desmoulins; note Desmoulins was executed for his answer; end with the Directory and Napoleon as the **cost** of the Terror.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **The legacy of the French Revolution for the colonised world.** — Feudalism abolished across Europe; **colonised peoples reworked freedom-from-bondage into sovereign nation-statehood**; Tipu Sultan and Rammohan Roy; and the deep irony that **a France declaring the rights of man was simultaneously running plantations on slave labour** — which is what made the anti-colonial use of these ideas so sharp.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **"Which groups benefited, which lost, which were disappointed?"** — *Benefited:* the **richer third estate** (property, offices, careers open to talent). *Lost:* **clergy and nobility** (tithes abolished, Church lands confiscated, feudal dues gone, many fled). *Disappointed:* **women**, the **non-propertied poor** (enfranchised 1792, disenfranchised again under the Directory), and **slaves in the colonies** after 1804.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **How did a fiscal crisis become a political revolution?** — Debt → the need to tax → the need for consent → the Estates-General → the voting dispute → the claim to represent the nation. **A state that must ask for money must eventually answer to those it asks.**
- ⭐⭐ **The rise of Napoleon as an institutional failure, not a personal triumph.**
- ⭐⭐ **Print, symbols and festivals as instruments of political mobilisation** — pairs beautifully with the Indian national movement's use of newspapers, songs and processions.

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Louis XVI, 1774**, aged 20, married to **Marie Antoinette**, inherits an **empty treasury**; the **American war** adds over **1 billion livres** to a **2 billion+** debt at **10% interest**.
2. **Three estates.** Clergy and nobility are **exempt from tax by birth**; the **third estate alone** pays the **tithe** (Church), **feudal dues** (nobles), the **taille** and indirect taxes (state). **90% are peasants**; **60% of land** is owned by nobles, Church and rich commoners.
3. **Population 23 → 28 million** ⇒ grain demand outruns supply ⇒ bread prices rise, wages don't ⇒ **subsistence crisis**: bad harvest → scarcity → price rise → riots → weaker bodies → epidemics → deaths.
4. **The middle class** — merchants, manufacturers, lawyers, officials — has what earlier rebels lacked: **a programme**. Its creed: **no privilege by birth; position by merit.**
5. **Locke** (against divine right) · **Rousseau** (social contract; one man one vote) · **Montesquieu** (separation of powers) · the **American Constitution** as the working example. Spread through **salons, coffee-houses, and reading aloud**.
6. ⭐ **5 May 1789** Estates-General (first since **1614**); 300+300 seated, **600 stand at the back**; the dispute is **one vote per estate vs per member**. **20 June — Tennis Court Oath**; they become the **National Assembly** under **Mirabeau** and **Abbé Sieyès**.
7. **14 July — the Bastille**, holding **seven** prisoners, is stormed as a **symbol**. The **Great Fear** sweeps the countryside; peasants **burn the records of manorial dues**.
8. ⭐ **Night of 4 August 1789** — feudal obligations abolished, **tithes abolished**, **Church lands confiscated** (2 billion livres to the state).
9. ⭐⭐ **Constitution of 1791** — constitutional monarchy, separation of powers, **indirect election**; only **active citizens** (men 25+, tax = 3 days' wages, **4 of 28 million**) vote; it opens with the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen** — *"natural and inalienable"*.
10. **April 1792** war on Prussia and Austria; the **Marseillaise**. **Jacobins** under **Robespierre**; the ***sans-culottes***. **10 August** — the Tuileries stormed; **all men 21+ get the vote**.
11. ⭐ **21 September 1792 — monarchy abolished, France a republic.** **21 January 1793 — Louis XVI executed** for treason.
12. **1793–94 Reign of Terror** — guillotine, price and wage ceilings, rationing, **pain d'égalité**, **Citoyen/Citoyenne**, churches shut. *"Terror is nothing but justice."* **Robespierre guillotined July 1794.**
13. **The Directory** — five-member executive, vote taken back from the non-propertied; its **instability produces Napoleon**.
14. **Women:** ~**60 clubs**, the **Society of Revolutionary and Republican Women**, the **October 1789 march to Versailles**; won **compulsory schooling for girls, marriage as free contract, legal divorce, training for jobs**; but remained **passive citizens**, clubs **closed in 1793**, **Olympe de Gouges** (*Declaration of the Rights of Woman*, **1791**) **executed**. **French women voted only in 1946.**
15. **Slavery:** triangular trade from **Bordeaux/Nantes**; **little criticism through the 18th century**; the Assembly **debated but did not act**; **the Convention abolished it in 1794**; **Napoleon reintroduced it**; **finally abolished 1848**.
16. **Everyday life:** **censorship abolished 1789**, print flooded the country, **opposing views could now be printed**, and plays, songs and festivals carried the ideas to the illiterate.
17. **Napoleon: emperor 1804, moderniser and invader, defeated at Waterloo 1815.** Legacy = **liberty and democratic rights**; feudalism swept from Europe; **colonised peoples reworked freedom from bondage into the demand for a sovereign nation state** — **Tipu Sultan** and **Rammohan Roy**.
