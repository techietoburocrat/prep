# Chapter 5 — Pastoralists in the Modern World

> **The one-line point:** Nomadic pastoralism is not a leftover from the past — it is a **precisely engineered response to environments where settled farming fails**, and its central mechanism is **movement**, which lets a pasture recover before it is grazed again. The colonial state's entire programme — settlement, reservation, taxation, criminalisation — attacked **exactly that mechanism**. *"Pastoralists are not relics of the past."*

## 1️⃣ Pastoral Nomads and Their Movements

### 🏔️ 1.1 In the mountains — the cycle is driven by **SNOW**

```
   THE HIMALAYAN ANNUAL CYCLE (all four communities share it)

   WINTER   HIGH MOUNTAINS UNDER SNOW
            -> herds in the LOW HILLS of the SIWALIK range
            -> "the DRY SCRUB FORESTS here provided pasture"
                            |
   END APRIL  the northern march begins
                            |
   SUMMER   "the snow melted and the mountainsides were LUSH GREEN.
             The VARIETY OF GRASSES that sprouted provided RICH
             NUTRITIOUS FORAGE"
                            |
   END SEPT   the downward journey begins, back to the winter base
```

| Community | Where | Animals | The route |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Gujjar Bakarwals** | **Jammu and Kashmir** | **goat and sheep** | Winter in the **Siwaliks**; by end April they **cross the PIR PANJAL passes** and enter the **valley of Kashmir**; ⭐ several households travel together in a ***KAFILA***. Many **migrated to the region in the 19th century in search of pastures** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Gaddi shepherds** | **Himachal Pradesh** | **goats and sheep** | Winter in the **Siwaliks**; by April north to ⭐ **LAHUL and SPITI**; then **higher mountain meadows** when the passes clear; return by September ⭐ *stopping in the villages of Lahul and Spiti to **reap their summer harvest and sow their winter crop*** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Gujjar cattle herders** | **Garhwal and Kumaon** | **cattle (buffaloes)** | Winter in the dry forests of the ⭐ ***BHABAR***; summer in the high meadows — the ⭐ ***BUGYALS***. *"Many of them were **originally from Jammu** and came to the UP hills in the nineteenth century in search of good pastures"* |
| ⭐⭐ **Bhotiyas, Sherpas, Kinnauris** | the Himalayas | — | *"This pattern of cyclical movement between summer and winter pastures was **typical of many pastoral communities of the Himalayas**"* |

> **Words:** ⭐ ***Bhabar* — a dry forested area below the foothills of Garhwal and Kumaun.** ⭐ ***Bugyal* — vast meadows in the high mountains** *(NCERT: "vast natural pastures… **above 12,000 feet**. They are **under snow in the winter** and **come to life after April**… by monsoon these pastures are **thick with vegetation and carpeted with wild flowers**")*. ⭐ ***Kafila* — the group formed when several households travel together.**

**⭐⭐⭐⭐ And the key ecological sentence — underline it, it recurs three times in this chapter:**

> *"All of them had to **adjust to seasonal changes** and **make effective use of available pastures in different places**. When the pasture was **exhausted or unusable** in one place they moved their herds and flock to new areas. **THIS CONTINUOUS MOVEMENT ALSO ALLOWED THE PASTURES TO RECOVER; IT PREVENTED THEIR OVERUSE.**"*

> 🎯 **This is the intellectual core of the chapter, and it answers NCERT's Question 1 completely.** Movement is not restlessness or backwardness; **it is a rotation system**, the exact pastoral equivalent of the **12–18 year fallow in shifting cultivation** you met in Chapter 4. Both are **regeneration cycles**, and the colonial state **misread both as waste** because in each case land was sitting idle at the moment an official looked at it.

**Source A — G.C. Barnes on the Gujjars of Kangra, 1850–55** *(useful for the gendered division of labour, which NCERT asks about)*:

> *"In the hills the Gujjars are **exclusively a pastoral tribe — they cultivate scarcely at all**. **The Gaddis keep flocks of sheep and goats and the Gujjars' wealth consists of buffaloes.** These people **live in the skirts of the forests**, and maintain their existence **exclusively by the sale of the milk, ghee, and other produce of their herds**. ⭐ **THE MEN graze the cattle, and frequently lie out for weeks in the woods tending their herds. THE WOMEN repair to the markets every morning with baskets on their heads, with little earthen pots filled with milk, butter-milk and ghee**… During the hot weather the Gujjars usually **drive their herds to the upper range**, where the buffaloes **rejoice in the rich grass which the rains bring forth** and at the same time gain **from the temperate climate and the immunity from venomous flies that torment their existence in the plains**."*

> ⭐⭐ **Note the last clause — it gives a second reason for the summer migration besides grass: escape from the biting flies of the plains.** And note the division of labour: **men herd and camp out for weeks; women carry the produce to market daily.** The household is a two-location enterprise.

### 🏜️ 1.2 On the plateaus, plains and deserts — the cycle is driven by the **MONSOON**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Unlike the mountain pastoralists, it was NOT THE COLD AND THE SNOW that defined the seasonal rhythms of their movement: rather it was THE ALTERNATION OF THE MONSOON AND DRY SEASON.**"*

**This one contrast is the most likely conceptual question from the chapter. Learn it as a pair:**

```
   MOUNTAIN PASTORALISTS          |  PLATEAU / DESERT PASTORALISTS
   -------------------------------|-------------------------------
   Driver: SNOW                   |  Driver: THE MONSOON
   Winter in the LOW hills        |  Monsoon on the DRY PLATEAU
   Summer in the HIGH meadows     |  Dry season in the COASTAL tracts
   They go UP when it warms       |  They LEAVE the coast when the
                                  |  rains come
   Because: high pasture is under |  Because: SHEEP CANNOT TOLERATE
   snow in winter                 |  WET MONSOON CONDITIONS -- only
                                  |  BUFFALOES like the swampy,
                                  |  wet coastal conditions
```

| Community | Where | Animals / work | The cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Dhangars** | **Maharashtra** — ⭐ population in the early 20th century estimated at **467,000** | *"Most were **shepherds**, some were **blanket weavers**, and still others were **buffalo herders**"* | **Monsoon** on the **central plateau** — *"semi-arid, low rainfall, poor soil, covered with thorny scrub. **Nothing but dry crops like BAJRA could be sown here**"*, but in the monsoon it became **a vast grazing ground**. ⭐ **By October they harvested their bajra and moved WEST**; after **about a month's march** they reached the ⭐ **KONKAN** |
| ⭐⭐ **Gollas** | **Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh** — dry central plateau *"covered with stone and grass"* | herded **cattle** | Dry season → **coastal tracts**; left when the rains came |
| ⭐⭐ **Kurumas and Kurubas** | same | reared **sheep and goats** and **sold woven blankets** | *"They **lived near the woods, cultivated small patches of land, engaged in a variety of petty trades** and took care of their herds"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Banjaras** | villages of **Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra** | **graziers** | *"In search of good pastureland… they **moved over long distances, selling PLOUGH CATTLE and other goods to villagers in exchange for GRAIN AND FODDER**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Raikas** | **deserts of Rajasthan** — **Barmer, Jaisalmer, Jodhpur, Bikaner** | ⭐ ***Maru* (desert) Raikas herded CAMELS**; another group reared **sheep and goat** | *"rainfall **meagre and uncertain**… harvests **fluctuated every year**. Over vast stretches **no crop could be grown**. **So the Raikas COMBINED CULTIVATION WITH PASTORALISM.**"* **Monsoon** in their **home villages** where pasture was available; **by October**, when grazing grounds were **dry and exhausted**, they **moved out in search of pasture and water**, returning the next monsoon. *(Their settlement is called a **dhandi**.)* |

### 🤝 The Dhangar–Konkan exchange — ⭐⭐⭐⭐ the best example in the chapter

```
   THE DHANGARS ARRIVE IN THE KONKAN (after their October march)
   "a FLOURISHING agricultural tract with HIGH RAINFALL and
    RICH SOIL"
                          |
   "Here the shepherds were WELCOMED BY KONKANI PEASANTS."
                          |
   WHY? The KHARIF harvest has just been cut, and the fields
   "had to be FERTILISED and MADE READY for the RABI harvest."
                          |
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  THE DHANGAR FLOCKS GIVE:  manure  (they "MANURED THE    │
   │                            FIELDS")                      │
   │  THE DHANGAR FLOCKS TAKE:  fodder  (they "FED ON THE     │
   │                            STUBBLE")                     │
   │  THE KONKANI PEASANTS GIVE: ⭐ "supplies of RICE which   │
   │                            the shepherds took back to    │
   │                            the plateau WHERE GRAIN WAS   │
   │                            SCARCE"                       │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                          |
   WITH THE ONSET OF THE MONSOON the Dhangars leave the Konkan
   -- "THE SHEEP COULD NOT TOLERATE THE WET MONSOON CONDITIONS."
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Notice how many problems this one arrangement solves at once.** The peasant gets **free fertiliser exactly when the field needs it**; the shepherd gets **free fodder from stubble that would otherwise rot**; the shepherd carries **rice back to a grain-scarce plateau**; and the flock **leaves before the monsoon that would kill the sheep**. **Nobody designed this. It emerged because two ecologies with opposite seasons were adjacent.** For GS3, this is your model answer on **integrated crop–livestock systems** — and it explains why breaking pastoral mobility damaged *settled agriculture too*, not just the pastoralists.

**Source B — Buchanan on the Gollas of Mysore, early 19th century**, on how a pastoral household actually splits its labour:

> *"Their families live in **small villages near the skirt of the woods**, where they **cultivate a little ground**, and keep some of their cattle, **selling in the towns the produce of the dairy**. Their families are very numerous, **seven to eight young men in each being common**. **Two or three of these attend the flocks in the woods, while the remainder cultivate their fields, and supply the towns with firewood, and with straw for thatch.**"*

> 🎯 **NCERT asks: "Why do you think pastoral groups often live on the edges of forests?"** — Because the edge is where **two** resources meet. From the forest: **grazing, firewood, fodder, thatch**. From the settled side: **a little cultivable ground and a town to sell dairy in**. **The edge is not marginal; it is optimal.** Answer it that way.

### 🧠 The skills the life demanded — quote this in any answer on pastoralism

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"the life of these pastoral groups was **sustained by a careful consideration of a host of factors**. They had to judge **how long the herds could stay in one area**, and know **where they could find water and pasture**. They needed to **calculate the timing of their movements**, and ensure that they **could move through different territories**. They had to **set up a relationship with farmers on the way**, so that the herds could **graze in harvested fields and manure the soil**. **They combined a range of different activities — cultivation, trade, and herding — to make their living.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐ **Read that as a list of competences: ecological judgement, hydrological knowledge, logistical timing, diplomacy across territories, and a diversified livelihood portfolio.** Everything the colonial state called "wandering" was in fact **skilled work under uncertainty**. And note **Fig. 9**: the Maru Raikas kept a **genealogist** who *"recounts the history of the community"* — *"Such oral traditions give pastoral groups their own sense of identity… they can tell us **how a group looks at its own past**."* **A community without writing is not a community without history.**

## 2️⃣ Colonial Rule and Pastoral Life

> **NCERT's summary of what happened, in one sentence:** *"Under colonial rule, the life of pastoralists changed dramatically. **Their grazing grounds shrank, their movements were regulated, and the revenue they had to pay increased. Their agricultural stock declined and their trades and crafts were adversely affected.**"*

### ⚖️ THE FOUR MEASURES — ⭐⭐⭐⭐ NCERT asks about these by name; know each one's *purpose* and its *effect*

#### 1️⃣ The **Waste Land Rules** (from the mid-19th century)

| | |
|---|---|
| **The state's motive** | *"the colonial state wanted to **transform all grazing lands into cultivated farms**. **Land revenue was one of the main sources of its finance.** By expanding cultivation it could **increase its revenue collection**"* — and at the same time produce more ⭐ **jute, cotton, wheat and other agricultural produce required in England** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The doctrine** | *"To colonial officials **all uncultivated land appeared to be UNPRODUCTIVE: it produced NEITHER REVENUE NOR AGRICULTURAL PRODUCE. It was seen as 'WASTE LAND' that needed to be brought under cultivation**"* |
| **The mechanism** | *"**Waste Land Rules** were enacted in various parts of the country. By these Rules **uncultivated lands were TAKEN OVER AND GIVEN TO SELECT INDIVIDUALS**. These individuals were granted **various concessions** and encouraged to settle these lands. **Some of them were made HEADMEN of villages in the newly cleared areas**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The catch** | *"**In most areas the lands taken over were ACTUALLY GRAZING TRACTS USED REGULARLY BY PASTORALISTS.** So expansion of cultivation **inevitably meant the decline of pastures and a problem for pastoralists**"* |

> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is *terra nullius* again, in Indian dress** (Chapter 4, Box 1 and Source A). **"Waste" is not a description — it is a legal category invented to make transfer look like improvement.** The land was in continuous, seasonal, productive use; it simply produced **milk, ghee, wool and manure** rather than **taxable grain**. **Whatever the state cannot count, it calls waste.** That sentence will serve you in an essay.

#### 2️⃣ The **Forest Acts** (from the mid-19th century)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Reserved forests** | Forests producing **commercially valuable timber like deodar or sal** were declared **'Reserved'** — ⭐ **"NO PASTORALIST WAS ALLOWED ACCESS to these forests"** |
| **Protected forests** | *"**some customary grazing rights** of pastoralists were granted **but their movements were severely restricted**"* |
| ⭐⭐ **The official justification** | *"The colonial officials believed that **grazing destroyed the saplings and young shoots of trees** that germinated on the forest floor. **The herds trampled over the saplings and munched away the shoots. This prevented new trees from growing.**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The permit regime** | *"Even in the areas they were allowed entry, **their movements were regulated. They needed a PERMIT for entry. The TIMING of their entry and departure was specified, and the NUMBER OF DAYS they could spend in the forest was LIMITED.** **Pastoralists could no longer remain in an area EVEN IF forage was available, the grass was succulent and the undergrowth in the forest was ample. They had to move because THE FOREST DEPARTMENT PERMITS THAT HAD BEEN ISSUED TO THEM NOW RULED THEIR LIVES.** The permit specified the periods in which they could be legally within a forest. **If they overstayed they were liable to fines.**"* |

> **Word:** ⭐ **Customary rights — rights that people are used to by custom and tradition.**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The deepest point in the chapter is in that permit paragraph, and it is easy to read past.** The pastoralists had *always* moved. What changed was **who decided when**. Movement timed by **grass, water and weather** was replaced by movement timed by **a piece of paper**. **The state did not abolish mobility — it confiscated the judgement that governed it.** And a schedule that ignores the actual state of the pasture is precisely what produces **both** premature departure and overgrazing.

> **Source C — H.S. Gibson, Deputy Conservator of Forests, Darjeeling, 1913**, stating the forester's logic with perfect clarity: *"**…forest which is used for grazing cannot be used for any other purpose and is unable to yield timber and fuel, which are the main LEGITIMATE forest produce…**"*
> 🎯 **The word to seize on is "legitimate."** Grazing was not judged harmful; it was judged **illegitimate** — an unauthorised use of an asset whose proper output had been defined in advance as timber. **Compare Chapter 4: "foresters and villagers had very different ideas of what a good forest should look like."**

#### 3️⃣ The **Criminal Tribes Act, 1871** — ⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
   THE SUSPICION
   "British officials were SUSPICIOUS OF NOMADIC PEOPLE. They
    DISTRUSTED mobile craftsmen and traders who hawked their
    goods in villages, and pastoralists who CHANGED THEIR PLACES
    OF RESIDENCE EVERY SEASON."
                          |
   THE PREFERENCE
   ⭐⭐ "The colonial government WANTED TO RULE OVER A SETTLED
    POPULATION. They wanted the rural people to LIVE IN VILLAGES,
    IN FIXED PLACES WITH FIXED RIGHTS ON PARTICULAR FIELDS.
    SUCH A POPULATION WAS EASY TO IDENTIFY AND CONTROL."
                          |
   THE EQUATION
   ⭐⭐⭐ "THOSE WHO WERE SETTLED WERE SEEN AS PEACEABLE AND LAW
    ABIDING; THOSE WHO WERE NOMADIC WERE CONSIDERED TO BE
    CRIMINAL."
                          |
   ⭐⭐ THE LAW -- 1871, THE CRIMINAL TRIBES ACT
   "By this Act many communities of CRAFTSMEN, TRADERS AND
    PASTORALISTS were classified as CRIMINAL TRIBES.
    ⭐ THEY WERE STATED TO BE CRIMINAL BY NATURE AND BIRTH."
                          |
   THE CONSEQUENCES
   - "expected to live ONLY IN NOTIFIED VILLAGE SETTLEMENTS"
   - "NOT ALLOWED TO MOVE OUT WITHOUT A PERMIT"
   - ⭐ "THE VILLAGE POLICE KEPT A CONTINUOUS WATCH ON THEM"
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Criminal by nature and birth"** — pause on that phrase. This is **not** a law against acts; it is a law against **descent**. A child was born a criminal. ⭐ **Compare Chapter 3's Nuremberg Laws**, where citizenship was made a matter of blood: **two very different regimes, and in both, the decisive move is converting a social category into a hereditary one.** Note the difference too — the British goal was **control and legibility**, not extermination — but the legal technique is recognisably the same.
> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐ **GS2 link worth knowing:** the Act was **repealed in 1952**, and the affected communities are today called **denotified tribes (DNTs)** — the **Renke** and **Idate** Commissions and the **Development and Welfare Board for DNTs** deal with their continuing marginalisation. **The stigma outlived the statute by seventy years.** If a question touches DNTs, this chapter is where the story starts.

#### 4️⃣ The **Grazing Tax** (from the mid-19th century)

| | |
|---|---|
| **The logic** | *"to **expand its revenue income**, the colonial government **looked for every possible source of taxation**. So tax was imposed on **land, on canal water, on salt, on trade goods, and even on ANIMALS**"* |
| ⭐⭐ **The tax** | *"Pastoralists had to **pay tax on EVERY ANIMAL they grazed** on the pastures… The **tax per head of cattle went up rapidly** and the **system of collection was made increasingly efficient**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **1850s–1880s — collection by contractors** | *"the right to collect the tax was **AUCTIONED OUT TO CONTRACTORS**. These contractors **tried to extract AS HIGH A TAX AS THEY COULD** to recover the money they had paid to the state and **earn as much profit as they could within the year**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **By the 1880s — direct collection** | *"the government began **collecting taxes DIRECTLY from the pastoralists**. **Each of them was given a PASS.** To enter a grazing tract, a cattle herder **had to show the pass and pay the tax**. **The number of cattle heads he had and the amount of tax he paid was entered on the pass**"* |

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Two things to notice.** First, the **contractor system was structurally extortionate**: a man who has bought a one-year right will squeeze maximum revenue within that year, because he bears **none** of the long-term cost of ruining the herd. (You saw the identical dynamic in **Chapter 4** with railway timber contractors *"cutting trees indiscriminately"*, and in **Class 8 Ch.3** with revenue farming.) Second, **the pass is the real innovation** — it converts a herd into a **countable, documented, checkable** thing. **Taxation required legibility, and legibility required that people stop moving unrecorded.** All four measures are ultimately the same project.

### 📉 2.1 How these changes affected pastoralists — ⭐⭐⭐⭐ the causal chain

```
   GRAZING LANDS TAKEN OVER (Waste Land Rules)
   + FORESTS RESERVED (Forest Acts)
                    |
                    v
   ⭐ "a SERIOUS SHORTAGE OF PASTURES"
                    |
   "the EXISTING ANIMAL STOCK had to feed on WHATEVER GRAZING
    LAND REMAINED"
                    |
                    v
   ⭐⭐ "CONTINUOUS INTENSIVE GRAZING of these pastures"
                    |
   -- because the old remedy is now illegal --
   "Usually nomadic pastoralists grazed their animals in one area
    and MOVED TO ANOTHER area. THESE PASTORAL MOVEMENTS ALLOWED
    TIME FOR THE NATURAL RESTORATION OF VEGETATION GROWTH.
    ⭐⭐ WHEN RESTRICTIONS WERE IMPOSED ON PASTORAL MOVEMENTS,
    GRAZING LANDS CAME TO BE CONTINUOUSLY USED AND THE QUALITY
    OF PASTURES DECLINED."
                    |
                    v
   "a further SHORTAGE OF FORAGE for animals and the
    DETERIORATION OF ANIMAL STOCK"
                    |
                    v
   ⭐⭐ "UNDERFED CATTLE DIED IN LARGE NUMBERS DURING SCARCITIES
    AND FAMINES."
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **State the irony explicitly in any answer, because it is the chapter's sharpest argument:** the colonial state restricted grazing **on the stated ground that grazing damaged vegetation** — and the restriction itself **produced the overgrazing it claimed to prevent**. Confining herds to a shrinking area **guarantees** continuous use of the same ground. **Mobility was the conservation mechanism; abolishing it created the degradation.**
> 🔗 **For GS3 this is directly usable against the naive "tragedy of the commons" argument**: the commons here was not ungoverned. It was governed by **rotation, seasonal timing and inter-community agreements** — exactly like Bastar's village forests in Chapter 4.

> **Source D — the Royal Commission on Agriculture, 1928**, confirming it in the state's own words: *"**The extent of the area available for grazing has gone down tremendously** with the extension of area under cultivation because of **increasing population, extension of irrigation facilities, acquiring the pastures for Government purposes — for example, defence, industries and agricultural experimental farms**. [Now] **breeders find it difficult to raise large herds. Thus their earnings have gone down. The quality of their livestock has deteriorated, dietary standards have fallen and indebtedness has increased.**"*
> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐ Note the **five** causes the Commission itself lists — population, irrigation, **defence**, **industries**, **experimental farms** — and note that it records the outcome as a **four-fold decline**: earnings, livestock quality, **diet**, and rising **debt**. This is an **official** source admitting the damage, which makes it far more persuasive in an answer than a nationalist assertion would be.

### 🔄 2.2 How pastoralists coped — ⭐⭐⭐ note that this section is about **agency**, not victimhood

| Strategy | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Reduce the herd** | *"Some **reduced the number of cattle** in their herds, since there was not enough pasture to feed large numbers"* |
| **Find new pastures** | *"Others **discovered new pastures** when movement to old grazing grounds became difficult"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The 1947 case — Partition redrew a pastoral route** | *"**After 1947**, the camel and sheep herding **Raikas** could **no longer move into SINDH and graze their camels on the banks of the INDUS**, as they had done earlier. **The new political boundaries between India and Pakistan stopped their movement.** So they had to find new places to go. **In recent years they have been migrating to HARYANA where sheep can graze on agricultural fields after the harvests are cut** — this is the time that the fields **need the manure that the animals provide**"* |
| **The rich settle** | *"Over the years, **some richer pastoralists began buying land and settling down, giving up their nomadic life**. Some became **settled peasants** cultivating land, others took to **more extensive trading**"* |
| ⭐ **The poor fall** | *"**Many poor pastoralists, on the other hand, BORROWED MONEY FROM MONEYLENDERS to survive. At times they LOST THEIR CATTLE AND SHEEP and became LABOURERS, working on fields or in small towns.**"* |

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The Raika case is the perfect illustration of how a political event becomes an ecological one.** Partition was a decision about sovereignty in Delhi and Karachi; for the Raikas it meant **the Indus bank was gone**. And their solution — **re-establishing the manure-for-stubble exchange in Haryana** — is exactly the Dhangar–Konkan arrangement, rebuilt on new ground. **The institution survived even though the route did not.**

**⭐⭐⭐⭐ And NCERT's conclusion to the Indian half — quote it:**

> *"**Yet, pastoralists not only continue to survive, in many regions their numbers have EXPANDED over recent decades.** When pastureland in one place was closed to them, they **changed the direction of their movement, reduced the size of the herd, combined pastoral activity with other forms of income and adapted to the changes in the modern world.** ⭐ **Many ecologists believe that IN DRY REGIONS AND IN THE MOUNTAINS, PASTORALISM IS STILL ECOLOGICALLY THE MOST VIABLE FORM OF LIFE.**"*

This is the origin of what is called **"fortress conservation"**: the assumption that nature is protected by **removing humans** from it. Set it beside **Chapter 4's** finding that Indian forests survived **because** villages protected them as sacred groves, and you have both sides of the modern conservation debate in a single book.
> **And NCERT gives you the bitter modern coda in a caption:** the areas *"hardest hit by drought and food shortage"* are **near Amboseli National Park**, which in one year generated *"approximately **240 million Kenyan Shillings** (about **$3.5 million US**) from tourism"* — while the **Kilimanjaro Water Project cuts through these communities** and ⭐ **"the villagers are barred from using the water for irrigation or for livestock."** **Revenue flows out; thirst stays behind.**

> **On Tanganyika (a boxed note worth knowing):** *"**Britain conquered what had been German East Africa during the First World War. In 1919 Tanganyika came under British control. It attained independence in 1961 and united with Zanzibar to form TANZANIA in 1964.**"*

### 🚧 3.2 The borders are closed

> *"In the nineteenth century, African pastoralists could **move over vast areas** in search of pastures… **From the late nineteenth century, the colonial government began imposing various restrictions on their mobility.**"*

| Restriction | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Reserves** | *"other pastoral groups were also forced to **live within the confines of special reserves**. **The boundaries of these reserves became the limits within which they could now move**"* |
| ⭐ **Permits** | *"**not allowed to move out with their stock without special permits**. And it was **difficult to get permits without trouble and harassment**. Those found guilty of disobeying the rules were **severely punished**"* |
| ⭐⭐ **Markets and trade** | *"Pastoralists were also **not allowed to enter the markets in white areas**. In many regions, they were **prohibited from participating in any form of trade**"* |
| **The reasoning** | *"**White settlers and European colonists saw pastoralists as dangerous and savage — people with whom all contact had to be minimised**"* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The contradiction** | *"**Cutting off all links was, however, NEVER REALLY POSSIBLE, because white colonists HAD TO DEPEND ON BLACK LABOUR to bore mines, and build roads and towns.**"* |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **That last line is the recurring hypocrisy of settler colonialism, and it is worth stating plainly:** the people declared **too dangerous to trade with** were simultaneously **indispensable to dig the mines and build the towns**. **Segregation was never separation; it was a mechanism for cheap labour.**

> **Source E — the Kaokoland herders of Namibia, 1949.** They had traditionally moved between **Kaokoland and nearby Ovamboland**, selling **skin, meat and other trade products** in neighbouring markets, *"All this was stopped with the new system of territorial boundaries."* Their own words:
> *"**We have difficulty. We cry. WE ARE IMPRISONED. We do not know why we are locked up. WE ARE IN JAIL. We have no place to live… We cannot get meat from the south… Our sleeping skins cannot be sent out… Ovamboland is closed for us.** We lived in Ovamboland for a long time. We want to take our cattle there, also our sheep and goats. **The borders are closed. THE BORDERS PRESS US HEAVILY. WE CANNOT LIVE.**"*
> 🎯 *"We are in jail"* — **said by people who had been moved nowhere.** The prison was built by drawing a line. **This is the most quotable source in the chapter** and it works for any answer about borders, mobility, or the human cost of partition — including India's own.

> **Source F — a magistrate's instruction to police, south-west Africa, 24 November 1937:** *"**Passes to enter the Territory should not be given to these Natives unless exceptional circumstances necessitate their entering… The object of the above proclamation is to RESTRICT THE NUMBER OF NATIVES ENTERING the Territory and to KEEP A CHECK ON THEM, and ordinary visiting passes should therefore NEVER be issued to them.**"*
> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐ **Note the identical instrument on two continents: the PASS.** A grazing pass in India recording head of cattle and tax paid; an entry pass in Namibia recording permission to exist in a place. **(And in South Africa the same device would become the pass laws of apartheid.) The document is how a state converts a person's movement into a permission.**

|---|---|
| **An enquiry in 1930 — Maasai holdings in Kenya** | **720,000 cattle · 820,000 sheep · 171,000 donkeys** |

> *"As the area of grazing lands shrank, **the adverse effect of the droughts INCREASED IN INTENSITY**. The frequent bad years led to **a steady decline of the animal stock**."*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **State the causal point precisely, because it is subtle and it is what earns marks:** the drought of 1933–34 was a **natural** event; **the death of half the herd was not**. Drought had always occurred; **nomadism was the insurance policy against it**. Colonial confinement did not cause the drought — **it cancelled the insurance**. This is the clearest available example of how **a "natural disaster" is made lethal by a political decision**, and it transfers directly to answers on **famine, disaster management and vulnerability** in GS1 and GS3.

## 🏁 The Conclusion — and it is the whole chapter's argument

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"So we see that pastoral communities in different parts of the world are affected in a variety of different ways by changes in the modern world. **New laws and new borders affect the patterns of their movement.** With increasing restrictions on their mobility, pastoralists find it difficult to move in search of pastures. **As pasture lands disappear grazing becomes a problem, while pastures that remain deteriorate through continuous overgrazing. Times of drought become times of crises, when cattle die in large numbers.**
>
> **YET, PASTORALISTS DO ADAPT TO NEW TIMES.** They **change the paths of their annual movement, reduce their cattle numbers, press for rights to enter new areas, exert political pressure on the government for relief, subsidy and other forms of support, and demand a right in the management of forests and water resources.**
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **PASTORALISTS ARE NOT RELICS OF THE PAST. THEY ARE NOT PEOPLE WHO HAVE NO PLACE IN THE MODERN WORLD. Environmentalists and economists have increasingly come to recognise that PASTORAL NOMADISM IS A FORM OF LIFE THAT IS PERFECTLY SUITED TO MANY HILLY AND DRY REGIONS OF THE WORLD.**"*

> 🎯 **Read the list of adaptations again — it ends in politics.** *Press for rights, exert political pressure, demand a right in the management of forests and water resources.* **The chapter closes with pastoralists as claimants in a democracy, not as a vanishing curiosity.** That is the note to end any answer on.

### 🟢 Mains angles

- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Explain why nomadic tribes need to move from one place to another. What are the advantages to the environment of this continuous movement?"** *(NCERT Q1.)* — **Why they move:** seasonal availability of pasture (snow in the mountains; the monsoon/dry alternation on the plateau); water; the animals' own tolerances (sheep cannot take the wet monsoon, buffaloes can; buffaloes cannot climb above ~11,000 ft); escape from **venomous flies** in the plains; and the **manure-for-stubble** exchange with settled farmers. **Environmental advantage:** *"This continuous movement **allowed the pastures to recover; it prevented their overuse**"* — a **rotation and regeneration system**, plus **nutrient transfer** from pasture to cropland via manure.
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Discuss why the colonial government brought in the Waste Land Rules, Forest Acts, Criminal Tribes Act and Grazing Tax, and how each changed the lives of pastoralists."** *(NCERT Q2 — the flagship question. Give purpose AND effect for each.)*

| Law | Why it was brought in | How it changed pastoral life |
|---|---|---|
| **Waste Land Rules** | **Revenue** — uncultivated land yielded neither revenue nor produce; plus **jute, cotton, wheat for England** | Grazing tracts **taken over and handed to select individuals**; **pastures shrank** |
| **Forest Acts** | To secure **commercially valuable timber (deodar, sal)**; belief that grazing **destroyed saplings** | **No entry to reserved forests**; permits, fixed timing and duration elsewhere; **fines for overstaying** — *"the permits… now ruled their lives"* |
| **Criminal Tribes Act 1871** | Desire to rule a **settled, identifiable, controllable population**; nomads equated with criminals | Declared **criminal by birth**; confined to **notified settlements**; **permit to move**; **continuous police watch** |
| **Grazing Tax** | To **expand revenue** from every possible source | Tax **per head of animal**, rising rapidly; **contractors extorted** (1850s–80s); then the **pass** system recording herd and tax |

- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Give reasons to explain why the Maasai community lost their grazing lands."** *(NCERT Q3 — the four reasons: the 1885 boundary; white settlement taking the best land, **60% lost**; encouraged expansion of peasant cultivation; and **game reserves** — Serengeti alone over **14,760 sq km**.)*
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Write about two changes that were similar for Indian pastoralists and the Maasai herders."** *(NCERT Q4 — pick any two and develop them:)*
  1. ⭐ **Grazing land lost to cultivation and to state reservation** — Waste Land Rules and Forest Acts in India; white settlement, encouraged peasant cultivation and game reserves in Maasailand.
  2. ⭐ **Mobility restricted by permits and borders** — forest permits and the grazing pass in India, reserve boundaries and entry passes in Africa; and **new political borders cutting old routes** — **Maasailand halved in 1885**, the **Raikas cut off from Sindh in 1947**, the **Kaokoland herders cut off from Ovamboland**.
  3. **Confinement made drought lethal** — underfed cattle dying in Indian scarcities; **over half the Maasai herd dead in 1933–34**.
  4. **Social differentiation** — richer pastoralists bought land and settled or traded, poorer ones fell into debt and wage labour, in **both** societies.
  5. **The state's preference for a settled, legible population** — *"criminal by nature and birth"* in India; pastoralists seen as *"dangerous and savage"* in Africa.
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **GS3: "Restrictions on pastoral mobility caused the very degradation they were meant to prevent." Examine.** — The strongest analytical answer available from this chapter: movement as a **rotation/regeneration mechanism**; confinement → **continuous intensive grazing** → pasture decline → forage shortage → stock deterioration → **mass deaths in drought**; supported by the **Royal Commission of 1928**; and extended to the critique of the naive **"tragedy of the commons"**, since the commons here was governed by **rotation, seasonal timing and inter-community arrangements**.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **"Fortress conservation": examine the costs of excluding communities from protected areas.** — Serengeti, Maasai Mara, Samburu; Amboseli's **240 million shillings of tourism revenue** beside drought-hit neighbours **barred from the Kilimanjaro water**; contrast with **Chapter 4's sacred groves** and community patrolling, and with India's **FRA 2006** and **eco-development/JFM** models.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **GS2/Society: from "criminal tribes" to "denotified tribes."** — The 1871 Act, repeal in **1952**, and the continuing stigma; the Renke and Idate Commissions; the Development and Welfare Board for DNTs.
- ⭐⭐⭐ **"Pastoralism is not a survival from the past but an adaptation to specific ecologies." Discuss.** — Use the closing paragraph, the ecologists' verdict on dry regions and mountains, the expansion of pastoral numbers in recent decades, and the Raikas' post-1947 reinvention in Haryana.

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Nomads move to earn a living, and NCERT's point is that they are "not simply survivors from a bygone era."**
2. ⭐ **Mountains — the cycle is set by SNOW.** **Gujjar Bakarwals (J&K, goat and sheep)** winter in the **Siwaliks**, cross the **Pir Panjal** in a **kafila** to the **Kashmir valley** by end April, return by end September. **Gaddis (Himachal, sheep and goats)** go to **Lahul and Spiti**, and on the way back **reap the summer harvest and sow the winter crop**. **Gujjar cattle herders (Garhwal and Kumaon)** move between the **bhabar** in winter and the **bugyals** in summer. Also the **Bhotiyas, Sherpas and Kinnauris**.
3. ⭐⭐ **The ecological principle:** *"This continuous movement **allowed the pastures to recover; it prevented their overuse**."*
4. ⭐ **Plateau, plains and desert — the cycle is set by the MONSOON, not the cold.** **Dhangars (Maharashtra, ~467,000)** spend the monsoon on the dry plateau growing **bajra**, march **west in October** to the **Konkan**, where they **manure the fields and feed on the stubble** and are given **rice** to carry home — and leave before the monsoon **because sheep cannot tolerate wet conditions**. **Gollas** herd **cattle**; **Kurumas and Kurubas** rear sheep and goats and **sell blankets**. **Banjaras** sell **plough cattle** for **grain and fodder** across UP, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP and Maharashtra. **Raikas** of the Rajasthan desert **combine cultivation with pastoralism**; the ***Maru* Raikas herd camels**.
5. ⭐ **The work demanded judgement** — how long a herd could stay, where water and pasture lay, timing, passage through others' territory, and **relationships with farmers on the way**.
6. ⭐⭐ **Under colonial rule:** *"their grazing grounds shrank, their movements were regulated, and the revenue they had to pay increased."*
7. ⭐⭐ **Waste Land Rules** — all uncultivated land looked **"unproductive"**, so it was **taken over and given to select individuals**; **in most areas it was grazing tract**.
8. ⭐⭐ **Forest Acts** — **no access at all to reserved forests** (deodar, sal); in protected forests, **permits fixing entry, exit and duration**, with **fines for overstaying**, because officials believed **grazing destroyed saplings**.
9. ⭐⭐ **Criminal Tribes Act, 1871** — the state *"wanted to rule over a settled population… easy to identify and control"*; **"those who were settled were seen as peaceable; those who were nomadic were considered to be criminal."** Communities declared **criminal by nature and birth**, confined to **notified settlements**, needing **permits**, under **continuous police watch**.
10. ⭐⭐ **Grazing tax** — per head of animal; **auctioned to contractors 1850s–1880s** who extorted to recoup their bid; then **direct collection with a pass** recording cattle heads and tax paid.
11. ⭐⭐⭐ **The result:** pastures shrank → **continuous intensive grazing** → pasture quality declined → forage shortage → stock deterioration → **underfed cattle died in large numbers in scarcities and famines**. The **Royal Commission on Agriculture, 1928** confirmed falling earnings, deteriorating livestock, **falling dietary standards and rising indebtedness**.
12. ⭐⭐ **Coping:** smaller herds; new pastures; **the Raikas, cut off from Sindh and the Indus after 1947, now migrate to Haryana** to graze harvested fields that need manure; **richer pastoralists bought land, settled or traded**; **poorer ones borrowed, lost their stock and became labourers**. Yet **numbers have expanded in many regions**, and ecologists hold pastoralism **still the most viable form of life in dry regions and mountains**.
13. ⭐⭐ **Africa holds over half the world's pastoralists; over 22 million depend on it** — **Bedouins, Berbers, Maasai, Somali, Boran, Turkana**. The **Maasai** number **300,000 in southern Kenya and 150,000 in Tanzania**; *Maa-sai* means **"my people"**; they held that **tilling the land is a crime against nature**.
14. ⭐⭐⭐ **Four reasons the Maasai lost their land:** the **1885 boundary between British Kenya and German Tanganyika cut Maasailand in half**; **white settlement took the best grazing** and left them **60% poorer in land**, confined to an **arid zone**; **peasant cultivation was encouraged** and pastures became fields — **reversing the Maasai's pre-colonial dominance over their agricultural neighbours**; and **game reserves** — **Maasai Mara, Samburu, Serengeti**, the last created over **14,760 sq km** of their grazing land.
15. ⭐⭐ **The borders closed:** confinement to reserves, **special permits** obtainable only *"with trouble and harassment"*, **exclusion from white markets and often from all trade** — while *"white colonists **had to depend on black labour** to bore mines and build roads and towns."* The **Kaokoland herders of Namibia, 1949**: *"**We are in jail… The borders press us heavily. We cannot live.**"*
16. ⭐⭐⭐ **Drought:** nomadism was the defence against it. Confined, the Maasai could not move — an enquiry in **1930** recorded **720,000 cattle, 820,000 sheep, 171,000 donkeys**, and **in just two years, 1933 and 1934, over half the cattle in the Reserve died**.
17. ⭐⭐⭐ **Social change at two levels:** the **age-based distinction between elders (who ruled in councils) and warriors (who protected and raided)** was **disturbed** by the British **appointing chiefs** and **banning raiding**; and **a new distinction between wealthy and poor pastoralists developed** — chiefs accumulated wealth, **lent money to neighbours who needed cash for taxes**, moved to towns and traded, and **survived war and drought**, while the poor **lost everything and became charcoal burners, odd-jobbers and construction labour**.
18. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The conclusion:** *"**Pastoralists are not relics of the past…** pastoral nomadism is a form of life that is **perfectly suited to many hilly and dry regions of the world**"* — and they adapt by **changing routes, reducing herds, pressing for rights, exerting political pressure, and demanding a role in the management of forests and water resources.**
