# Theme 9 — Colonialism and the Countryside
## *Exploring Official Archives* (1765 – 1878)

> **The one-line point:** This chapter looks like three regional stories — Bengal's zamindars, the Rajmahal hills, the Deccan riots — but its real subject is announced in its own subtitle: **the colonial archive is not a neutral record.** The two great documents it examines, the **Fifth Report (1813)** and the **Deccan Riots Report (1878)**, were written by **officials with an interest in what the document said** — one wanted to convict the Company of misrule, the other wanted to acquit the government of causing a rebellion — and **each one bent its own evidence accordingly.** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Learn the agrarian facts (they are heavy Prelims fodder). But the sentence that wins Mains marks is NCERT's own: *"We need to know who wrote the reports and why they were written."***

## 🎬 The Framing — read this before anything else

NCERT tells you in the opening paragraph that this chapter has **two subjects, not one**. First, what colonial rule did to people:

> *"In this chapter you will see **what colonial rule meant to those who lived in the countryside**… You will look at the way the English East India Company (E.I.C.) **established its raj in the countryside, implemented its revenue policies, what these policies meant to different sections of people, and how they changed everyday lives.**"*

Then it makes a claim about **law** that you should carry into GS-II and GS-III as well:

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Laws introduced by the state have consequences for people**: they determine to an extent **who grows richer and who poorer, who acquires new land and who loses the land they have lived on, where peasants go when they need money.**"*

And immediately refuses the picture of the peasant as a passive victim:

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"As you will see, however, **people were not only subject to the working of laws, they also resisted the law by acting according to what they believed to be just.** In doing so **people defined the way in which laws operated, thereby modifying their consequences.**"*

**Read that sentence twice.** It is the analytical key to the whole chapter, and it explains four otherwise unconnected episodes: the raja who buys back his own estate through servants; the jotedar who provokes lawsuits he knows will never end; the Paharia chief who refuses the British allowance; and the ryot at Supa who burns a ledger instead of stealing the cash inside the house. **In each case a law existed, and people reshaped what it actually did.**

And then the second subject — the one in the subtitle:

> *"You will also come to know about **the sources that tell us about these histories, and the problems historians face in interpreting them**. You will read about **revenue records and surveys, journals and accounts left by surveyors and travellers, and reports produced by enquiry commissions.**"*

```
   THE ARCHITECTURE OF THIS CHAPTER
   -- three regions, one argument about EVIDENCE --

   SECTION 1   BENGAL, the WETLANDS              1793 onwards
        |      Permanent Settlement -> auctions -> benami ->
        |      the rise of the JOTEDARS
        |      THE DOCUMENT: ** THE FIFTH REPORT, 1813 **
        |      Its bias: written to PROSECUTE the Company, so it
        |      EXAGGERATED zamindari collapse
        |

        |      PAHARIAS (hoe, shifting cultivation, forest)
        |      vs SANTHALS (plough, rice and cotton, market)
        |      DAMIN-I-KOH 1832 -> SANTHAL REVOLT 1855-56
        |      THE DOCUMENT: ** BUCHANAN'S JOURNAL **
        |      Its bias: a Company employee travelling with a
        |      Company army, told what to look for
        |
   SECTION 3   THE BOMBAY DECCAN, the DRYLANDS    1820s-1875
        |      RYOTWARI -> debt -> famine -> COTTON BOOM 1861
        |      -> credit collapse 1865 -> REVOLT AT SUPA 1875
        |
   SECTION 4   ** THE DECCAN RIOTS COMMISSION, report 1878 **
        |      Its bias: asked ONE question by the government
        |      and answered it in the government's favour
        v
   ** THE CONCLUSION THAT COVERS ALL THREE:
      "Official reports ... are invaluable sources ... BUT THEY HAVE
       TO BE ALWAYS READ WITH CARE AND JUXTAPOSED WITH EVIDENCE
       CULLED FROM NEWSPAPERS, UNOFFICIAL ACCOUNTS, LEGAL RECORDS
       AND, WHERE POSSIBLE, ORAL SOURCES." **
```

> 🎯 **Why the framing decides your marks.** Two candidates answer *"What are the problems of using official sources in writing about the history of peasants?"* One writes: *"Official sources are biased and one-sided."* The other writes: *"The Fifth Report of 1813 and the Deccan Riots Report of 1878 fail in opposite directions and for the same reason. The Fifth Report was produced by a Parliamentary Select Committee at a moment when private traders and industrialists wanted the Company's charter revoked; intent on criticising Company maladministration, it exaggerated the collapse of zamindari power and overestimated the land zamindars had lost — which is exactly what the benami evidence from the zamindars' own family archives disproves. The Deccan Riots Commission, by contrast, was appointed by the government and asked specifically whether the revenue demand had caused the revolt; it answered no, and blamed the moneylenders. Both reports are accurate about many details and wrong about the thing their sponsors cared about."* **The second candidate has read the chapter as a chapter, not as three stories.**

### 📒 The analogy that fits this chapter: **a debt ledger written entirely in the creditor's hand**

Not a software analogy. This chapter's own central object is a **book of accounts**, and the whole methodological lesson is already sitting inside it.

Picture yourself as a Deccan ryot in 1874. You cannot read. You borrow Rs 100 to pay the revenue. The **only record** of what you owe is the **bahi khata** — a book in the *sahukar's* handwriting, kept in the *sahukar's* house, in a script you do not know. You repay in grain; he gives you **no receipt**. He carries away your harvest and says he will credit it; it never appears as an entry. You sign a bond with a thumb impression, not knowing its clauses. And if there is ever a dispute, **that book is what the court will read.**

```
   THE LEDGER -- and why it is not a lie

   WHAT THE BOOK GETS RIGHT   :  most entries are REAL. The loan
                                 was real. The date was real. The
                                 rate was written down.

   WHAT THE BOOK LEAVES OUT   :  the repayment with no receipt;
                                 the grain removed and never
                                 credited; the price charged 25-50%
                                 above cash rate; what the borrower
                                 THOUGHT he was signing.

   WHO HELD THE PEN           :  the man with a stake in the total.

   ** THE POINT: the ledger is not FALSE. It is INTERESTED.
      Its silences are as precise as its entries. **
```

**Now make the leap the chapter is asking you to make.** The **Fifth Report** and the **Deccan Riots Report** are ledgers of exactly this kind, kept at the scale of an empire — the state's own account of its relationship with the countryside, written by the party that gained from it. Their figures are real; their omissions are placed. The Fifth Report over-enters one column (zamindari ruin) because its authors were prosecuting the Company. The Deccan Riots Report leaves one column blank (the revenue demand) because its authors were the government.

**And the ryots of Supa understood the principle better than most historians.** On 12 May 1875 they did not first break open the cash box. They **demanded the bahi khatas and the debt bonds, and burnt them in the street** — *"heaps of ashes are still to be seen."* They had worked out that in a colonial order **the document was the power**, and that destroying the record was the only way to cancel a debt they could never repay.

> 📒 **Where the analogy is exact:** a historian confronting the colonial archive is in the ryot's position — the record is in the other party's hand, the receipts are missing, and yet the record is the only evidence there is. The historian's answer is **not** the ryot's (you cannot burn the archive). It is to **audit** it: read the Fifth Report against the zamindars' own family papers and district records; read Buchanan's enthusiasm against what a Paharia would have said about the same hillside; read the Deccan Riots Commission's *conclusion* against the *petitions it printed in its own appendices.*
>
> ⚠️ **Where the analogy stops:** a private ledger has one author and one interest. The colonial archive has thousands of authors, and it frequently **carries its own refutation inside it** — the Deccan Riots Report's conclusion says the revenue demand was blameless, while the petitions it collected describe a demand raised **50 to 100 per cent**. That is a piece of luck the indebted ryot never had. **The analogy builds the model. The dates, figures and quotations still have to be learned.**

The chapter opens on a bullock cart carrying Deccan cotton toward a world market that is about to make some men rich and ruin many more.

## 1️⃣ Bengal and the Zamindars

> *"As you know, **colonial rule was first established in Bengal**. It is here that the **earliest attempts were made to reorder rural society and establish a new regime of land rights and a new revenue system.**"*

📎 **Margin term:** ***Raja*** *(literally king)* — *"a term that was often used to designate **powerful zamindars**."* ⭐⭐⭐ **So a "Raja of Burdwan" is not a sovereign; he is a big revenue-payer with a royal title.**

> 🖼️ **Fig. 9.2 — the Burdwan raja's City Palace on Diamond Harbour Road, Calcutta.** *"By the late nineteenth century many rich zamindars of Bengal had **city palaces with ballrooms, large grounds, entrance porches supported by Corinthian columns**."*
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Do not treat this as decoration — it is evidence for two arguments at once.** (a) The zamindars **did not disappear** after 1793; by the late 1800s they were rich enough for Corinthian columns. (b) They were **absentee** — living in Calcutta, not in the villages — which is precisely the space into which the **jotedar** moved. *(Corinthian columns are Greco-Roman; the ballroom is European. The zamindar's power is now displayed in the coloniser's architectural vocabulary.)*

### 🔨 1.1 An auction in Burdwan — the puzzle that opens the chapter ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"In **1797** there was an auction in **Burdwan (present-day Bardhaman)**. It was a **big public event**. A number of **mahals (estates)** held by the **Raja of Burdwan** were being sold."*

**The legal machinery behind the auction, in NCERT's own three steps:**

```
   STEP 1 : "The PERMANENT SETTLEMENT had come into operation
             in ** 1793 **."
   STEP 2 : "The East India Company had ** FIXED THE REVENUE **
             that each zamindar had to pay."
   STEP 3 : "The estates of those who ** FAILED TO PAY ** were to
             be ** AUCTIONED to recover the revenue **."
                        |
   THE RAJA OF BURDWAN  :  "had accumulated ** HUGE ARREARS **",
                           so his estates were put up for auction.
```

**And then the twist, which is the reason the chapter begins here:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Numerous purchasers came to the auction and the estates were sold to the highest bidder. But the **Collector soon discovered a strange twist to the tale. Many of the purchasers turned out to be servants and agents of the raja who had bought the lands on behalf of their master. Over 95 per cent of the sale at the auction was fictitious.** The raja's estates had been **publicly sold, but he remained in control of his zamindari.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The single most quotable number in Section 1: OVER 95 PER CENT of the 1797 Burdwan auction was fictitious.** Do not confuse it with the other percentage in this section — *over 15 per cent* of **all** auction sales in Bengal were fictitious *(§ 1.5)*. **95% = the Burdwan auction. 15% = the Bengal-wide total.**

**NCERT then poses the three questions the rest of Section 1 answers.** Use them as your revision headings:

```
   Q1  "Why had the raja FAILED TO PAY the revenue?"     -> answered in 1.3
   Q2  "WHO WERE THE PURCHASERS at the auction?"         -> answered in 1.4 and 1.5
   Q3  "What does the story tell us about what was
        happening in the RURAL AREAS OF EASTERN INDIA
        at that time?"                                    -> answered in 1.6
```

### 💸 1.2 The problem of unpaid revenue — why the Permanent Settlement existed ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The estates of the Burdwan raj were **not the only ones** sold during the closing years of the eighteenth century. **Over 75 per cent of the zamindaris changed hands after the Permanent Settlement.**"*

**The crisis the settlement was meant to solve:**

> *"By the **1770s**, the **rural economy in Bengal was in crisis**, with **recurrent famines and declining agricultural output**."*

*(Beyond NCERT: the catastrophe behind that sentence is the **Bengal Famine of 1770**, in which an estimated one-third of the population of Bengal died. NCERT does not name it here; know that it is what "recurrent famines" refers to.)*

**The theory — memorise this causal chain; it is the answer to half the possible questions on the Permanent Settlement:**

```
   THE COMPANY'S REASONING, 1793

   "Officials felt that AGRICULTURE, TRADE and the REVENUE
    RESOURCES OF THE STATE could all be developed by
    ** ENCOURAGING INVESTMENT IN AGRICULTURE **."
                        |
   HOW?  "by ** SECURING RIGHTS OF PROPERTY ** and
          ** PERMANENTLY FIXING THE RATES OF REVENUE DEMAND **"
                        |
        -------------------------------------
        |                                   |
   FOR THE STATE:                     FOR THE INVESTOR:
   "the Company could look             "entrepreneurs could feel
    forward to a ** REGULAR             sure of ** EARNING A PROFIT
    FLOW OF REVENUE **"                 from their investment, since
                                        the state would NOT SIPHON
                                        IT OFF by increasing its
                                        claim"
        -------------------------------------
                        |
   THE HOPED-FOR RESULT:
   "the emergence of a class of ** YEOMEN FARMERS AND RICH
    LANDOWNERS ** who would have the ** CAPITAL AND ENTERPRISE
    TO IMPROVE AGRICULTURE **"
                        |
   AND THE POLITICAL BONUS:
   ** "Nurtured by the British, this class would also be
      LOYAL TO THE COMPANY." **
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That last clause is the one candidates forget.** The Permanent Settlement was **not only** a fiscal device. It was an attempt to **manufacture a loyal propertied class** in a conquered province. When you are asked to evaluate the settlement, judge it on **both** objectives — and note that it failed at improving agriculture while succeeding, in the long run, at creating a landed class with a stake in British rule.

> 🖼️ **Fig. 9.3 — Charles Cornwallis (1738–1805), painted by Thomas Gainsborough, 1785.** *"He was the **commander of the British forces during the American War of Independence** and the **Governor General of Bengal when the Permanent Settlement was introduced there in 1793**."*
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Both halves are examinable.** Cornwallis is the general who **surrendered at Yorktown in 1781** — the man who lost America — and who then reorganised the land system of Bengal. *(Beyond NCERT: the Permanent Settlement is also called the **Istamrari** or **Zamindari settlement**, and Cornwallis is associated with the **Cornwallis Code (1793)**, the separation of revenue and judicial functions, and the **Europeanisation of the higher services**.)*

📎 **Margin term:** ***Taluqdar*** — *"literally means '**one who holds a taluq**' or a **connection**. **Taluq came to refer to a territorial unit.**"*

**Who the settlement was made with — and the definition you must be able to reproduce:**

> *"After a **prolonged debate amongst Company officials**, the Permanent Settlement was made with the **rajas and taluqdars of Bengal**. They were now **classified as zamindars**, and they had to pay the revenue demand that was **fixed in perpetuity**."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"In terms of this definition, the zamindar was **NOT a landowner in the village, but a revenue Collector of the state.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the highest-frequency conceptual trap in the entire land-revenue topic.** The Permanent Settlement is loosely called a "landlord settlement", and the zamindar did in practice behave as a landlord. **But the settlement's own legal definition made him a revenue collector, not an owner.** The ambiguity is real and it is the source of a century of litigation — and of the tenancy legislation that followed. **In an answer, state the definition first, then the practice.**

**How a zamindari actually worked:**

```
   THE REVENUE ESTATE -- the unit the Company invented

   ONE ZAMINDAR
        |  "had several (sometimes as many as ** 400 **) villages
        |   under them"
        v
   "In Company calculations the villages within one zamindari
    formed ** ONE REVENUE ESTATE **."
        |
   "The Company fixed the ** TOTAL DEMAND OVER THE ENTIRE ESTATE **
    whose revenue the zamindar contracted to pay."
        |
        v
   THE ZAMINDAR'S BUSINESS MODEL:
   collects RENT from the different villages
        -> pays the REVENUE to the Company
        -> ** RETAINS THE DIFFERENCE AS HIS INCOME **
        -> "expected to pay the Company REGULARLY, failing which
            his estate could be ** AUCTIONED **"
```

### ⛔ 1.3 Why zamindars defaulted on payments — the FOUR reasons ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Company officials felt that a **fixed revenue demand would give zamindars a sense of security** and, assured of returns on their investment, encourage them to improve their estates. **In the early decades after the Permanent Settlement, however, zamindars regularly failed to pay the revenue demand and unpaid balances accumulated.**"*

**NCERT numbers the reasons itself — reproduce them in this order:**

```
   FOUR REASONS FOR DEFAULT (NCERT's own "First / Second /
   Third / Fourth")

   FIRST -- ** THE INITIAL DEMANDS WERE VERY HIGH **
        WHY: "if the demand was fixed for all time to come, the
        Company would NEVER be able to claim a share of increased
        income from land when prices rose and cultivation
        expanded. To ** MINIMISE THIS ANTICIPATED LOSS **, the
        Company pegged the revenue demand high, arguing that the
        burden on zamindars ** WOULD GRADUALLY DECLINE ** as
        agricultural production expanded and prices rose."

   SECOND -- ** THE TIMING WAS WRONG: THE 1790s WERE A SLUMP **
        "the prices of agricultural produce were DEPRESSED, making
        it difficult for the ryots to pay their dues to the
        zamindar."
        ** "If the zamindar could not collect the rent, how could
           he pay the Company?" **

   THIRD -- ** THE DEMAND WAS INVARIABLE AND PUNCTUAL **
        "the revenue was invariable, ** REGARDLESS OF THE
        HARVEST **, and had to be paid punctually."
        ** THE SUNSET LAW: "if payment did not come in by SUNSET
           of the specified date, the ZAMINDARI WAS LIABLE TO BE
           AUCTIONED." **

   FOURTH -- ** THE ZAMINDAR'S OWN POWERS WERE CUT **
        "the Permanent Settlement initially ** LIMITED THE POWER OF
        THE ZAMINDAR TO COLLECT RENT FROM THE RYOT AND MANAGE HIS
        ZAMINDARI **."
```

> 🧠 **How to remember them:** the demand was **too big**, at the **wrong time**, on a **rigid clock**, given to a man whose **hands had been tied**. *(Big / Bad-timing / Rigid / Tied.)*

📎 **Margin term:** ***Ryot*** — *"the way the term **raiyat**, used to designate **peasants** (Chapter 8), was spelt in British records. **Ryots in Bengal did not always cultivate the land directly, but leased it out to under-ryots.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That second sentence is doing quiet, heavy work.** The word "ryot" does not mean "poor tiller". A Bengal ryot could himself be a rentier over **under-ryots**. **So the countryside had at least four tiers — zamindar, ryot, under-ryot, and the landless labourer below them — and colonial law only saw the top one.** This is why "peasant" is a useless word in an answer unless you specify the layer.

**The political demotion of the zamindar — the paradox at the heart of the settlement:**

> *"The Company had **recognised the zamindars as important, but it wanted to control and regulate them, subdue their authority and restrict their autonomy.**"*

```
   WHAT THE COMPANY TOOK AWAY FROM THE ZAMINDAR

   *  "The zamindars' ** TROOPS were DISBANDED **"
   *  "** CUSTOMS DUTIES ABOLISHED **"
   *  "their '** CUTCHERIES ' (COURTS) brought under the
      SUPERVISION OF A COLLECTOR ** appointed by the Company"
   *  "Zamindars ** LOST THEIR POWER TO ORGANISE LOCAL JUSTICE
      AND THE LOCAL POLICE **"
                        |
                        v
   "Over time the ** COLLECTORATE emerged as an ALTERNATIVE
    CENTRE OF AUTHORITY **, severely restricting what the
    zamindar could do."
```

**And NCERT's chilling one-line illustration** — the instruction sent with a Company official to a defaulting raja's zamindari:

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"to **take charge of the District** and to use the **most effectual means to destroy all the influence and the authority of the raja and his officers**."*

> 💡 **The paradox in one line, worth writing in a Mains answer:** *the Permanent Settlement made the zamindar economically indispensable and politically powerless at the same time — it demanded that he behave like an entrepreneur while stripping him of the coercive apparatus with which he had always collected.*

**Rent collection on the ground:**

- *"At the time of rent collection, an officer of the zamindar, usually the **amlah**, came around to the village."*
- *"But **rent collection was a perennial problem**. Sometimes **bad harvests and low prices** made payment of dues difficult for the ryots. At other times **ryots deliberately delayed payment**."*
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Rich ryots and village headmen — jotedars and mandals — were only too happy to see the zamindar in trouble.** The zamindar could therefore **not easily assert his power over them.**"*
- *"Zamindars could **prosecute defaulters, but the judicial process was long drawn**."*
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**In Burdwan alone there were over 30,000 pending suits for arrears of rent payment in 1798.**"*

> 🔴 **Prelims-grade number: 30,000 pending suits, Burdwan, 1798.** And note *what it proves*: the Company gave the zamindar a **court** instead of an **army**, and the court was so slow that the substitution amounted to disarmament. **A right you cannot enforce in time is not a right.**

### 🌾 1.4 The rise of the jotedars ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"While many zamindars were facing a crisis at the end of the eighteenth century, a **group of rich peasants were consolidating their position in the villages.**"*

**The source for this — and note that it is a survey, i.e. an official record:**

> *"In **Francis Buchanan's survey of the Dinajpur district in North Bengal** we have a **vivid description of this class of rich peasants known as jotedars**."*

```
   WHO THE JOTEDAR WAS -- four powers in one man

   1. ** LAND **        "By the early nineteenth century, jotedars
                        had acquired ** VAST AREAS OF LAND --
                        sometimes as much as SEVERAL THOUSAND
                        ACRES **"
   2. ** TRADE **       "They controlled ** LOCAL TRADE **"
   3. ** CREDIT **      "as well as ** MONEYLENDING **"
   4. ** LABOUR **      cultivation through ** SHARECROPPERS
                        (ADHIYARS or BARGADARS) ** who
                        "brought their ** OWN PLOUGHS **, laboured
                        in the field, and handed over ** HALF THE
                        PRODUCE ** to the jotedars after the
                        harvest"
                        |
                        v
   ** "exercising IMMENSE POWER over the poorer cultivators of
      the region." **
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Three separate Prelims facts hide in that box:** *(a)* **adhiyars / bargadars = sharecroppers**, not tenants and not the jotedars themselves; *(b)* they gave **half** the produce, not a third; *(c)* they **brought their own ploughs** — i.e. the jotedar supplied land alone and took 50%, which is the definition of a rentier relationship dressed as farming.

**Why the jotedar beat the zamindar inside the village — NCERT's own comparison:**

| The zamindar | The jotedar |
|---|---|
| *"often lived in **urban areas**"* — the Calcutta palace of Fig. 9.2 | *"were **located in the villages**"* |
| Power exercised **through the amlah**, at a distance | *"exercised **direct control over a considerable section of poor villagers**"* |
| Depended on courts that took decades | Depended on **debt** — an instantly enforceable relationship |
| Legally the revenue-payer | ⭐ *"Within the villages, the **power of jotedars was more effective than that of zamindars**"* |

**The five specific tactics — learn them as a list, this is NCERT Exercise 1:**

```
   HOW THE JOTEDARS FOUGHT THE ZAMINDAR

   1. "** FIERCELY RESISTED efforts by zamindars to INCREASE THE
       JAMA of the village **"
   2. "** PREVENTED ZAMINDARI OFFICIALS from executing their
       duties **"
   3. "** MOBILISED RYOTS who were DEPENDENT ON THEM **"
   4. "** DELIBERATELY DELAYED PAYMENTS of revenue to the
       zamindar **"
   5. "when the estates of the zamindars were AUCTIONED for
       failure to make revenue payment, ** JOTEDARS WERE OFTEN
       AMONGST THE PURCHASERS **"
                        |
                        v
   ** ANSWER TO NCERT's QUESTION 2 -- "who were the purchasers
      at the auction?" -- the zamindar's OWN AGENTS on one side,
      and THE JOTEDARS on the other. **
```

**Where they were strongest, and what they were called elsewhere:**

> *"The jotedars were **most powerful in North Bengal**, although rich peasants and village headmen were emerging as commanding figures in the countryside in other parts of Bengal as well. In some places they were called **haoladars**, elsewhere they were known as **gantidars or mandals**. **Their rise inevitably weakened zamindari authority.**"*

> 🖼️ **Fig. 9.4 — Bengal village scene, painted by George Chinnery, 1820.** *"Chinnery stayed in India for **23 years (1802–25)**, painting portraits, landscapes and scenes of the everyday life of the common people. **Jotedars and moneylenders in rural Bengal lived in houses like the one you see on the right.**"* ⭐⭐⭐ **Use this caption as evidence of the jotedar's *visibility* — his power was in the village and could be seen from the road; the zamindar's was in Calcutta.**

### 📜 Source 1 — *"The jotedars of Dinajpur"* (Buchanan) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Buchanan describing how the jotedars of North Bengal resisted being disciplined:**

> *"**Landlords do not like this class of men, but it is evident that they are absolutely necessary**, unless the landlords themselves would advance money to their necessitous tenantry …*
>
> *The jotedars who cultivate large portions of lands are **very refractory, and know that the zamindars have no power over them**. They **pay only a few rupees on account of their revenue and then fall in balance almost every kist (instalment)**, they **hold more lands than they are entitled to by their pottahs (deeds of contract)**. Should the zamindar's officers, in consequence, **summon them to the cutcherry, and detain them for one or two hours with a view to reprimand them**, they **immediately go and complain at the Fouzdarry Thanna (police station) for imprisonment and at the munsiff's (a judicial officer at the lower court) cutcherry for being dishonoured** and **whilst the causes continue unsettled, they instigate the petty ryots not to pay their revenue** consequently …"*

> ❓ **NCERT's question: *"Describe the ways in which the jotedars resisted the authority of the zamindars."*** → ✅ **Model answer, using the source itself:**
> **(1) Chronic part-payment** — they *"pay only a few rupees… and then fall in balance almost every kist"*, so the zamindar's cash flow is permanently short. **(2) Encroachment** — they *"hold more lands than they are entitled to by their pottahs."* **(3) Weaponising colonial law against its own beneficiary** — the moment the zamindar detains them for even *"one or two hours"*, they file **two** complaints, criminal (**Fouzdarry Thanna**) and civil (**munsiff's cutcherry**), and *"whilst the causes continue unsettled"* the zamindar is paralysed. **(4) Mobilising the poor** — they *"instigate the petty ryots not to pay their revenue"*, converting a personal dispute into a village-wide rent strike. **(5) The structural weapon Buchanan admits in his first line** — the jotedar is the **only source of credit**, so *"landlords do not like this class of men, but… they are absolutely necessary."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The mark-winning observation:** point (3) is the chapter's opening thesis in miniature. *"People resisted the law by acting according to what they believed to be just… thereby modifying its consequences."* The Company built the thana and the munsiff's court to make Bengal governable; the jotedar used both to make his own zamindar ungovernable.

### 🎭 1.5 The zamindars resist — the fictitious sale ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The authority of the zamindars in rural areas, however, **did not collapse**. Faced with an exorbitantly high revenue demand and possible auction of their estates, they **devised ways of surviving the pressures. New contexts produced new strategies.**"*

**The mechanism, step by step. This is one of the most enjoyable passages in the whole NCERT and it is very examinable:**

```
   THE FICTITIOUS SALE -- how to be auctioned and keep your estate

   MOVE 0 : ** TRANSFER LAND TO A WOMAN. **
            The Raja of Burdwan "first transferred some of his
            zamindari to his ** MOTHER **, since the Company had
            decreed that the ** PROPERTY OF WOMEN WOULD NOT BE
            TAKEN OVER **."
              |
   MOVE 1 : ** DEFAULT ON PURPOSE. **
            "The revenue demand of the Company was ** DELIBERATELY
            WITHHELD **, and unpaid balances were allowed to
            accumulate."
              |
   MOVE 2 : ** BUY YOUR OWN ESTATE. **
            "When a part of the estate was auctioned, the
            ** ZAMINDAR'S MEN BOUGHT THE PROPERTY, OUTBIDDING
            OTHER PURCHASERS **."
              |
   MOVE 3 : ** DON'T PAY FOR IT. **
            "Subsequently they ** REFUSED TO PAY UP THE PURCHASE
            MONEY **, so that the estate ** HAD TO BE RESOLD **."
              |
   MOVE 4 : ** REPEAT UNTIL EVERYONE ELSE GIVES UP. **
            "Once again it was bought by the zamindar's agents,
            once again the purchase money was not paid, and once
            again there was an auction. ** This process was
            repeated ENDLESSLY, EXHAUSTING THE STATE, AND THE
            OTHER BIDDERS AT THE AUCTION. **"
              |
              v
   THE RESULT : "At last the estate was ** SOLD AT A LOW PRICE
            BACK TO THE ZAMINDAR **. ** The zamindar never paid
            the full revenue demand; the Company rarely recovered
            the unpaid balances that had piled up. **"
```

**The scale — two figures to memorise:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Between **1793 and 1801** **four big zamindaris of Bengal, including Burdwan**, made **benami purchases** that collectively yielded as much as **Rs 30 lakh**. **Of the total sales at the auctions, over 15 per cent were fictitious.**"*

📎 **Margin terms — both are high-value:**
- ***Benami*** — *"literally **anonymous**, is a term used in Hindi and several other Indian languages for **transactions made in the name of a fictitious or relatively insignificant person, whereas the real beneficiary remains unnamed.**"* *(Beyond NCERT: the word survives in current law — the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988**, heavily amended in **2016**. When you write about benami property today you are using a category the Burdwan raja helped invent.)*
- ***Lathyal*** — *"literally **one who wields the lathi or stick**, functioned as a **strongman of the zamindar**."*

**The two other survival tactics — force and loyalty:**

> *"When people from **outside the zamindari** bought an estate at an auction, they **could not always take possession**. At times their agents would be **attacked by lathyals of the former zamindar**."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Sometimes even **the ryots resisted the entry of outsiders**. They felt **bound to their own zamindar through a sense of loyalty** and perceived him as a **figure of authority** and themselves as his **proja (subjects)**. **The sale of the zamindari disturbed their sense of identity, their pride.** The zamindars therefore were **not easily displaced**."*

> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Do not skip past the ryots' resistance — it is the most interesting fact in Section 1.** The tenants of an oppressive landlord fought to **keep** him. Why? Because the relationship was not purely economic: the word ***proja*** means **subjects**, not tenants. An auction turned a lord into a commodity and his people into an item on a schedule, and they experienced that as **an injury to their own identity**. **Deploy this whenever an examiner asks whether colonial law "modernised" Indian rural society: the market in land ran into a moral order the market had no vocabulary for.**

**And the ending — the date that everyone gets wrong:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"By the beginning of the nineteenth century **the depression in prices was over**. Thus those who had survived the troubles of the 1790s **consolidated their power**. **Rules of revenue payment were also made somewhat flexible.** As a result, **the zamindar's power over the villages was strengthened**. It was **only during the Great Depression of the 1930s that they finally collapsed and the jotedars consolidated their power in the countryside.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Prelims/Mains trap of the first order.** The standard textbook story — "the Permanent Settlement destroyed the old zamindars and created a new class of merchant-landlords" — is **the Fifth Report's story**, and this chapter is written to correct it. **NCERT's chronology:** the zamindars were **squeezed in the 1790s**, **recovered from c. 1800**, held on through the nineteenth century, and **collapsed only in the 1930s** — killed not by Cornwallis but by the **Great Depression's collapse in agricultural prices**. *(Beyond NCERT: the legal end came with the **zamindari abolition Acts of the 1950s**, beginning with the **UP and Bihar** legislation and the **West Bengal Estates Acquisition Act, 1953**.)*

> 🖼️ **Fig. 9.6 — Maharaja Mehtab Chand (1820–79).** *"When the Permanent Settlement was imposed, **Tejchand was the Raja of Burdwan**. Subsequently under **Mehtab Chand the estate prospered**. **Mehtab Chand helped the British during the Santhal rebellion and the 1857 revolt.**"*
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This caption is a whole argument compressed into three lines.** *(a)* **Tejchand** is the raja of the 1797 auction — name him if you narrate it. *(b)* The estate **prospered** afterwards — the direct evidence for "the zamindars were not displaced". *(c)* And the payoff for the Company: the man whose family cheated the auction **helped crush the Santhal rebellion (1855–56) and the revolt of 1857.** **The Permanent Settlement's political objective — a landed class "loyal to the Company" — was, in the end, achieved.** Use Mehtab Chand as your one-line evidence for it.

### 📕 1.6 The Fifth Report — the first great "official archive" ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Many of the changes we are discussing were documented in detail in a report that was **submitted to the British Parliament in 1813**. It was the **fifth of a series of reports on the administration and activities of the East India Company in India.**"*

```
   THE FIFTH REPORT, 1813 -- the facts to memorise

   *  SUBMITTED TO : ** THE BRITISH PARLIAMENT ** (not to the
                     Company), in ** 1813 **
   *  PRODUCED BY  : ** A SELECT COMMITTEE ** of Parliament
   *  LENGTH       : ** 1,002 PAGES **
   *  OF WHICH     : ** OVER 800 PAGES WERE APPENDICES **
   *  CONTENTS OF THE APPENDICES:
        - ** PETITIONS OF ZAMINDARS AND RYOTS **
        - ** REPORTS OF COLLECTORS from different districts **
        - ** STATISTICAL TABLES on revenue returns **
        - ** NOTES on the revenue and judicial administration of
          BENGAL and MADRAS ** (present-day Tamil Nadu)
   *  EFFECT       : "became the basis of ** INTENSE
                     PARLIAMENTARY DEBATES ** on the nature of
                     the East India Company's rule in India"
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Note the ratio: 200 pages of argument, 800 pages of evidence.** That is why the Report remains indispensable even though its conclusions are unsafe — **the appendices contain the raw material that later historians used to refute the main text.** This is exactly the audit-the-ledger point from the analogy.

**WHY the Report was written — the political economy behind it. This is the part students skip and examiners love:**

```
   WHO WANTED THE EAST INDIA COMPANY INVESTIGATED, AND WHY

   1. ** ANTI-MONOPOLY GROUPS ** -- "many groups in Britain who
      were OPPOSED TO THE MONOPOLY that the East India Company
      had over trade with INDIA AND CHINA"; they wanted
      ** A REVOCATION OF THE ROYAL CHARTER **
   2. ** PRIVATE TRADERS ** -- "an increasing number of private
      traders wanted A SHARE IN THE INDIA TRADE"
   3. ** BRITISH INDUSTRIALISTS ** -- "keen to OPEN UP THE INDIAN
      MARKET FOR BRITISH MANUFACTURES"
   4. ** POLITICAL GROUPS ** -- "argued that the conquest of
      Bengal was benefiting ONLY THE EAST INDIA COMPANY BUT NOT
      THE BRITISH NATION AS A WHOLE"
   5. ** THE PRESS ** -- "incidents of the GREED AND CORRUPTION of
      Company officials were WIDELY PUBLICISED"
                        |
                        v
   PARLIAMENT'S RESPONSE: "passed a ** SERIES OF ACTS IN THE LATE
   EIGHTEENTH CENTURY to REGULATE AND CONTROL Company rule **",
   ** FORCED the Company to produce REGULAR REPORTS **, and
   ** APPOINTED COMMITTEES to enquire into its affairs. **
```

> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Now the crucial inference, which NCERT expects you to make yourself.** Every one of those five groups had **an interest in the Company looking bad.** The Fifth Report is therefore **prosecution evidence**, not a neutral survey — and prosecution evidence is systematically strongest exactly where the prosecution's case needs it. *(Beyond NCERT: the timing is not a coincidence. The **Charter Act of 1813** — the same year — **ended the Company's monopoly of trade with India** (retaining tea and the China trade) and opened India to private British traders and missionaries. **The Fifth Report is part of the ammunition for that Act.** Also remember the earlier statutes: the **Regulating Act, 1773** and **Pitt's India Act, 1784**.)*

**And NCERT's verdict, which is the chapter's methodological core:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"For over **a century and a half**, the Fifth Report has **shaped our conception** of what happened in rural Bengal in the late eighteenth century. **The evidence contained in the Fifth Report is invaluable. But official reports like this have to be read carefully. We need to know WHO WROTE the reports and WHY they were written.** In fact, recent researches show that the **arguments and evidence offered by the Fifth Report cannot be accepted uncritically.**"*

**The TWO specific errors — learn them as a pair, they are the answer to NCERT Exercise 9:**

> *"Researchers have carefully examined the **archives of various Bengal zamindars and the local records of the districts**… They indicate that, **intent on criticising the maladministration of the Company**, the Fifth Report:*
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(1) **exaggerated the collapse of traditional zamindari power**, as also*
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *(2) **overestimated the scale on which zamindars were losing their land.**"*
>
> *"As we have seen, **even when zamindaris were auctioned, zamindars were not always displaced, given the ingenious methods they used to retain their zamindaris.**"*

```
   HOW THE FIFTH REPORT WAS CAUGHT OUT
   -- a worked example of historical method --

   THE OFFICIAL RECORD SAYS   :  estates advertised, estates sold,
                                 purchase money received.
                                 -> looks like MASS DISPOSSESSION

   THE ZAMINDARS' OWN PAPERS  :  the buyers were the seller's
   AND DISTRICT RECORDS SAY      servants; the purchase money was
                                 never paid; the estate came back.
                                 -> ** OVER 95% OF THE BURDWAN
                                    AUCTION WAS FICTITIOUS **

   ** THE LESSON: an auction register records TRANSACTIONS, not
      OUTCOMES. The document was accurate and the conclusion drawn
      from it was false. THIS is what "reading a source critically"
      actually means -- not doubting the figures, but asking what
      the figures can and cannot show. **
```

### 📜 Source 2 — *From the Fifth Report* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Referring to the condition of zamindars and the auction of lands, the Fifth Report stated:**

> *"**The revenue was not realised with punctuality, and lands to a considerable extent were periodically exposed to sale by auction.** In the native year **1203, corresponding with 1796-97**, the land **advertised for sale** comprehended a jumma or assessment of **sicca rupees 28,70,061**, the extent of land **actually sold** bore a jumma or assessment of **14,18,756**, and the amount of **purchase money sicca rupees 17,90,416**. In **1204, corresponding with 1797-98**, the land **advertised** was for sicca rupees **26,66,191**, the quantity **sold** was for sicca rupees **22,74,076**, and the **purchase money** sicca rupees **21,47,580**. **Among the defaulters were some of the oldest families of the country.** Such were the **rajahs of Nuddea, Rajeshaye, Bishenpore** (all districts of Bengal), … and others, **the dismemberment of whose estates at the end of each succeeding year, threatened them with poverty and ruin**, and in some instances **presented difficulties to the revenue officers, in their efforts to preserve undiminished the amount of public assessment.**"*

**The numbers, laid out so you can see what the Report is doing with them:**

```
   THE FIFTH REPORT'S OWN TABLE (in sicca rupees)

   YEAR        ADVERTISED     ACTUALLY SOLD   PURCHASE MONEY
   1796-97     28,70,061      14,18,756       17,90,416
   (1203)                     (= ~49% of advertised)
   1797-98     26,66,191      22,74,076       21,47,580
   (1204)                     (= ~85% of advertised)

   ** WHAT THE REPORT WANTS YOU TO SEE: a rising tide of ruin --
      more land actually going under the hammer in the second year,
      and the OLDEST FAMILIES among the defaulters. **
   ** WHAT THE REPORT DOES NOT TELL YOU: WHO BOUGHT. And that is
      the only question that decides whether these figures mean
      dispossession or theatre. **
```

> ❓ **NCERT's three questions on Source 2 — answered:**
>
> **(1) *"From the tone in which evidence is recorded, what do you think is the attitude of the report to the facts narrated?"*** → ✅ **Sympathetic to the zamindars and hostile to the administration.** Look at the loaded vocabulary: *"not realised with punctuality"*, *"exposed to sale"*, *"some of the OLDEST FAMILIES of the country"*, *"dismemberment"*, *"threatened them with **poverty and ruin**"*. This is not the language of a ledger; it is the language of a **lament for a dispossessed aristocracy**. And note the final clause — the difficulty it worries about is *"presented difficulties **to the revenue officers**"* — i.e. **the system is not working even on its own terms.** ⭐ **The tone is an indictment of the Company, and that is exactly what a Select Committee sitting in 1813 was assembled to produce.**
>
> **(2) *"What is the Report trying to say through the figures?"*** → ✅ That **the Permanent Settlement was producing wholesale ruin**: large sums advertised, large sums sold, ancient families dismembered *"at the end of each succeeding year"*. The figures are marshalled as **proof of systemic collapse.**
>
> **(3) *"Can you think of any problem in making long-term generalisations from these figures of two years?"*** → ✅ **Yes — at least five, and this is a superb source-criticism drill:**
> **(a) Two years is not a trend** — and these two were chosen from the worst of the 1790s price depression, a slump NCERT tells us *"was over"* by the early 1800s. **(b) The years are not comparable to each other** — in 1796-97 about half the advertised land sold, in 1797-98 about 85%, and two data points cannot show a direction. **(c) "Sold" does not mean "lost"** — over 95% of the Burdwan sale was fictitious and the purchase money was often never paid. **(d) The figures are in *jumma* (assessment), not acreage or output** — so they measure the revenue attached to land, not land itself, still less agricultural distress. **(e) No denominator is given** — without the total assessment of Bengal, Rs 28 lakh advertised is a number without a size.
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Learn this five-point drill. It transfers directly to data-based questions in Mains and to statistics in Essay.**

> 📌 **NCERT's "Discuss…" box: *"Compare the account of the zamindars you have just read with that in Chapter 8."*** → ✅ **This is a gift of a question — a ready-made Mains comparison.** Theme 8 (*Peasants, Zamindars and the State*, Mughal agrarian society) shows a zamindar who is **a hereditary local magnate with armed retainers, a fort, milkiyat (personal) lands, the right to a share of the produce, and a genuine social base** — he is a **little lord**, and the Mughal state deals with him as a partner it cannot fully control. Theme 9 shows a zamindar whom the law defines as **"not a landowner in the village, but a revenue Collector of the state"**, whose **troops are disbanded, customs duties abolished, courts subordinated to a Collector**, and whose estate can be **auctioned at sunset**.
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The comparison in one line:** *the Mughal zamindar's power rested on armed force and local standing and was constrained by custom; the colonial zamindar's power rested on a contract and was constrained by a clock.* **But the continuity is just as important:** in both systems the zamindar is the **intermediary** between the peasant and the state, and in both the state's real problem is that it cannot collect without him. **The colonial innovation was not the intermediary — it was making his position transferable by auction.**

## 2️⃣ The Hoe and the Plough

> **NCERT's own transition, and it tells you exactly what changes:** *"Let us now shift our focus from **the wetlands of Bengal to drier zones**, from a region of **settled cultivation** to one where **shifting agriculture** was practised. You will see the changes that came about when **the frontiers of the peasant economy expanded outwards, swallowing up pastures and forests** in the Rajmahal hills."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The section title is the whole argument.** A **hoe** scratches the ground lightly, is carried by one person, and works land you will abandon in three years. A **plough** cuts deep, needs cattle, and only makes sense on land you intend to keep. **These are not two farming tools. They are two theories of what land is** — and the colonial state backed one of them with law, money and troops.

```
   THE HOE                          THE PLOUGH
   -------                          ----------
   PAHARIAS                         SANTHALS
   shifting cultivation             settled plough agriculture
   burn undergrowth, use the ash    permanent fields
   move on when fertility drops     stay, and improve
   land held by the GROUP,          land held under a GRANT,
     "the entire region as their      inside surveyed boundaries
      land"                           marked by pillars
   forest = food, income, pasture   forest = an obstacle to clear
   NO revenue to the Company        REVENUE to the Company
      |                                 |
      v                                 v
   PUSHED DEEPER INTO THE HILLS     40 villages (1838)
   onto barren, rocky upper slopes    -> 1,473 villages (1851)
   -- and IMPOVERISHED, because     3,000 people -> 82,000
   shifting cultivation NEEDS
   the ability to move
      |                                 |
      |                                 v
      |                            AND THEN THE SANTHALS
      |                            LOSE IT TOO -- to taxes,
      |                            to DIKUS, to zamindars
      |                                 |
      v                                 v
   RESISTANCE, RAIDS                SANTHAL REVOLT 1855-56

   ** NCERT's line: "If Paharia life was symbolised by the HOE,
      which they used for shifting cultivation, the settlers came
      to represent the power of the PLOUGH. The battle between the
      hoe and the plough was a long one." **
```

### 🏞️ 2.1 In the hills of Rajmahal — and the man who could not get anyone to talk to him ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"In the early nineteenth century, **Buchanan travelled through the Rajmahal hills**. From his description, the hills appeared **impenetrable**, a zone where **few travellers ventured**, an area that **signified danger**. Wherever he went, **people were hostile, apprehensive of officials and unwilling to talk to them. In many instances they deserted their villages and absconded.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Start with the behaviour, not the landscape, because NCERT's whole method is here.** Villagers fled at his approach. A naïve reading says: *wild, dangerous country, primitive suspicious people* — which is exactly what the report was designed to convey. **The correct reading is the reverse: the flight is EVIDENCE, and what it is evidence of is a memory.** By the time Buchanan arrived in the winter of **1810–11**, these people had lived through a British **extermination campaign in the 1770s** and thirty years of "pacification". **They were not afraid of strangers. They were afraid of him specifically, and they had excellent reasons.** NCERT makes you wait several pages before giving you those reasons — that delay is deliberate.

**And the honest statement of what a travel journal can and cannot do — ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ quote this in any source-criticism answer:**

> *"His journal was written as **a diary of places he visited, people he encountered, and practices he saw**. It **raises questions in our mind, but does not always help us answer them**. It tells us about **a MOMENT IN TIME, but not about the LONGER HISTORY of people and places**. For that historians have to turn to other records."*

#### 🌾 Who the Paharias were — the full inventory

Late-eighteenth-century **revenue records** (note: a *different* source, used to fill Buchanan's gap) tell us these hill folk were the **PAHARIAS**.

```
   THE PAHARIA ECONOMY -- a complete year

   SHIFTING CULTIVATION
     * "cleared patches of forest by CUTTING BUSHES and
        BURNING THE UNDERGROWTH"
     * on patches "ENRICHED BY THE POTASH FROM THE ASH"
     * grew "a variety of PULSES AND MILLETS -- FOR CONSUMPTION"
        ** note: for CONSUMPTION, not for a market **
     * "SCRATCHED THE GROUND LIGHTLY WITH HOES"
     * cultivated a few years, "then LEFT IT FALLOW so that it
        could RECOVER ITS FERTILITY, and MOVED TO A NEW AREA"

   FROM THE FOREST
     * MAHUA (a flower)  -> FOOD
     * SILK COCOONS and RESIN -> FOR SALE
     * WOOD -> CHARCOAL PRODUCTION

   PASTURE
     * "the undergrowth that spread like a mat below the trees"
       and "the patches of grass that covered the LANDS LEFT
       FALLOW provided pasture for cattle"
       ** the fallow is not waste -- it IS the pasture **

   SO THEIR OCCUPATIONS WERE, in NCERT's own list:
   ** hunters * shifting cultivators * food gatherers *
      charcoal producers * silkworm rearers **
```

- They lived *"in **hutments within tamarind groves**, and rested in the **shade of mango trees**."*
- ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"They considered **the ENTIRE REGION as their land**, the basis of **their identity as well as survival**; and they **resisted the intrusion of outsiders**."*
- Their **chiefs** *"maintained the unity of the group, **settled disputes**, and **led the tribe in battles** with other tribes and plainspeople."*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The sentence about "the entire region as their land" is the one to keep**, and it is the root of everything that follows. Paharia land was held as **a territory by a community**, not as **a plot by a person**. A revenue system needs a *named individual* who *owns a measured plot* and can be *sued*. **The Paharias were not resisting taxation — they were unintelligible to it.** *(This is the same collision you met in Class 9 Ch.4 and Ch.5, and it is why NCERT put those chapters in the school syllabus.)*

#### ⚔️ The raids — and why NCERT insists they were not simply banditry ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"With their base in the hills, the Paharias **regularly raided the plains** where settled agriculturists lived."*

**NCERT then gives THREE distinct functions, and an exam answer must give all three:**

| # | Function | NCERT's words |
|:--:|---|---|
| 1 | **Subsistence** | *"necessary for **survival**, particularly in **years of scarcity**"* |
| 2 | **Political assertion** | *"a way of **asserting power over settled communities**"* |
| 3 | **Diplomacy** | *"a means of **negotiating political relations with outsiders**"* |

**And the settlement that grew out of it — this is the part everyone forgets:**

> *"The **zamindars on the plains had to often purchase peace by paying a REGULAR TRIBUTE to the hill chiefs**. Traders similarly gave a small amount to the hill folk **for permission to use the passes controlled by them**. ⭐ **Once the toll was paid, the Paharia chiefs PROTECTED the traders, ensuring that their goods were not plundered by anyone.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Read that last clause carefully, because it destroys the "savage raider" picture from the inside.** Once paid, the chiefs **provided security**. That is not plunder — **that is a protection regime with a tariff**, of exactly the kind states run. The Paharias were **operating a functioning political relationship with the plains**, and the zamindars and traders were **parties to it**. ⭐ **NCERT's own term for the arrangement is "this NEGOTIATED PEACE"** — and calling it *negotiated* concedes that both sides were negotiators.

#### 💥 How the peace broke — and the vocabulary that justified it ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"This negotiated peace was **somewhat fragile**. It broke down in the **last decades of the eighteenth century** when the **frontiers of settled agriculture were being aggressively extended in eastern India**. The British **encouraged forest clearance**, and **zamindars and jotedars turned uncultivated lands into rice fields.**"*

⭐ **Notice who does the clearing: the jotedars of § 1.4.** The same men rising in Bengal are pushing the agrarian frontier into the hills. **Sections 1 and 2 are one process seen from two ends.**

**The British motives — NCERT gives three, then the ideology underneath:**

```
   WHY THE BRITISH WANTED THE FOREST GONE

   THE THREE STATED REASONS
     1. "to ENLARGE THE SOURCES OF LAND REVENUE"
     2. "to PRODUCE CROPS FOR EXPORT"
     3. "to establish THE BASIS OF A SETTLED, ORDERED SOCIETY"

   AND THE BELIEF UNDERNEATH THEM
     "They also ASSOCIATED FORESTS WITH WILDNESS, and saw
      forest people as
          ** SAVAGE, UNRULY, PRIMITIVE, and DIFFICULT TO GOVERN **"

   THEREFORE
     "forests had to be CLEARED, settled agriculture ESTABLISHED,
      and forest people
          ** TAMED, CIVILISED and PERSUADED TO GIVE UP HUNTING
             AND TAKE TO PLOUGH AGRICULTURE **"

   ** Read the four adjectives again. "DIFFICULT TO GOVERN" is
      doing the real work; the other three are decoration on it.
      A people who cannot be assessed, listed, or sued is not
      "savage" -- it is ADMINISTRATIVELY INCONVENIENT. **
```

**The consequence was a squeeze, and NCERT states the mechanism plainly:**

> *"As settled agriculture expanded, **the area under forests and pastures CONTRACTED**. This **sharpened the conflict** between hill folk and settled cultivators. The former began to **raid settled villages with increasing regularity**, carrying away food grains and cattle."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The causation runs from dispossession to raiding, not the other way round.** The raids intensified *because* the resource base shrank. **Colonial officials read the raids as proof that the Paharias were lawless, and used that reading to justify the clearing that caused the raids.** That circle is the chapter's most transferable insight and it recurs exactly in Section 3 with the Deccan ryots.

#### 🩸 Extermination, then "pacification" ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

| Decade | Policy | NCERT's words |
|---|---|---|
| **1770s** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Extermination** | *"the British embarked on a **brutal policy of EXTERMINATION, hunting the Paharias down and killing them**"* |
| **1780s** | **Pacification**, under **Augustus Cleveland, Collector of Bhagalpur** | Paharia chiefs *"were given an **annual allowance** and **made responsible for the proper conduct of their men**… expected to **maintain order in their localities and discipline their own people**"* |

**And the result, which is the examinable half:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Many Paharia chiefs REFUSED the allowances.** Those who accepted, **most often LOST AUTHORITY WITHIN THE COMMUNITY**. Being in the pay of the colonial government, they came to be **perceived as SUBORDINATE EMPLOYEES or STIPENDIARY CHIEFS**."*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is one of the sharpest points in the chapter and it generalises across the whole of colonial history.** A chief's authority came from **leading his own people**. The moment it came from **a British salary**, the source of the authority changed — and so the authority itself dissolved. **Co-option destroyed the very thing it was trying to buy.** ⭐ *(The word to learn is **"stipendiary chief"** — a chief on a stipend, i.e. no longer a chief. Compare Theme 11's Gandhi coalition and Class 11 Theme 2's "empire by co-option": co-option works when the co-opted man's standing survives it, and fails when it does not.)*

**So, back to the opening puzzle:**

> *"As the pacification campaigns continued, the Paharias **withdrew deep into the mountains**, insulating themselves from hostile forces, and **carrying on a war with outsiders**. **So when Buchanan travelled through the region in the winter of 1810–11 the Paharias naturally viewed him with suspicion and distrust.** The experience of pacification campaigns and **memories of brutal repression** shaped their perception… **Every white man appeared to represent a power that was destroying their way of life and means of survival, snatching away their control over their forests and lands.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The answer to page one arrives on page six.** The villagers who "absconded" were reading Buchanan correctly. **He WAS an agent of the power that had hunted them.** ⭐ **Write it like this in an answer:** *the hostility recorded in the source is not a fact about the Paharias' character — it is a fact about what had been done to them, preserved in the record of the people who did it.*

#### 🖼️ Two paintings by William Hodges — the picturesque as a political act ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Fig. 9.8, *A view of a hill village in Rajmahal*, 1782** and **Fig. 9.9, *A view of Jangal territory*.**

| Fact NCERT gives | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Hodges was *"a British artist who **accompanied Captain Cook on his second voyage to the Pacific (1772–75)**, and then came to India"* | ⭐ He is a **voyage-of-discovery artist** — trained to render the unfamiliar for a home audience |
| *"In **1781** he became a **friend of Augustus Cleveland**, the Collector of Bhagalpur. On the invitation of Cleveland, Hodges accompanied him to the **Jangal Mahals in 1782**, and painted a set of **aquatints**"* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **He was the guest of the pacification officer.** The painter of the idyll travelled with the man running the campaign |
| Artists in search of **the picturesque** *"were inspired by the ideals of **Romanticism**, a tradition of thought that **celebrated nature and admired its magnificence and power**"* — nature as *"an **idyll, uncorrupted by modern civilisation**"*, discovering unknown landscapes, appreciating *"the **sublime play of light and shade**"* | The convention dictates what can appear |
| *"He found **flat landscapes monotonous**, and discovered **beauty in roughness, irregularity and variety**"* | The Rajmahal hills were chosen because they *fit a style* |
| ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**A landscape that colonial officials found DANGEROUS AND WILD, peopled by TURBULENT TRIBES, appears in the paintings of Hodges as EXOTIC AND IDYLLIC.**"* | **The same place, two incompatible official representations, produced by the same establishment in the same years** |
| Fig. 9.9: the hills are *"nowhere actually **above 2,000 feet**"*, but *"**by centring the hills and viewing them from below, Hodges emphasises their inaccessibility**"* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **A measurable fact and a contrary impression, in one image** |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Fig. 9.9 is the best single teaching example in the chapter, and the reason is the number.** *Nowhere above 2,000 feet* — these are **hills, not mountains**. But choose a low viewpoint, centre the ridge, and they read as impassable. **No fact has been falsified; a viewpoint has been chosen.** ⭐ **That is precisely how the Fifth Report worked in § 1.6 and how the Deccan Riots Report will work in § 4** — no lies, just a selected vantage. **A picture and a parliamentary report can be misleading by exactly the same technique.**
>
> 💡 **And hold the contradiction together, because that is the mark:** to the **administrator** the hills were dangerous and wild (justifying extermination); to the **artist** they were exotic and idyllic (delighting London). **Both images served the same empire.** One licensed the violence; the other made the country charming to the public that paid for it. ⭐ *(Beyond NCERT: **aquatint**, as NCERT defines it, is *"a picture produced by **cutting into a copper sheet with acid** and then printing it"* — a **reproducible** medium. That matters: these images were made to **circulate in Britain**, exactly like the *Illustrated London News* engravings in § 2.2.)*

### 🪓 2.2 The Santhals: pioneer settlers ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Buchanan's own moment of arrival, end of 1810** — he crossed **Ganjuria Pahar**, part of the Rajmahal ranges, and reached an old village whose surrounding land *"had been **recently cleared to extend cultivation**"*:

> *"Buchanan found evidence of the region having been transformed through **'proper application of human labour'**. He wrote: **'Gunjuriya is just sufficiently cultivated to SHOW WHAT A GLORIOUS COUNTRY THIS MIGHT BE MADE. I think its beauty and riches might be made equal to ALMOST ANY IN THE UNIVERSE.'**"* The soil was rocky but *"**uncommonly fine**"*, and **nowhere had Buchanan seen finer tobacco and mustard.**

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Every verb in that quotation is in the conditional: "might be made", "might be made".** Buchanan is not describing a place — **he is appraising a place against what it could yield.** *(Hold this; § 2.3 and Source 5 turn it into the chapter's whole argument about him.)*

**How the Santhals got there — the sequence matters:**

```
   1780s   Santhals "begin to come into Bengal"
             * ZAMINDARS HIRED THEM to reclaim land and expand
               cultivation
             * BRITISH OFFICIALS INVITED THEM to settle in the
               JANGAL MAHALS
                    |
   c.1800  Santhals move into the lower Rajmahal slopes,
           "DISPLACED THE HILL FOLK who lived on these lower
            slopes, cleared the forests and settled the land"
                    |
   1832    ** DAMIN-I-KOH demarcated ** -- "declared to be the
           land of the Santhals"
                    |
   1838    40 Santhal villages        3,000 people
   1851    1,473 Santhal villages     over 82,000 people
                    |
   1855-56 ** THE SANTHAL REVOLT **
                    |
   after   ** SANTHAL PARGANA created ** -- 5,500 square miles
           carved out of BHAGALPUR and BIRBHUM districts
```

**⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Why the British switched from Paharias to Santhals — this is a direct exam question (NCERT Exercise 7):**

> *"**Having FAILED to subdue the Paharias and transform them into settled agriculturists, the British turned to the Santhals.** The **Paharias refused to cut forests, resisted touching the plough, and continued to be turbulent.** The Santhals, by contrast, **appeared to be IDEAL SETTLERS, clearing forests and ploughing the land with vigour.**"*

**The terms of the Damin-i-Koh grant — learn all five, they are Prelims-shaped:**

| # | Term | NCERT's words |
|:--:|---|---|
| 1 | **A defined territory** | *"a large area of land was demarcated as **Damin-i-Koh**"*, *"declared to be **the land of the Santhals**"* |
| 2 | **A residence condition** | *"They were to **live within it**"* |
| 3 | **A method condition** | *"**practise plough agriculture**, and become **settled peasants**"* |
| 4 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **A performance condition** | *"at least **ONE-TENTH of the area was to be CLEARED AND CULTIVATED within the FIRST TEN YEARS**"* |
| 5 | **A visible boundary** | *"The territory was **surveyed and mapped**. **Enclosed with BOUNDARY PILLARS**, it was **separated from both** the world of the settled agriculturists of the plains **and** the Paharias of the hills"* |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Condition 4 is the giveaway and few candidates spot it.** A grant of land that comes with **a clearing quota and a deadline** is not a gift to a people — **it is a land-development contract with a forest-clearance target**, and the Santhals are the contractors. ⭐ **The state's interest is stated plainly by NCERT two lines later: *"As cultivation expanded, an INCREASED VOLUME OF REVENUE FLOWED INTO THE COMPANY'S COFFERS."*** **That is what the Damin was for.**
>
> 💡 **And condition 5 has a cost the chapter makes you calculate.** The pillars separated the Santhals from the plains *and* from the hills — which sounds protective. **But a boundary that keeps others out also keeps you in**, and the Paharias, on the wrong side of it, lost access to their best soils. **A map with pillars on it is an instrument, not a description.**

**The Santhals' own memory of the journey — worth quoting, it is a rare non-official source:**

> *"**Santhal myths and songs of the nineteenth century** refer very frequently to **a long history of travel**: they represent the Santhal past as one of **continuous mobility, a tireless search for a place to settle**. **Here in the Damin-i-Koh their journey seemed to have come to an end.**"*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Note what NCERT has just done: after five pages of revenue records and a Company surgeon's journal, it reaches for MYTHS AND SONGS** — because that is the only place the Santhals speak in their own voice. *"Seemed to have come to an end"* is doing quiet, devastating work: **it did not.**

#### ⛰️ What it cost the Paharias — the mechanism, not just the outcome ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"the Paharias **resisted but were ultimately forced to withdraw deeper into the hills**. Restricted from moving down to the lower hills and valleys, they were **confined to the DRY INTERIOR and to the MORE BARREN AND ROCKY UPPER HILLS**. This severely affected their lives, **impoverishing them in the long term**."*

**And now the precise reason — this is a beautiful piece of explanation and it is very examinable:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Shifting agriculture DEPENDED ON THE ABILITY TO MOVE to newer and newer land and utilisation of the natural fertility of the soil. When the most fertile soils became inaccessible to them, being part of the Damin, the Paharias could not effectively sustain their mode of cultivation.**"* And *"when the forests of the region were cleared for cultivation the **hunters** amongst them also faced problems."*

```
   WHY CONFINEMENT DESTROYS SHIFTING CULTIVATION
   -- and settled agriculture survives it

   SHIFTING CULTIVATION is a system that BANKS FERTILITY
   IN SPACE. You use a patch, exhaust it, leave it to
   recover, and live off the next one while it does.
        |
        |  Its capital is NOT the field.
        |  Its capital is ** ACCESS TO THE NEXT FIELD. **
        v
   FENCE IT IN, and the system does not shrink --
   ** IT STOPS WORKING. **
   You return to a patch before it has recovered, yields
   fall, and they fall FURTHER every cycle.

   ** THIS IS WHY THE PAHARIAS WERE IMPOVERISHED RATHER
      THAN MERELY REDUCED. The Damin did not take a
      share of their land. It took the MOBILITY that made
      their land productive at all. **
```

> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is exactly Jaipal Singh's charge in the Constituent Assembly** — *"dispossessed of the land they had settled, **deprived of their forests and pastures**, and **forced to move in search of new homes**"* (see [Theme 12 § 3.2](12_Theme12_Framing_the_Constitution.md)). **Theme 9 is the documentation for the accusation Theme 12 records.** Use the pair.

#### 🔥 And then the Santhals lost it too ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The Santhals *"gave up their earlier life of mobility and settled down, **cultivating a range of COMMERCIAL CROPS FOR THE MARKET**, and **dealing with traders and moneylenders**."*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That sentence is the trap door.** Entering the market meant entering **credit** — and NCERT immediately delivers the consequence:

> *"The Santhals, however, soon found that **the land they had brought under cultivation was SLIPPING AWAY FROM THEIR HANDS**:*
> - *the **STATE was levying HEAVY TAXES** on the land that the Santhals had cleared,*
> - *⭐ **MONEYLENDERS (DIKUS) were charging them HIGH RATES OF INTEREST and TAKING OVER THE LAND when debts remained unpaid**,*
> - *and **ZAMINDARS were asserting control over the Damin area**."*

> *"By the 1850s, the Santhals felt that the time had come to **rebel against zamindars, moneylenders and the colonial state**, in order to **create an IDEAL WORLD for themselves WHERE THEY WOULD RULE**."*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THREE enemies, and you must name all three** — *zamindars, moneylenders, AND the colonial state.* This is NCERT Exercise 4 (*"Why did the Santhals rebel against British rule?"*) and the commonest error is to answer with only the moneylender. ⭐ **The word to keep is *DIKU*** — the Santhal term for the outsider/moneylender, and the fact that they had **a word of their own for the category** tells you how completely it had entered their world.
>
> 🎯 **And note the aim: not relief, not lower rates — *"an ideal world for themselves WHERE THEY WOULD RULE."* That is a claim to sovereignty, not a petition.** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Compare it directly with the Deccan ryots of § 3, who burnt bonds but demanded no state at all.** *Two peasant revolts, twenty years apart, with completely different political horizons* — that comparison is worth a full paragraph in any Mains answer on nineteenth-century agrarian resistance.

**The settlement afterwards:**

> *"It was **after the Santhal Revolt (1855–56)** that the **SANTHAL PARGANA** was created, carving out **5,500 square miles** from the districts of **Bhagalpur and Birbhum**. The colonial state hoped that **by creating a new territory for the Santhals and imposing some SPECIAL LAWS within it, the Santhals could be CONCILIATED.**"*

> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Notice the pattern repeating: the answer to a problem caused by a demarcated territory (the Damin) was… another demarcated territory (the Pargana).** *(Beyond NCERT, and genuinely useful: this is the ancestor of the **Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act**, which restricted the transfer of tribal land to outsiders — and of the whole family of protective tribal-land laws, right down to the **Fifth Schedule** of the Constitution. ⭐ **And Ranga's speech in Theme 12 tells you how well such laws worked in practice: *"According to law, their own traditional law, their tribal law, THEIR LANDS CANNOT BE ALIENATED. Yet our merchants go there, and in the so-called free market they are able to SNATCH THEIR LANDS."*** The protective law arrives in 1855 and Ranga is still complaining about its failure in 1946.)*
>
> ⚠️ **Do not add the leaders' names from memory unless you are sure.** NCERT names only **Sidhu Manjhi** (Fig. 9.11, *"the leader of the Santhal rebellion"*). *(Beyond NCERT: the revolt is conventionally associated with the brothers **Sidhu and Kanhu**, and is often called the **Hul**. Stop there.)*

#### 📰 Figs. 9.12–9.14 — how a rebellion was sold to the British public ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

All three are from the ***Illustrated London News***, and NCERT prints them as a **set with a before-and-after**:

| Figure | What it shows | NCERT's own comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Fig. 9.10** *(23 Feb 1856; sketched by **Walter Sherwill** in the early 1850s)* | A hill village in Santhal country | *"The village appears to be **peaceful, calm and idyllic**. Life seems **unaffected by the outside world**."* |
| **Fig. 9.12** *(23 Feb 1856)* | *Santhals fight the sepoys of the British Raj* | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**The rebellion CHANGED THE BRITISH PERCEPTION of the Santhals. Villages that had earlier seemed calm and peaceful now appeared to have become places of VIOLENT AND SAVAGE DEEDS.**"* |
| **Fig. 9.13** *(23 Feb 1856)* | *Burning of Santhal villages* | *"After the rebellion was crushed, **the region was searched, suspects were picked up, and villages set on fire**. Images of the burning villages were **shown to the public in England — once again as a DEMONSTRATION OF THE MIGHT OF THE BRITISH and their ability to crush rebellion and impose colonial order**."* |
| **Fig. 9.14** *(1856)* | *Santhal prisoners being taken away* | *"**Notice how images like this one SEEK TO CONVEY POLITICAL MESSAGES.** At the centre… **British officials triumphantly riding on an elephant**. One officer on a horse **smoking a hookah**: a picture that emphasises that **the time of trouble was over**… The rebels are now **in chains**, escorted and surrounded by soldiers of the Company."* |

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Figs. 9.10 and 9.12 appeared in the SAME ISSUE of the same magazine — 23 February 1856.** The idyllic village and the savage rebels, on the same day, for the same readers. ⭐ **The Santhals had not changed between the two pictures. The relationship had.** *(Note also that Fig. 9.10 was **sketched in the early 1850s** and published in 1856 — the peaceful image is genuinely older; it is the **caption and the context** that repurpose it.)*
>
> 💡 **The hookah in Fig. 9.14 deserves its own sentence in an answer.** A man smoking on horseback is **at leisure** — and leisure signals *safety*. **The picture is not reporting that the rebellion was crushed; it is performing the crushing as an accomplished, relaxed fact.** ⭐ **That is a visual argument, and reading it is a Class 12 skill.**
>
> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **These three images are the direct rehearsal for [Theme 10 § 5](10_Theme10_Rebels_and_the_Raj_1857.md), where British visual representations of 1857 get a whole section to themselves.** NCERT teaches the technique here on a small revolt and then applies it to a large one. **Read the two sections together.**
>
> 💬 **NCERT's margin question — *"Imagine you are a reader of the Illustrated London News in England. How will you react to the images in Figs. 9.12, 9.13 and 9.14? What image of the Santhals would these pictures create in your mind?"*** → ✅ **Answer in the reader's voice, then step back.** As a London reader in 1856 you would see: *treacherous tribesmen attacking disciplined troops; villages justly burnt; order restored by calm, mounted gentlemen.* You would conclude that **the violence began with the Santhals and ended with the British.** ⭐ **What the pictures cannot show you is the sequence that Section 2.2 gives: heavy taxes, dikus taking land, zamindars encroaching on the Damin. The images begin the story at the moment of the attack** — and a story that begins at the attack can only have one villain.

### 🧭 2.3 The accounts of Buchanan — reading the surveyor ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"We have been drawing on Buchanan's account, but while reading his reports **we should not forget that he was AN EMPLOYEE OF THE BRITISH EAST INDIA COMPANY**. His journeys were **not simply inspired by the love of landscape and the desire to discover the unknown**."*

**The evidence NCERT gives that he was an instrument, not a traveller:**

```
   WHAT BUCHANAN ACTUALLY WAS

   HE DID NOT TRAVEL ALONE
     "He marched everywhere with a LARGE ARMY OF PEOPLE --
      DRAUGHTSMEN, SURVEYORS, PALANQUIN BEARERS, COOLIES."

   HE DID NOT PAY
     "The costs of the travels were BORNE BY THE EAST INDIA
      COMPANY since IT NEEDED THE INFORMATION that Buchanan
      was expected to collect."

   HE DID NOT CHOOSE WHAT TO RECORD
     "Buchanan had SPECIFIC INSTRUCTIONS about WHAT HE HAD TO
      LOOK FOR and WHAT HE HAD TO RECORD."

   AND THE VILLAGERS UNDERSTOOD ALL THREE AT A GLANCE
     "When he arrived at a village with his army of people, he
      was IMMEDIATELY PERCEIVED AS ** AN AGENT OF THE SARKAR. **"

   ** So the flight of the villagers on page one was not
      superstition. It was an ACCURATE READING of a column
      of surveyors arriving at public expense. **
```

**Why the Company wanted this at all:**

> *"As the Company **consolidated its power and expanded its commerce**, it **looked for NATURAL RESOURCES IT COULD CONTROL AND EXPLOIT**. It **surveyed landscapes and revenue sources, organised voyages of discovery, and sent its geologists and geographers, its botanists and medical men to collect information.** Buchanan, undoubtedly **an extraordinary observer**, was one such individual."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Note that NCERT calls him "undoubtedly an extraordinary observer" — it is not dismissing him.** The critique is not that he was a bad recorder; **it is that he was an excellent recorder of a particular set of things.** *(Same move as § 1.6 on the Fifth Report: the data is real, the frame is interested.)*

**What he obsessively recorded:**

> *"Everywhere Buchanan went, he **obsessively observed the stones and rocks and the different strata and layers of soil**. He **searched for minerals and stones that were COMMERCIALLY VALUABLE**, he recorded all signs of **IRON ORE and MICA, GRANITE and SALTPETRE**. He carefully observed **local practices of SALT-MAKING and IRON-ORE-MINING**."*

⭐ **Learn the four minerals as a set — iron ore, mica, granite, saltpetre — plus the two local industries he studied, salt-making and iron-ore-mining.** *(Saltpetre matters more than it looks: it is the key ingredient of **gunpowder**, and Bihar saltpetre was a major Company export.)*

**And the decisive sentence about his method ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

> *"When Buchanan wrote about a landscape, he most often described **not just what he saw, what the landscape WAS like, but also HOW IT COULD BE TRANSFORMED AND MADE MORE PRODUCTIVE** — what crops could be cultivated, which trees cut down, and which ones grown. And we must remember that **his vision and his priorities were DIFFERENT FROM THOSE OF THE LOCAL INHABITANTS**: his assessment of what was necessary was **shaped by the COMMERCIAL CONCERNS OF THE COMPANY and MODERN WESTERN NOTIONS OF WHAT CONSTITUTED PROGRESS**. **He was inevitably CRITICAL OF THE LIFESTYLES OF FOREST DWELLERS and felt that FORESTS HAD TO BE TURNED INTO AGRICULTURAL LANDS.**"*

#### 📜 The three Buchanan Sources — and what each one exposes

**Source 3 — *Buchanan on the Santhals*** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"They are **very clever in clearing new lands**, but **live meanly**. Their huts have **no fence**, and the walls are made of **small sticks placed upright, close together and plastered within with clay**. They are **small and slovenly**, and **too flat-roofed, with very little arch**."*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Read the structure of that judgement: one clause of praise, then four of contempt — and the praise is for the thing the Company wanted.** *"Very clever in clearing new lands"* is an **assessment of utility**; *"live meanly… small and slovenly"* is an **assessment of worth**. ⭐ **And look at the criterion in the last clause — *"too flat-roofed, with very little arch."*** He is judging Santhal huts against **an architectural expectation they were never built to meet**. A flat roof in that climate is a *design*, not a *failure*. **This is what it looks like when a surveyor mistakes his own conventions for a standard.**

**Source 4 — *The rocks near Kaduya*** ⭐⭐⭐

> *"About a mile farther on, (I) came to a **low ledge of rocks without any evident strata**; it is a **small grained granite with reddish feldspar, with quartz and black mica** … More than half a mile from thence, I came to another rock **not stratified**, and consisting of **very fine-grained granite with yellowish feldspar, whitish quartz and black mica**."*

> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NCERT's introduction to this Source is the point: *"Buchanan's journal is PACKED with observations like the following."*** Pages of granite and feldspar. ⭐ **Set Source 4 beside Source 3 and the imbalance is the finding: the rocks get precise, patient, repeated measurement; the people get four adjectives.** **What a survey counts carefully tells you what it was sent to find.** *(That single comparison is worth a mark in any question on official archives, and it is the kind of observation almost nobody makes.)*

**Source 5 — *On clearance and settled cultivation*** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The view of the country is **exceedingly fine**, the cultivation, especially the narrow valleys of rice winding in all directions, the cleared lands with scattered trees, and the rocky hills are **in perfection**; **ALL THAT IS WANTED is some appearance of PROGRESS in the area and a VASTLY EXTENDED AND IMPROVED CULTIVATION, of which the country is highly susceptible.** Plantations of **Asan and Palas, for Tessar (Tassar silk worms) and Lac**, should **occupy the place of woods** to as great an extent as the demand will admit; **THE REMAINDER MIGHT BE ALL CLEARED**, and the greater part cultivated, while what is not fit for the purpose, might rear **Plamira (palmyra) and Mowa (mahua)**."*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the Source to know cold, and here is why it is devastating.** Buchanan calls the country *"in perfection"* — and then, in the same breath, says *"all that is wanted"* is that it be **almost entirely cleared**. **Perfection that requires the removal of the forest is not an aesthetic judgement; it is a development plan.**
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And now put it against § 2.1's inventory of Paharia life.** Buchanan proposes replacing the woods with **plantations of Asan and Palas for tassar silk and lac**, and rearing **palmyra and mahua** on the leftovers. **Mahua is the flower the Paharias ate. Silk cocoons and resin were what they sold.** ⭐ **He is proposing to keep the FOREST'S PRODUCTS and abolish the FOREST — to convert a system that fed a people into a system that yields commodities.** *That sentence, written out in an answer, is the whole of Section 2 in one move.*
>
> 💬 **NCERT's "Discuss…" box — *"What does Buchanan's description tell us about his ideas of development? Illustrate your argument by quoting from the excerpts. If you were a Paharia forest dweller how would you have reacted to these ideas?"*** → ✅ **Part 1 (his ideas):** development = **land under the plough**, forests as **unrealised assets**, value measured by **what the market will take** (*"to as great an extent as **the demand will admit**"*), and human worth measured by **willingness to clear and plough** (Source 3: *"very clever in clearing new lands, but live meanly"*). ⭐ **Part 2 (as a Paharia):** *the "waste" he proposes to clear is my food, my pasture, my cash income and my defence; the mobility he calls backwardness is the thing that keeps my soil fertile; and the "progress" he wants would leave me on the barren upper hills — which is exactly what happened.* **Answer part 2 in the first person; NCERT is inviting you to, and it forces you to use the § 2.2 evidence.**

## 3️⃣ A Revolt in the Countryside — the Bombay Deccan

> **NCERT's transition:** *"You have read about how the lives of peasants and zamindars of **colonial Bengal** and the **Paharias and Santhals** of the Rajmahal hills were changing. Now let us move across to **western India**, and to a **later period**."*

### 🔑 Why the chapter studies a REVOLT — the methodological hinge ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**This passage is the intellectual centre of the entire chapter. If you learn one paragraph, learn this one.**

> *"One way of exploring such changes is by **focusing on a peasant revolt**. In such climactic times **rebels express their anger and fury**; they rise against **what they perceive to be injustice**… If we try to understand the premises of their resentment, and **peel the layers of their anger**, we get a glimpse of their life and experience **that is otherwise HIDDEN FROM US**."*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Revolts also PRODUCE RECORDS that historians can look at.** Alarmed by the actions of rebels and keen on restoring order, **state authorities DO NOT SIMPLY REPRESS a rebellion. They try and understand it, ENQUIRE INTO ITS CAUSES so that policies can be formulated and peace established. THESE ENQUIRIES PRODUCE EVIDENCE THAT HISTORIANS CAN EXPLORE.**"*

```
   THE ARGUMENT, AND WHY IT IS UNCOMFORTABLE

   Ordinary peasants do not write. They leave no diaries,
   no letters, no memoirs. For most years of most centuries
   they are simply ABSENT from the record.
        |
        v
   Then they REBEL.
        |
        v
   And the state, frightened, does something it never
   does in a quiet year: it goes and ASKS THEM WHY.
   It records their statements. It collects their petitions.
   It tabulates interest rates and prices and revenue.
        |
        v
   ** SO THE ARCHIVE OF PEASANT LIFE EXISTS BECAUSE OF
      THE VIOLENCE. THE DECCAN RIOTS REPORT IS THE BEST
      SOURCE WE HAVE ON THESE RYOTS -- AND IT WOULD NOT
      EXIST IF THEY HAD SUFFERED QUIETLY. **
        |
        v
   AND THE STING IN THE TAIL (Section 4):
   the enquiry was commissioned to answer a question the
   government had already chosen -- so the very record
   that lets the ryot speak is also the record that was
   built to acquit the state.
```

> 🎯 **Deploy this in Mains like so:** *"Our knowledge of the Deccan ryot is an artefact of his rebellion. The colonial state investigated peasants only when they became dangerous, and framed the investigation around the question it wished answered. The historian therefore inherits a rich source with a built-in verdict."* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That formulation answers NCERT Exercise 9 (*"What are the problems of using official sources in writing about the history of peasants?"*) almost by itself.**

### 🔥 3.1 Account books are burnt ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Through the nineteenth century, peasants in various parts of India **rose in revolt against moneylenders and grain dealers**. One such revolt occurred in **1875 in the Deccan**."*

```
   THE DECCAN RIOTS, 1875 -- the facts to memorise

   WHERE IT STARTED   ** SUPA **, "a large village in POONA
                      (present-day Pune) district"
                      -- "a MARKET CENTRE where many
                         SHOPKEEPERS AND MONEYLENDERS lived"

   WHEN               ** 12 MAY 1875 **

   WHAT THEY DID      ryots from surrounding rural areas
                      "gathered and attacked the shopkeepers,
                       ** DEMANDING THEIR BAHI KHATAS
                          (ACCOUNT BOOKS) AND DEBT BONDS. **
                       They BURNT THE KHATAS, LOOTED GRAIN
                       SHOPS, and in some cases SET FIRE TO
                       THE HOUSES OF SAHUKARS."

   HOW IT SPREAD      Poona -> ** AHMEDNAGAR **
                      then "over the next TWO MONTHS ...
                      over an area of ** 6,500 SQUARE KM **"
                      ** MORE THAN THIRTY VILLAGES ** affected

   THE PATTERN        "Everywhere the pattern was THE SAME:
                      sahukars attacked, ACCOUNT BOOKS BURNT
                      and DEBT BONDS DESTROYED."
                      Sahukars fled, "very often leaving their
                      property and belongings behind"

   THE RESPONSE       ** "British officials saw the SPECTRE
                         OF 1857." **
                      Police posts established in villages
                      "to frighten rebellious peasants into
                       submission"; troops called in;
                      ** 951 PEOPLE ARRESTED **, many convicted
                      -- and "it took SEVERAL MONTHS to bring
                         the countryside under control"
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The single most important fact about this revolt is WHAT THEY WENT FOR.** Not the grain, not the money, not the moneylender's life — **the paperwork.** They demanded the **bahi khatas and the debt bonds** and **burnt them in the street**. ⭐ **A crowd that burns account books is making a precise legal argument: the debt exists because the document exists. Destroy the document and the debt is unprovable.**
>
> 🎯 **And that is the second half of the chapter's thesis about archives.** Section 1 taught you that the record is written by the interested party. **Section 3 shows you peasants who understood that perfectly well — and attacked the record itself.** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The ryots of the Deccan grasped what this chapter is teaching: in a colonial economy, the document was the instrument of domination. That is why they burnt the ledgers and left the grain."* **Write that sentence and you have understood Theme 9.**
>
> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"British officials saw the spectre of 1857" is the link to [Theme 10](10_Theme10_Rebels_and_the_Raj_1857.md), and it is causally important, not decorative.** 1857 is **eighteen years** in the past. The memory of it is what turns a local riot into an emergency, brings in troops, and — as § 4 will show — **is the specific reason the Government of India forces a reluctant Bombay government to hold an enquiry.** ⭐ **No 1857, no Deccan Riots Report, no chapter.**

#### 📜 Source 6 — *"On that day in Supa"* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**The District Magistrate of Poona to the Police Commissioner, 16 May 1875:**

> *"On arrival at Supa on Saturday 15 May I learnt of the disturbance. **One house of a moneylender was burnt down; about a dozen were forcibly broken into and completely gutted of their content. ACCOUNT PAPERS, BONDS, GRAINS, COUNTRY CLOTH were burnt in the street where HEAPS OF ASHES ARE STILL TO BE SEEN.** The chief constable apprehended **50 persons**. Stolen property worth **Rs 2,000** was recovered. **The estimated loss is over Rs 25,000. Moneylenders claim it is over 1 LAKH.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Do not skip past the last two sentences — they are a source-criticism exercise NCERT has left lying on the page.** The official estimate is **Rs 25,000**. The moneylenders' claim is **over Rs 1,00,000** — **four times higher.** ⭐ **And the Magistrate reports both**, side by side, without adjudicating.
>
> **Three things to take from that gap:**
> **(1)** The moneylenders had **every incentive to inflate** — compensation, and a case for repression. **(2)** The official had **some incentive to deflate** — a smaller loss is a smaller failure of order in his district. **(3)** ⭐ **Most importantly: the moneylenders' figure is a claim about the value of BURNT PAPER.** Grain and cloth can be priced. What made the loss enormous in their eyes was **the destruction of the bonds** — i.e. of debts they could no longer prove. **The 4× gap is itself evidence of how much of a sahukar's wealth lived in his documents.**
>
> 💡 **And note the detail that dates the document — *"heaps of ashes are still to be seen"*.** He is writing **three days after** the event, from the spot. **This is a first-hand administrative record, and its evidentiary strength is exactly that; its weakness is that it counts what an administrator counts.**

> 🔑 **NCERT's definition, worth memorising exactly:** *"A **SAHUKAR** was someone who acted as **BOTH A MONEYLENDER AND A TRADER**."* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That double role is the engine of the whole section** — the man who lends you money is the same man who buys your crop and sells you your cloth, so he sets **both** the interest you pay **and** the price you get. *(See Source 8, where the ryot complains of being charged 25–50% above cash prices by the very man he owes.)*

#### 📰 Source 7 — a newspaper, and a lesson in reading tone ⭐⭐⭐⭐

*"The ryot and the moneylender"*, ***Native Opinion*, 6 June 1876**, quoted in the ***Report of the Native Newspapers of Bombay***:

> *"**They (the ryots) first place SPIES on the boundaries of their villages** to see if any Government officers come, and **to give timely intimation of their arrival to THE OFFENDERS**. They then **assemble in a body** and go to the houses of **their creditors**, and **demand from them a surrender of their bonds and other documents**, and **threaten them in case of refusal with assault and plunder**. If any Government officer happens to approach… the spies give intimation to **the offenders** and **the latter DISPERSE IN TIME**."*

> 💬 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NCERT's question is about prejudice, and it is easy marks if you look at the nouns.** *"The words and terms used by a writer often tell us something about his or her prejudices… pick out the terms that indicate any prejudices of the writer. Discuss how a ryot of the area would have described the same situation."*
>
> ✅ **The loaded words:** ⭐ **"OFFENDERS"** — used **twice**, and it convicts them in advance; the report has decided the legal question before describing the act. ⭐ **"SPIES"** — a word for covert malice; *lookouts* or *watchmen* would describe the identical behaviour neutrally. ⭐ **"CREDITORS"** — a neutral commercial term for the sahukar, which quietly grants that the debts are legitimate, exactly the point in dispute. ⭐ **"THREATEN… ASSAULT AND PLUNDER"** — the ryots' action is criminal; **nothing whatever is said about how the bonds came to exist.** ⭐ **"ASSEMBLE IN A BODY"** — conspiracy, not a village meeting.
>
> ✅ **The same events in a ryot's words:** *"We kept watch at the village boundary so that we would not be surprised. We went together — a man alone is arrested, a village is heard — to the houses of the men who hold our forged bonds, and demanded the papers by which they claim what we have already paid. We asked first. And when the sarkar's men came, we went home, because the sarkar has always come for us and never for them."*
>
> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The deeper point, and the one worth making explicitly: this is *Native Opinion*, an INDIAN-OWNED newspaper — and it sides with the creditors.** ⭐ **"Indian" is not the same as "sympathetic to the peasant."** The Indian-language press of Bombay was read and written largely by **urban, literate, propertied men**, and on a question of debt and property their interests lay with the sahukar. **The colonial archive is not the only interested archive.** *(Note too that we are reading it inside the ***Report of the Native Newspapers*** — a government compilation. **So this is an Indian newspaper, filtered by a colonial surveillance digest.** Two frames, stacked. Compare Theme 11 § 6.4, where the same doubling appears with Gandhi and the press.)*

### 📐 3.2 A new revenue system — and why Bengal's model was NOT repeated ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"As British rule expanded from Bengal to other parts of India, new systems of revenue were imposed. **The Permanent Settlement was RARELY EXTENDED to any region beyond Bengal.**"*

**Reason 1 — the arithmetic of a fixed demand in a rising market:**

> *"after **1810, agricultural prices ROSE**, increasing the value of harvest produce, and **enlarging the income of the Bengal zamindars**. **Since the revenue demand was FIXED under the Permanent Settlement, the colonial state COULD NOT CLAIM ANY SHARE of this enhanced income.** Keen on expanding its financial resources… in territories annexed in the nineteenth century, **TEMPORARY revenue settlements were made.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the beautiful symmetry of the chapter, and you should state it explicitly.** In **§ 1.3** the Permanent Settlement failed the **zamindars**, because the demand stayed high when prices **fell** in the 1790s. Now, after 1810, prices **rose** — and the same fixed demand failed the **state**. ⭐ **A permanently fixed sum is a bet on prices, and BOTH parties lost the bet, in opposite directions, twenty years apart.** **That single observation explains why the Permanent Settlement was never repeated, and it is worth a mark on its own.**

**Reason 2 — an economic theory learnt at college ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

> *"When officials devise policies, **their thinking is deeply shaped by economic theories they are familiar with.** By the **1820s**, the economist **DAVID RICARDO** was a celebrated figure in England. **Colonial officials had learnt Ricardian ideas during their COLLEGE YEARS.** In Maharashtra when British officials set about formulating the terms of the early settlement **in the 1820s**, they operated with some of these ideas."*

```
   THE RICARDIAN ARGUMENT, as NCERT states it

   1. A landowner should have a claim only to
      ** "THE AVERAGE RENT" ** prevailing at a given time.

   2. When the land yields MORE than this average rent,
      the landowner has ** A SURPLUS THAT THE STATE
      NEEDS TO TAX. **

   3. WHY tax it? Because if it is NOT taxed,
      "cultivators were likely to turn into ** RENTIERS **,
       and their surplus income was ** UNLIKELY TO BE
       PRODUCTIVELY INVESTED IN THE IMPROVEMENT OF THE
       LAND. **"

   4. AND THE PROOF THEY THOUGHT THEY HAD:
      "Many British officials in India thought that
       ** THE HISTORY OF BENGAL CONFIRMED RICARDO'S THEORY.
       There the zamindars seemed to have TURNED INTO
       RENTIERS, LEASING OUT LAND AND LIVING ON THE
       RENTAL INCOMES. **"

   -> "It was therefore necessary, the British officials
       now felt, to have A DIFFERENT SYSTEM."

   ** RENTIER: NCERT's own gloss -- "a term used to designate
      people who LIVE ON RENTAL INCOME FROM PROPERTY." **
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The examinable insight is the loop between the two reasons, and it is subtle.** The British had **created** the Bengal zamindar-rentier by fixing his demand for ever. They then **read Bengal as EVIDENCE for Ricardo's theory** about rentiers. And they used that theory to justify a harsher, resurveyed system elsewhere. ⭐ **A policy failure was reclassified as a confirmed economic law — and the law was then used to authorise the next policy.** *That is how theory and administration actually interact, and NCERT hands it to you in two paragraphs.*
>
> 💡 **And keep NCERT's opening sentence — *"When officials devise policies, their thinking is deeply shaped by economic theories they are familiar with"* — for essays and GS3.** It is a general claim about how states act, and it is quotable well beyond this chapter.

**The system itself — the RYOTWARI settlement ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

| Feature | The Bombay Deccan **ryotwari** | Bengal's **Permanent Settlement** |
|---|---|---|
| **Settled with whom** | ⭐ **directly with the RYOT** — *"Unlike the Bengal system, the revenue was **directly settled with the ryot**"* | the **zamindar** |
| **How assessed** | *"The **average income from different types of soil was estimated**, the **revenue-paying capacity of the ryot was assessed**, and **a proportion of it fixed as the share of the state**"* | a fixed sum on the estate |
| **For how long** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The lands were **RESURVEYED EVERY 30 YEARS** and the **revenue rates INCREASED**"* | **permanent** |
| **Therefore** | *"the revenue demand was **NO LONGER PERMANENT**"* | fixed for ever |

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Resurveyed every 30 years and the rates increased" is the sentence that sets up the entire crisis of § 3.5.** The revision is not a possibility — **it is a scheduled event, and NCERT tells you in advance that the rates go UP.** ⭐ **So when the second settlement lands in the 1860s "from 50 to 100 per cent" higher, at the exact moment the cotton boom collapses, that collision was built into the system's design thirty years earlier.** *Say that in an answer and you have shown you understand a system rather than a sequence.*
>
> 🔗 *(Beyond NCERT, and standard Prelims material — **know all three systems and their proponents**: **Permanent Settlement / zamindari**, Bengal, **Lord Cornwallis, 1793**; **RYOTWARI**, Madras and Bombay, associated with **Thomas Munro** and **Alexander Read**; **MAHALWARI**, the North-Western Provinces and Punjab, associated with **Holt Mackenzie** and later **R. M. Bird**, settled with the *mahal* or village body. ⭐ **NCERT's chapter covers only the first two — the third is very commonly asked, so learn it here.**)*

### 💰 3.3 Revenue demand and peasant debt ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The **first revenue settlement in the Bombay Deccan was made in the 1820s**. The revenue that was demanded was **SO HIGH that in many places PEASANTS DESERTED THEIR VILLAGES AND MIGRATED TO NEW REGIONS.**"*

**Why it bit hardest where it did, and how it was enforced:**

> *"In areas of **poor soil and fluctuating rainfall** the problem was particularly acute. When rains failed and harvests were poor, peasants found it **impossible to pay the revenue**. **However, the collectors in charge of revenue collection were KEEN ON DEMONSTRATING THEIR EFFICIENCY AND PLEASING THEIR SUPERIORS. So they went about extracting payment with UTMOST SEVERITY.** When someone failed to pay, **his crops were seized and A FINE WAS IMPOSED ON THE WHOLE VILLAGE.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Two mechanisms here that are worth naming, because they explain colonial administration generally.**
> **(1) The career incentive.** Severity was not a policy — **it was a promotion strategy.** The collector's audience was **upward**, not local, so his interest lay in a full remittance regardless of the harvest. ⭐ **A system that measures officials only by collection will produce cruelty without ever ordering it.** *(Compare Theme 11 § 6.3, where the police wrote what senior officials **wanted to believe**. Same upward-facing incentive, different document.)*
> **(2) The collective fine.** *"A fine was imposed on the whole village"* — **the village is made to police the individual.** It converts the state's collection problem into a quarrel among neighbours, and it is why revenue pressure fractured village solidarity rather than uniting it.

**The 1830s — the compound disaster ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

```
   WHAT HIT THE DECCAN IN ONE DECADE

   PRICES     "Prices of agricultural products FELL SHARPLY
              AFTER 1832 and did not recover FOR OVER A
              DECADE AND A HALF."
              -> "a further DECLINE IN PEASANTS' INCOME"

   FAMINE     ** THE FAMINE OF 1832-34 **
              * "ONE-THIRD of the CATTLE of the Deccan
                 were killed"

      A peasant without bullocks cannot plough, so the

```

> ⚠️ ⭐⭐⭐ **"Half the human population died" is NCERT's own figure and it is extraordinarily high.** Reproduce it **as NCERT's statement** — *"NCERT records that the famine of 1832–34 killed one-third of the Deccan's cattle and half its human population"* — rather than as a settled demographic fact. **Attributing a startling figure to its source is always safer than asserting it, and it is exactly the habit this chapter is teaching.**

**And then the sentence the whole section has been building to:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"How did cultivators live through such years? How did they pay the revenue, procure their consumption needs, purchase their ploughs and cattle, or **get their children married**? **INEVITABLY, THEY BORROWED. REVENUE COULD RARELY BE PAID WITHOUT A LOAN FROM A MONEYLENDER.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the causal claim at the heart of Theme 9, and it is the claim the Deccan Riots Commission would later deny.** **The debt was not a private failing. It was a REQUIREMENT OF THE REVENUE SYSTEM.** The state demanded a fixed cash sum on a fixed date; the harvest was neither fixed nor cash; **the moneylender bridged the gap, and the state relied on him to do it.** ⭐ **Hold on to this — in § 4 the Commission will conclude that the government demand was *not* the cause and the moneylender *was*, and NCERT's own narrative here is the refutation, written three pages earlier.**
>
> 💡 **And notice the item in NCERT's list that is not about production at all: *"get their children married"*.** ⭐ **Debt was incurred for the social obligations that make a life, not only for seed and cattle** — which is why indebtedness was near-universal rather than a mark of the improvident. **Mention it; it shows you read the sentence rather than the topic.**

**The spiral, in NCERT's own steps:**

> *"But **once a loan was taken, the ryot found it difficult to pay it back**. As debt mounted, and loans remained unpaid, **peasants' dependence on moneylenders INCREASED. They now needed loans EVEN TO BUY THEIR EVERYDAY NEEDS and meet their production expenditure.** By the **1840s**, officials were finding evidence of **alarming levels of peasant indebtedness EVERYWHERE.**"*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Trace the escalation of the *purpose* of borrowing — it is the measure of the collapse:** borrow to **pay revenue** → borrow to **buy ploughs, cattle, seed** (production) → borrow for **everyday needs** (subsistence). **When a household borrows to eat, it is no longer in debt; it is owned.**

**The false dawn ⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

> *"By the **mid-1840s** there were signs of an **economic recovery of sorts**. **Many British officials had begun to realise that the settlements of the 1820s HAD BEEN HARSH. The revenue demanded was EXORBITANT, the system RIGID, and the peasant economy ON THE VERGE OF COLLAPSE.** So the revenue demand was **moderated** to encourage peasants to expand cultivation. **After 1845 agricultural prices recovered steadily.** Cultivators were now **extending their acreage, moving into new areas, and transforming PASTURELAND into cultivated fields.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Two things not to miss here.**
> **(1) The admission is official.** *"Exorbitant… rigid… on the verge of collapse"* is **the British administration's own verdict on its own settlement** — which makes it the strongest possible evidence against the Commission's later exoneration of the state in § 4. **Quote the officials against the Commission.**
> **(2) Recovery deepened the dependence.** *"But to expand cultivation peasants needed **more ploughs and cattle**. They needed money to **buy seeds and land**. For all this they **had to turn ONCE AGAIN to moneylenders for loans**."* ⭐ **Both the bust and the boom ran on credit.** In bad years they borrowed to survive; in good years they borrowed to expand. **There was no state of the world in which the ryot was not in the sahukar's book** — and *that* is the structural point, not the individual misfortune.
>
> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐ **And note the quiet ecological line: *"transforming PASTURELAND into cultivated fields."*** The same frontier expansion as § 2, in another region — **grazing land converted to crop land.** *(The pastoralists displaced by exactly this process are the subject of **Class 9 Ch.5**.)*

### 📉 3.5 Credit dries up ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**The dream, quoted at its peak:**

> *"While the boom lasted, cotton merchants in India had visions of **capturing the world market in raw cotton, permanently displacing America**. The editor of the ***Bombay Gazette*** had asked in **1861**: **'What can prevent India from SUPPLANTING THE SLAVE STATES (of the U.S.A.) as THE FEEDER OF LANCASHIRE?'**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **That quotation repays a second look, and it is superb essay material.** India's cotton opportunity existed **because American slavery had gone to war with itself**. The Bombay editor's phrase — **"the feeder of Lancashire"** — describes the ambition exactly: **not to build an Indian industry, but to become a more reliable supplier of raw material to a British one.** ⭐ **The highest hope of Indian commerce in 1861 was to inherit the position of the slave states in the same supply chain.** *(Set this beside **Class 10 Ch.4** on Manchester and Indian weavers, and beside Theme 9's own opening about a countryside reorganised for export.)*

**The collapse:**

> *"**By 1865 these dreams were over.** As the **Civil War ended, cotton production in America revived** and **Indian cotton exports to Britain steadily declined.** Export merchants and sahukars in Maharashtra **were no longer keen on extending long-term credit**… So they decided to **CLOSE DOWN THEIR OPERATIONS, RESTRICT THEIR ADVANCES to peasants, and DEMAND REPAYMENT of outstanding debts.**"*

> 🎯 **And note the precise trigger, which most answers miss.** The ryots did not rebel when they **fell into debt** — they had been in debt for fifty years. **They rebelled when the credit was WITHDRAWN.** ⭐ **The moneylender's refusal is what ended the relationship, and it is what turned a grievance into a riot.** *(This is why they burnt the ledgers rather than killing the lenders: the bond was now a pure claim on them with nothing flowing back.)*

### ⚖️ 3.6 The experience of injustice ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**NCERT's most important psychological point in the chapter — and it is a distinction, so state it as one:**

> *"The refusal of moneylenders to extend loans **enraged** the ryots. **What infuriated them was NOT SIMPLY that they had got deeper and deeper into debt, or that they were utterly dependent on the moneylender for survival, BUT THAT MONEYLENDERS WERE BEING INSENSITIVE TO THEIR PLIGHT. THE MONEYLENDERS WERE VIOLATING THE CUSTOMARY NORMS OF THE COUNTRYSIDE.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is a claim about the MORAL ECONOMY of the village, and it is the difference between a good answer and an excellent one.** The ryots were not protesting **debt** — debt was old, and moneylenders had always been powerful. **They were protesting a BREACH OF THE RULES that had always governed debt.** ⭐ **Exploitation within custom is endured; exploitation that abandons custom is rebelled against.** *(The same logic drives the 1857 sepoys in [Theme 10](10_Theme10_Rebels_and_the_Raj_1857.md) — not new hardship, but the violation of understood limits.)*

**The norm that broke ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

> *"Moneylending was certainly **widespread before colonial rule** and moneylenders were **often powerful figures**. A variety of **customary norms regulated the relationship** between the moneylender and the ryot. **⭐ ONE GENERAL NORM WAS THAT THE INTEREST CHARGED COULD NOT BE MORE THAN THE PRINCIPAL.** This was meant to **limit the moneylender's exactions** and **defined what could be counted as 'FAIR INTEREST'**. **Under colonial rule this norm BROKE DOWN.** In one of the many cases investigated by the Deccan Riots Commission, **the moneylender had charged OVER Rs 2,000 AS INTEREST ON A LOAN OF Rs 100.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Memorise the norm — *interest may not exceed the principal* — and the violation — *Rs 2,000 interest on Rs 100*, i.e. TWENTY TIMES the ceiling.** This is the chapter's single most quotable pair of facts.
>
> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And ask WHY the norm collapsed, because that is the analytical half.** The customary ceiling was enforced by **the village** — by reputation, by the lender's need to go on living among his debtors, by the absence of any outside body that would help him seize more. **Colonial law replaced all of that with a court that would enforce whatever the bond said.** ⭐ **The moneylender no longer needed the village's approval; he needed a signature and a magistrate.** *(Beyond NCERT: the principle that interest should not exceed principal is the rule known as ***damdupat***, long recognised in Indian custom. Mention it only if you are confident; NCERT does not name it.)*

#### 📜 Source 8 — *A ryot petitions* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**From a ryot of the village of Mirajgaon, Taluka Karjat, to the Collector, Ahmednagar — in the records of the Deccan Riots Commission:**

> *"The **sowkars (sahukars)… have of late begun to oppress us**. As we **cannot earn enough to defray our household expenses**, we are **actually FORCED TO BEG of them** to provide us with **money, clothes and grain**, which we obtain from them **not without great difficulty**, nor **without their COMPELLING US TO ENTER INTO HARD CONDITIONS IN THE BOND**. Moreover the necessary **clothes and grain are NOT SOLD TO US AT CASH RATES. The prices asked from us are generally TWENTY-FIVE OR FIFTY PER CENT MORE than demanded from customers making ready money payments**… **The produce of our fields is ALSO TAKEN by the sowkars, who at the time of removing it ASSURE US THAT IT WILL BE CREDITED TO OUR ACCOUNT, BUT THEY DO NOT ACTUALLY MAKE ANY MENTION OF IT IN THE ACCOUNTS. They also REFUSE TO PASS US ANY RECEIPTS for the produce so removed.**"*

> 💬 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NCERT asks four questions on this Source. Here is the full answer.**
>
> **(1) *Explain the complaints the ryot is making.*** → ✅ **Four distinct complaints, and they escalate:** ⭐ **(a) Coerced borrowing** — income no longer covers household expenses, so they must *"beg"* for money, clothes and grain. **(b) Extortionate bond terms** — *"hard conditions in the bond"*, imposed as the price of the loan. **(c) A hidden second charge — the price mark-up.** Goods bought on credit cost **25–50% above the cash price**, which is **interest levied a second time, disguised as a price**, and it never appears in the interest rate at all. **(d) Uncredited produce and no receipts** — the harvest is taken, promised against the account, **and never entered**.
>
> **(2) *Why was the harvest taken by the moneylenders not credited to the peasants' account?*** → ✅ **Because an uncredited repayment keeps the debt alive.** The sahukar gets the crop **and** keeps the claim. ⭐ **Recall § 3.5: this is happening precisely when he wants to close his position and recover everything — so suppressing credits maximises what he can still demand.**
>
> **(3) *Why were peasants not given any receipts?*** → ✅ **Because a receipt is EVIDENCE, and the ryot cannot read the ledger.** Without a receipt, the only written record of the transaction is **the one the sahukar keeps** — and the bond is enforceable in a British court while the peasant's word is not. ⭐ **The absence of the receipt is the whole asymmetry of the system in one missing piece of paper — and it is exactly why the crowds at Supa went for the account books.**
>
> **(4) *If you were a moneylender what reasons would you give?*** → ✅ **Argue it properly, because NCERT wants the other side stated:** *"I lend to men with no collateral, in a district where the rains fail and the revenue does not; my losses are real and I price them in. The 'mark-up' is not interest but the ordinary difference between cash and credit sale, and I carry that risk for months. I take produce at harvest because that is the only moment there is anything to take, and I value it at what I can get for it, not at what he hoped. And I keep the accounts because I am the one who can keep accounts."* ⭐ **Then puncture it in one line:** *the defence explains a high rate; it does not explain refusing a receipt.* **A lender charging fairly has no reason to withhold proof of payment.**

#### 📜 Source 9 — *Deeds of hire* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**NCERT's explanation of the mechanism, first:**

> *"When debts mounted the peasant was unable to pay back the loan… He had **no option but to give over ALL HIS POSSESSIONS — land, carts, and animals — to the moneylender**. **But without animals he could not continue to cultivate. So he took land ON RENT and animals ON HIRE. ⭐ HE NOW HAD TO PAY FOR THE ANIMALS WHICH HAD ORIGINALLY BELONGED TO HIM.** He had to sign a **deed of hire** stating very clearly that **these animals and carts DID NOT BELONG TO HIM**. In cases of conflict, **these deeds could be ENFORCED THROUGH THE COURT.**"*

**The deed itself — signed by a peasant in November 1873, from the Deccan Riots Commission records:**

> *"**I have SOLD to you**, on account of the debt due to you, **my two carriages having iron axles, with their appurtenances and four bullocks** … **I have TAKEN FROM YOU ON HIRE under (this) deed THE VERY SAME two carriages and four bullocks.** I shall pay **every month the hire thereof at Rupees four a month**, and **obtain a receipt in your own handwriting**. ⭐ **In the absence of a receipt I shall not contend that the hire had been paid.**"*

```
   WHAT THE DEED OF HIRE ACTUALLY DOES
   -- read the four clauses in order

   CLAUSE 1   "I have SOLD to you ... my two carriages ...
               and four bullocks"
              -> ownership transferred to settle the debt

   CLAUSE 2   "I have TAKEN FROM YOU ON HIRE ...
               ** THE VERY SAME ** two carriages and four
               bullocks"
              -> the SAME objects come straight back to the
                 same yard, under the same man's hands

   CLAUSE 3   "at Rupees four a month"
              -> ** A NEW, PERMANENT, MONTHLY OUTFLOW **
                 created out of property he already owned

   CLAUSE 4   "In the absence of a receipt I shall not contend
               that the hire had been paid."
              -> ** HE SIGNS AWAY, IN ADVANCE, THE RIGHT TO
                 CLAIM HE PAID -- and Source 8 has just told
                 us the sahukars ROUTINELY REFUSED RECEIPTS. **

   ** NOTHING MOVES. NOTHING IS BUILT. NOTHING CHANGES HANDS
      IN THE FIELD. A DOCUMENT CONVERTS AN OWNER INTO A
      TENANT OF HIS OWN BULLOCKS. **
```

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Clause 4 is the masterstroke and you should point at it directly.** Put Source 8 and Source 9 side by side: in Source 8 the ryots complain that sahukars **"refuse to pass us any receipts."** In Source 9 the ryot signs a deed saying that **without a receipt he may not even argue that he paid.** ⭐ **The two documents interlock: one creates a rule that only a receipt proves payment, and the other establishes the practice of never issuing one.** **That is not sharp dealing; it is a designed trap, and both halves of it survive in the same archive.**
>
> 💬 **NCERT's questions on Source 9 — *"List all the commitments the peasant is making. What does such a deed tell us about the relationship between the peasant and the moneylender? How would it change the relationship between the peasant and the bullocks he previously owned?"*** → ✅ **Commitments:** he sells the carts and bullocks against the debt; he hires back the identical items; he pays **Rs 4 a month indefinitely**; he undertakes to obtain a handwritten receipt; and **he waives the right to assert payment without one.** ✅ **The relationship:** the sahukar is no longer a lender but **a landlord and an owner of the means of production**, and the debt has become **a permanent rent**. ✅ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The bullocks — this is the question worth answering well:** they were **capital** and are now **a cost**. An asset that made him a cultivator becomes a monthly liability he must earn before he has fed anyone. **He performs exactly the same work, with the same animals, in the same field — and every month he must first pay for the right to do it.** *(That is the most concrete image of colonial rural indebtedness in the whole NCERT series. Use it.)*

#### ⚖️ The Limitation Law of 1859 — a protective law turned inside out ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The ryots came to see the moneylender as **devious and deceitful**. They complained of moneylenders **manipulating laws and forging accounts**. In **1859 the British passed a LIMITATION LAW** that stated that **the loan bonds signed between moneylenders and ryots would have validity for ONLY THREE YEARS. This law was MEANT TO CHECK THE ACCUMULATION OF INTEREST OVER TIME.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**The moneylender, however, TURNED THE LAW AROUND, forcing the ryot to SIGN A NEW BOND EVERY THREE YEARS. When a new bond was signed, THE UNPAID BALANCE — that is, THE ORIGINAL LOAN AND THE ACCUMULATED INTEREST — WAS ENTERED AS THE PRINCIPAL on which a NEW SET OF INTEREST CHARGES was calculated.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the best single example in the chapter of colonial law producing the opposite of its intention, and it is a guaranteed Mains point.** Parliament's aim was to **cap** the growth of a debt by expiring the bond. **The effect was to force a renewal every three years — and each renewal CAPITALISED the accrued interest into a new principal.** ⭐ **A statute designed to prevent interest from piling up instead installed a legally-mandated three-year compounding cycle.** *Say it exactly like that.*
>
> 💡 **Why it worked for the lender and not the borrower:** the law fixed a **deadline** but said nothing about **what could go into the new bond**. **The ryot had no alternative lender, so "renegotiate or get nothing" was not a negotiation.** ⭐ **A protective rule with no bargaining power behind it becomes a schedule for the stronger party** — which is precisely N. G. Ranga's argument in [Theme 12 § 3.2](12_Theme12_Framing_the_Constitution.md): *"they need props, they need a ladder"*, because **a right you cannot enforce is a right the other side will administer.**

**The other techniques, listed by NCERT:**

> Moneylenders *"used a variety of other means to short-change the ryot: they **(a) refused to give receipts when loans were repaid**, **(b) entered fictitious figures in bonds**, **(c) acquired the peasants' harvest at low prices**, and **(d) ultimately took over peasants' property**."*

#### ✍️ Why bonds and deeds became the enemy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"**Deeds and bonds appeared as SYMBOLS OF THE NEW OPPRESSIVE SYSTEM. In the past such deeds had been RARE.** The British, however, were **suspicious of transactions based on INFORMAL UNDERSTANDING**, as was common in the past. **The terms of transactions, they believed, had to be CLEARLY, UNAMBIGUOUSLY AND CATEGORICALLY STATED in contracts, deeds and bonds, and REGULATED BY LAW. ⭐ UNLESS THE DEED OR CONTRACT WAS LEGALLY ENFORCEABLE, IT HAD NO VALUE.**"*

> *"Over time, peasants came to **associate the misery of their lives with THE NEW REGIME OF BONDS AND DEEDS**. They were **made to sign and put THUMB IMPRESSIONS on documents, but THEY DID NOT KNOW WHAT THEY WERE ACTUALLY SIGNING.** They had **no idea of the clauses that moneylenders inserted**. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THEY FEARED THE WRITTEN WORD.** But they had **no choice**, because **to survive they needed loans, and moneylenders were unwilling to give loans without legal bonds.**"*

```
   THE DEEPEST POINT IN THE CHAPTER

   The British reform was GENUINE and, on its face, MODERN:
     * no more vague informal understandings
     * everything written down
     * everything clear and enforceable at law
        |
        v
   BUT WRITING FAVOURS WHOEVER CAN READ.
        |
        v
   In a village where the LENDER writes, the LENDER keeps
   the book, and the COURT enforces the book,
   ** FORMALISATION DID NOT REPLACE THE OLD POWER --
      IT ARMED IT. **
        |
        v
   The customary norm ("interest not more than principal")
   had been enforced by the VILLAGE.
   The written bond was enforced by the STATE.
   ** The ryot lost the first protection and gained a
      creditor with a magistrate behind him. **
        |
        v
   ** SO WHEN THEY BURNT THE KHATAS AT SUPA THEY WERE NOT
      DESTROYING RECORDS OF DEBT. THEY WERE DESTROYING THE
      INSTRUMENT THAT MADE THE DEBT ENFORCEABLE. **
```

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ ***"They feared the written word"* — carry this sentence into the exam.** It answers, in five words, **why the revolt targeted paper**, and it is the bridge from § 3 back to the chapter's opening question about official archives. ⭐ **The same document that oppressed the ryot in 1875 is the document from which we now reconstruct his life. The historian's source was the peasant's enemy.**

#### 🧮 Source 10 — *How debts mounted* ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**A ryot's own explanation, in a petition to the Deccan Riots Commission:**

> *"A sowkar lends his debtor **Rs 100** on bond at **Rs 3-2 annas per cent per mensem**. The latter agrees to pay the amount **within eight days**… **Three years after** the stipulated time for repaying the amount, the sowkar takes from his debtor **another bond for the PRINCIPAL AND INTEREST TOGETHER at the same rate of interest**, and allows him **125 days'** time to liquidate the debt. After the lapse of **3 years and 15 days a third bond** is passed by the debtor … (this process is repeated) **at the end of 12 years … his interest … amounts to Rs 2,028-10 annas-3 paise.**"*

> 💬 **NCERT's margin question: *"Calculate the rate of interest that the ryot was paying over the years."*** → ✅ **Here is the working, and it is worth doing because the arithmetic proves the ryot's point.**
>
> ```
>    THE STATED RATE
>    "Rs 3-2 annas per cent per mensem"
>    16 annas = 1 rupee, so 3 annas 2 = Rs 3.125
>       = ** 3.125% PER MONTH **
>       = 37.5% per year, simple
>
>    WHAT THE THREE-YEAR ROLL-OVER DOES
>    In one 3-year cycle: 36 months x 3.125% = 112.5%
>       -> the sum owed multiplies by about ** 2.125 **
>    Then the WHOLE amount (principal + interest) becomes
>    the new principal -- and the cycle restarts.
>
>    OVER 12 YEARS = FOUR SUCH CYCLES
>       100 x 2.125 x 2.125 x 2.125 x 2.125
>       = 100 x (2.125)^4
>       ~ 100 x 20.4
>       ~ ** Rs 2,040 owed on a loan of Rs 100 **
>       -> interest of roughly ** Rs 1,940-2,030 **
>
>    ** WHICH MATCHES THE PETITION'S FIGURE OF
>       Rs 2,028-10a-3p ALMOST EXACTLY. **
>
>    THE EFFECTIVE RATE, compounded 3-yearly,
>    is therefore about ** 20 TIMES THE PRINCIPAL IN
>    12 YEARS ** -- roughly 29% a year compounding.
> ```
>
> ⚠️ ⭐⭐⭐ **A textual caution.** The passage opens with a loan of **Rs 100** but its closing line reads *"his interest on **Rs 1000**"*. ⭐ **The arithmetic only works for Rs 100** — as shown above, Rs 100 rolled over four times at this rate produces almost exactly the stated Rs 2,028. **Treat the "Rs 1000" as a misprint and answer from the Rs 100 that the same sentence begins with.** *(This is the kind of internal inconsistency worth spotting rather than reproducing.)*
>
> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And connect it to § 3.6's norm.** Custom said **interest must not exceed the principal** — a ceiling of Rs 100 on a Rs 100 loan. **The Commission's own records show Rs 2,028.** ⭐ **The customary limit was exceeded twenty-fold, and the mechanism that did it was the Limitation Law of 1859.** *That is the entire indictment, arithmetic included.*

## 4️⃣ The Deccan Riots Commission ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Why the enquiry happened at all — and it nearly did not:**

> *"When the revolt spread in the Deccan, **the Government of Bombay was INITIALLY UNWILLING to see it as anything serious.** But the **Government of India, WORRIED BY THE MEMORY OF 1857, PRESSURISED the Government of Bombay to set up a commission of enquiry** to investigate into the causes of the riots. **The commission produced a report that was presented to the BRITISH PARLIAMENT in 1878.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Three facts to fix, because each can be asked separately:** the **provincial** government did not want an enquiry; the **central** government forced one, **because of 1857**; and the report went to **the British Parliament, in 1878** — exactly as the **Fifth Report** of § 1.6 had gone to Parliament in **1813**. ⭐ **Both of this chapter's great official archives were produced for the same audience: MPs in London, not administrators in India.** *That shared destination shapes both documents, and noticing it is a first-class point.*

**What the Report contains — a genuinely rich archive:**

```
   THE DECCAN RIOTS REPORT (1878) -- what is IN it

   * enquiries held "in the districts where the riots spread"
   * ** RECORDED STATEMENTS of RYOTS, SAHUKARS and
        EYEWITNESSES **
   * ** STATISTICAL DATA on REVENUE RATES, PRICES and
        INTEREST RATES in different regions **
   * ** COLLATED REPORTS sent by DISTRICT COLLECTORS **
   * and the PETITIONS -- Sources 8, 9 and 10 of this
     chapter all come from its records

   ** This is why the chapter can quote a ryot at all.
      Without the Commission we would have the sahukar's
      ledger and nothing else. **
```

**And then NCERT's verdict on it — the paragraph the whole chapter has been building toward ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐:**

> *"In looking at such sources we have to again remember that they are **OFFICIAL sources** and **reflect OFFICIAL CONCERNS AND INTERPRETATIONS of events**. **The Deccan Riots Commission, for instance, was SPECIFICALLY ASKED TO JUDGE WHETHER THE LEVEL OF GOVERNMENT REVENUE DEMAND WAS THE CAUSE OF THE REVOLT. And after presenting all the evidence, the commission reported that THE GOVERNMENT DEMAND WAS NOT THE CAUSE OF PEASANT ANGER. IT WAS THE MONEYLENDERS WHO WERE TO BLAME.** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This argument is found VERY FREQUENTLY in colonial records. This shows that there was A PERSISTENT RELUCTANCE ON THE PART OF THE COLONIAL GOVERNMENT TO ADMIT THAT POPULAR DISCONTENT WAS EVER ON ACCOUNT OF GOVERNMENT ACTION.**"*

```
   THE STRUCTURE OF THE ACQUITTAL
   -- how an honest-looking enquiry reaches a chosen answer

   STEP 1   THE QUESTION IS SET BY THE ACCUSED
            The Commission was "SPECIFICALLY ASKED to judge
            whether THE LEVEL OF GOVERNMENT REVENUE DEMAND
            was the cause".
            ** Note what that framing does: it makes the
               state's guilt an ALL-OR-NOTHING question
               about ONE variable. **

   STEP 2   THE EVIDENCE IS REAL
            Statements, statistics, petitions, collectors'
            reports -- genuinely collected, and still
            valuable today.

   STEP 3   THE ANSWER IS 'NO'
            "the government demand was NOT the cause of
             peasant anger. It was THE MONEYLENDERS."

   STEP 4   AND THE ALTERNATIVE IS ALREADY IN THE ROOM
            The moneylender is a convenient culprit:
            unpopular, Indian, private, and ALREADY BEING
            ATTACKED BY THE RYOTS THEMSELVES.

   ** WHY THE VERDICT IS FALSE ON THE REPORT'S OWN EVIDENCE:
      Section 3.3 -- "REVENUE COULD RARELY BE PAID WITHOUT A
      LOAN FROM A MONEYLENDER."  The debt EXISTED to meet the
      revenue. Section 3.5 -- the demand rose 50 to 100 per
      cent at the very moment credit was withdrawn.
      ** You cannot separate the moneylender from the revenue
         demand, because the revenue demand is WHY the
         moneylender was in the village. ** **
```

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the payoff of the entire chapter, and the way to say it is this:** *"The Deccan Riots Commission did not lie. It collected genuine evidence, published it, and reached a conclusion its own evidence does not support — because the question it was set made the moneylender and the revenue demand into alternatives, when in fact the second had produced the first."*
>
> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And note NCERT's generalisation, which is what makes this more than one report's failing:** *"This argument is found **very frequently** in colonial records… a **persistent reluctance** to admit that popular discontent was **ever** on account of government action."* **"Ever" is a strong word and NCERT means it.** ⭐ **The colonial state's default explanation for Indian discontent was always an Indian intermediary — the moneylender here, the zamindar in the Fifth Report, "fanatics" and "agitators" elsewhere.** *(Compare Theme 11 § 6.3, where the police insisted nobody was responding to the Salt March while 60,000 were being arrested. **Same institutional reflex, sixty years later.**)*

**The chapter's closing instruction — ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ learn this verbatim, it is the answer to Exercise 9:**

> *"**Official reports, thus, are INVALUABLE SOURCES for the reconstruction of history. BUT THEY HAVE TO BE ALWAYS READ WITH CARE AND JUXTAPOSED WITH EVIDENCE CULLED FROM NEWSPAPERS, UNOFFICIAL ACCOUNTS, LEGAL RECORDS AND, WHERE POSSIBLE, ORAL SOURCES.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Note the balance, and reproduce it.** NCERT does **not** say official records are worthless — it calls them **"invaluable"**. **The instruction is to JUXTAPOSE, not to discard.** ⭐ **And the four named counterweights are a ready-made checklist: newspapers · unofficial accounts · legal records · oral sources.** *(This chapter has demonstrated three of the four — Source 7 is a newspaper, Sources 8–10 are petitions and deeds, and § 2.2's Santhal **myths and songs** are as close to an oral source as this chapter gets. The fourth, oral history proper, is the specialism of the deleted Partition chapter — see [Supplement A](13_Supplement_A_Understanding_Partition.md).)*
>
> 💬 **NCERT's "Discuss…" box here — *"Check what rates of interest are charged in the region where you live at present. Find out whether these rates have changed over the last 50 years. Is there a variation in the rates paid by different groups of people? What are the reasons for the differences?"*** → ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Worth actually doing, because it is a GS3 question in disguise.** The finding will be that **the poorest borrowers pay the highest rates** — the opposite of what risk-pricing alone would predict once collateral is accounted for — and that formal credit (banks, cooperatives, SHGs, Kisan Credit Card) charges a fraction of what the informal lender does, while **still not reaching everyone**. ⭐ **That gap between formal and informal rural credit is the direct descendant of this chapter, and Source 10's arithmetic is the historical benchmark to quote against it.**

## ⚡ UPSC FOCUS

### 🔴 Prelims traps — the claim vs the truth

| ❌ The tempting claim | ✅ The truth |
|---|---|
| The Permanent Settlement made the zamindar **the owner of the land** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **No.** He was **a revenue collector for the state**, not a landowner in the village. The distinction is the whole basis of § 1 |
| The zamindars defaulted because they were **lazy and extravagant** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ That is **the colonial explanation**, and NCERT rejects it. The structural reasons: **the initial demand was very high · it was fixed in the 1790s when agricultural PRICES WERE DEPRESSED · the demand was INVARIABLE regardless of the harvest · and the Company had simultaneously LIMITED the zamindar's own power** to collect and manage |
| A zamindar could pay late if the harvest failed | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The SUNSET LAW** — if the payment did not come in **by sunset of the appointed day**, the estate was liable to be **auctioned** |
| The Burdwan auction of **1797** transferred estates to new owners | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Overwhelmingly FICTITIOUS.** The **Raja of Burdwan's own agents bought back** the estates, and the purchase money was very often **never paid** |
| Outsiders who bought at auction simply took possession | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ They frequently **could not** — the former zamindar's ***lathiyals*** drove them off, **ryots resisted the entry of outsiders**, and some purchasers were **murdered** |
| Zamindars had no way to protect property from auction | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ One device: **transferring holdings to WOMEN**, because the Company's own regulations decreed that **women's property could not be taken over** |
| The **jotedars** were officials of the zamindar | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Rich PEASANTS**, and the zamindar's **rivals**. They held large areas, **controlled local trade and moneylending**, and were **located IN the villages** — which the zamindar, living in an urban centre, was not |
| Jotedars cultivated their own land | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ A large part was worked by **SHARECROPPERS — *adhiyars* / *bargadars*** — who *"brought their own ploughs, laboured in the field, and handed over **half the produce** to the jotedar after the harvest"* |
| "Jotedar" was the term used all over Bengal | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ They were most powerful in **NORTH BENGAL**; elsewhere similar figures were called ***haoladars*, *gantidars* or *mandals*** |
| The Fifth Report was the fifth chapter of a single book | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ It was **the fifth of a SERIES of reports** on the administration and activities of the **East India Company in India**, submitted to the **BRITISH PARLIAMENT in 1813** |
| The Fifth Report is a neutral survey | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ It was **a weapon in a British political fight** — over the Company's **monopoly** and its **misrule**. It therefore **EXAGGERATED the collapse of traditional zamindari power** and the failure of the settlement |
| The Fifth Report is therefore worthless | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **No** — NCERT's position throughout is *"invaluable… **but** to be read with care"*. It contains **petitions of zamindars and ryots, collectors' reports, statistical tables** |
| The **Paharias** practised settled plough agriculture | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Shifting cultivation with the HOE** — clearing patches by **burning the undergrowth**, using **the potash in the ash**, growing **pulses and millets FOR CONSUMPTION**, then leaving the land fallow and moving on |
| The Paharias were purely forest-dwellers with no economy | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Five occupations in NCERT's own list: **hunters · shifting cultivators · food gatherers · charcoal producers · silkworm rearers**. From the forest: **mahua (a flower) for food, silk cocoons and resin FOR SALE, wood for charcoal** |
| Paharia raids on the plains were simple banditry | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THREE functions:** survival in scarcity · **asserting power** over settled communities · **negotiating political relations** with outsiders. And **zamindars paid a regular TRIBUTE, traders paid a TOLL — after which the Paharia chiefs PROTECTED them** |
| The British began with a policy of conciliating the Paharias | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **In the 1770s they pursued "a brutal policy of EXTERMINATION, hunting the Paharias down and killing them."** Pacification came only **in the 1780s** |
| **Augustus Cleveland** was Governor-General | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Collector of Bhagalpur**, author of the **pacification** policy of the 1780s — and the man who took **William Hodges** to the Jangal Mahals in 1782 |
| Paharia chiefs who took the British allowance grew stronger | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The reverse — *"those who accepted **MOST OFTEN LOST AUTHORITY within the community**"*, becoming **"subordinate employees or STIPENDIARY CHIEFS"**. Many chiefs **refused** outright |
| Buchanan visited the Rajmahal hills as an independent naturalist | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **An employee of the East India Company**, travelling at Company expense with **draughtsmen, surveyors, palanquin bearers and coolies**, under **specific instructions on what to record** — *"immediately perceived as **an agent of the sarkar**"* |
| Buchanan and Buchanan-Hamilton are two people | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The same man.** He assumed his **mother's family name, Hamilton**, on inheriting her property after **1815** |
| Buchanan was a trained geologist | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **A PHYSICIAN** — **Bengal Medical Service, 1794–1815**, briefly **surgeon to Lord Wellesley**; he also organised the zoo that became the **Calcutta Alipore Zoo** and ran the **Botanical Gardens** |
| Buchanan recorded landscapes as he found them | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ He described *"not just what he saw… **but also HOW IT COULD BE TRANSFORMED AND MADE MORE PRODUCTIVE**"*, shaped by **the Company's commercial concerns and Western notions of progress**; he was *"inevitably **critical of the lifestyles of forest dwellers**"* |
| **William Hodges** painted the Rajmahal hills as officials saw them | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The opposite — *"a landscape that colonial officials found **dangerous and wild**… appears in the paintings of Hodges as **EXOTIC AND IDYLLIC**"*, in the tradition of **the picturesque** and **Romanticism** |
| The Rajmahal hills were high mountains | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"nowhere actually above 2,000 feet"** — Hodges created the impression of inaccessibility **by centring the hills and viewing them from below** |
| The Santhals were the original inhabitants of the Rajmahal hills | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **No** — they began coming into Bengal **around the 1780s**, moved to the lower Rajmahal slopes **around 1800**, and **displaced the hill folk (the Paharias)** who lived there |
| The Santhals settled there of their own accord | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Zamindars HIRED them** to reclaim land, and **British officials INVITED them** to settle in the **Jangal Mahals** — after the British **failed to subdue the Paharias** |
| **Damin-i-Koh** was created in 1855 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **By 1832.** *(1855–56 is the **Santhal REVOLT**; the **Santhal Pargana** came after it)* |
| The Damin-i-Koh was an unconditional grant | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ It carried conditions: **live within it · practise plough agriculture · and clear and cultivate AT LEAST ONE-TENTH of the area WITHIN TEN YEARS.** It was **surveyed, mapped, and enclosed with BOUNDARY PILLARS** |
| Santhal settlement grew modestly | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **40 villages in 1838 → 1,473 villages by 1851**; population **3,000 → over 82,000** |
| The Santhals rebelled against the moneylenders alone | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THREE targets — zamindars, moneylenders (*dikus*) AND the colonial state** — and the aim was *"to create **an ideal world for themselves where THEY WOULD RULE**"* |
| The Santhal Pargana was created before the revolt | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **After** it — **5,500 square miles** carved out of **Bhagalpur and Birbhum**, with **special laws**, in the hope that the Santhals *"could be conciliated"* |
| The Paharias lost land but their farming was unaffected | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Their system **stopped working**: *"shifting agriculture **depended on the ability to MOVE**… when the most fertile soils became inaccessible, being part of the Damin, the Paharias **could not effectively sustain their mode of cultivation**"* |
| The Deccan riots began in Bombay city | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ At **SUPA**, *"a large village in **POONA** district"* — **a market centre** where shopkeepers and moneylenders lived — on **12 May 1875**, spreading to **Ahmednagar** |
| The rioters were after grain and money | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ They demanded **the *bahi khatas* (ACCOUNT BOOKS) and the DEBT BONDS**, and **burnt them**. *(Grain shops were looted too, but the paperwork was the object)* |
| The riots covered a few villages near Poona | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Over 6,500 sq km**, **more than thirty villages**, over **two months**; **951 people arrested** |
| A **sahukar** was a moneylender | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **BOTH a moneylender AND a trader** — which is why he could set the interest **and** the price of the grain and cloth he sold |
| The Permanent Settlement was extended across India | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**rarely extended to any region beyond Bengal**"* — because after **1810 prices rose** and the state, with a **fixed** demand, **could claim no share** of the gain |
| The **ryotwari** settlement was fixed in perpetuity | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Lands were **RESURVEYED EVERY 30 YEARS and the rates INCREASED** — *"the revenue demand was **no longer permanent**"* |
| Ryotwari was settled with the village body | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Directly with the RYOT.** *(Settlement with the village/*mahal* is **MAHALWARI** — beyond this chapter, but commonly asked)* |
| **David Ricardo** advised the Indian government | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ He never came. His ideas reached India because **"colonial officials had learnt Ricardian ideas during their COLLEGE YEARS"** — a landowner should claim only **"the average rent"**, and untaxed surplus would turn cultivators into ***rentiers*** |
| The 1832–34 Deccan famine was a minor event | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ NCERT records that **one-third of the cattle** of the Deccan were killed and **half the human population died** |
| Deccan peasants borrowed because they were improvident | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**INEVITABLY, THEY BORROWED. REVENUE COULD RARELY BE PAID WITHOUT A LOAN FROM A MONEYLENDER.**"* Debt was **a requirement of the revenue system** |
| Before the 1860s Britain got most of its cotton from India | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **THREE-FOURTHS came from AMERICA.** The **Cotton Supply Association (1857)** and **Manchester Cotton Company (1859)** were founded **before** the Civil War to find alternatives |
| Indian cotton exports rose gradually after 1861 | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ American imports into Britain collapsed to **under 3% of normal** — **2,000,000 bales (1861) → 55,000 bales (1862)**; Deccan cotton **acreage DOUBLED 1860–64**, and **by 1862 over 90% of Britain's cotton imports came from India** |
| The cotton boom enriched the Deccan peasantry | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"these boom years **did NOT bring prosperity to all**… **some RICH peasants did gain, but for THE LARGE MAJORITY, cotton expansion meant HEAVIER DEBT**"* — the money arrived as **advances (Rs 100 per acre)**, i.e. as debt |
| The revolt followed immediately on the end of the boom | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The boom ended **1865**; the revolt came **1875** — after **credit was withdrawn** *and* the **new settlement raised the demand by 50 to 100 per cent** |
| The customary limit on interest was 50% | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The interest charged could not exceed the PRINCIPAL.** The Commission found a case of **over Rs 2,000 interest on a loan of Rs 100** |
| The **Limitation Law of 1859** protected peasants | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ It **intended** to, by making bonds valid for only **three years** — but moneylenders **"turned the law around"**, forcing a **new bond every three years** in which **principal + accumulated interest became the new principal**. It created **a legally-scheduled compounding cycle** |
| Ryots refused to sign bonds | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ They signed — *"they were made to sign and put **thumb impressions** on documents, but **they did not know what they were actually signing**… **THEY FEARED THE WRITTEN WORD.** But they had **no choice**"* |
| Under a "deed of hire" the peasant kept his bullocks | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ He **sold** them against the debt and **hired back the very same animals**, paying **Rs 4 a month** — *"he now had to **pay for the animals which had originally belonged to him**"* — and waived the right to claim payment without a receipt |
| The Deccan Riots Commission was set up by the Bombay government on its own initiative | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Bombay was *"**initially unwilling** to see it as anything serious"*; the **GOVERNMENT OF INDIA**, *"**worried by the memory of 1857**"*, **pressurised** it. The report went to the **British Parliament in 1878** |
| The Commission blamed the government's revenue demand | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The opposite.** It was *"**specifically asked**"* to judge that question and concluded *"the government demand was **NOT** the cause… it was **THE MONEYLENDERS** who were to blame"* — reflecting *"a **persistent reluctance**… to admit that popular discontent was **ever** on account of government action"* |
| NCERT concludes that official records should be discarded | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Official reports… are **INVALUABLE sources**… **BUT** they have to be always **read with care and JUXTAPOSED** with evidence culled from **newspapers, unofficial accounts, legal records and, where possible, ORAL sources**"* |

### 🟢 Mains angles

1. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"What are the problems of using official sources in writing about the history of peasants?"** *(NCERT Exercise 9 — the chapter's own central question)*
   → **Build the answer on the chapter's three archives.** ⭐ **(a) The FIFTH REPORT (1813)** was produced inside a British political fight over the Company's monopoly, so it **exaggerated** zamindari collapse and misrule. ⭐ **(b) BUCHANAN'S JOURNAL** was written by a paid Company servant with **specific instructions on what to record** — it measures granite obsessively and dismisses Santhal houses in four adjectives. ⭐ **(c) The DECCAN RIOTS REPORT (1878)** was **"specifically asked"** whether the revenue demand caused the revolt, and acquitted the state on evidence that contradicts the acquittal. ⭐ **The general problems, drawn from these:** the question is set by the accused; the categories are the state's (revenue, not subsistence); the observer is identified with power, so the observed flee or lie; and the record exists only where the state was anxious. ⭐ **Finish with NCERT's own balance:** invaluable, but to be **juxtaposed** with newspapers, unofficial accounts, legal records and oral sources — and note that **the archive exists at all because the peasants rebelled.**

2. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Why were many zamindaris auctioned after the Permanent Settlement?"** *(NCERT Exercise 6)*
   → **Four structural causes**, not moral ones: **the initial demand was very high**; it was **fixed in the 1790s, when agricultural prices were depressed**, so the zamindar's income was at its lowest exactly when his obligation was set; **the demand was invariable** and enforced by **the sunset law**; and the Company had **simultaneously curtailed the zamindar's own authority** to collect. ⭐ **Add the internal dimension:** ryots and village headmen could **deliberately delay payment**, and **jotedars had a positive interest in the zamindar's default** — they were often the buyers. ⭐ **Then the twist that makes the answer:** the auctions **frequently did not transfer anything** — over 95% of the Burdwan sale was **fictitious**, agents bought back, money went unpaid, and outsiders were driven off by *lathiyals*. **The auction system looked like a market and functioned as a ritual.**

3. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Why was the jotedar a powerful figure in many areas of rural Bengal?"** *(NCERT Exercise 1)*
   → **Five sources of power, and the last is decisive.** Large consolidated holdings; **control of local trade AND moneylending**, so poorer cultivators were doubly dependent; **sharecropping** (*adhiyars*/*bargadars*) giving him half the produce with none of the labour or capital; the ability to **mobilise dependent ryots** against the zamindar and to **obstruct zamindari officials**; and ⭐ **crucially, LOCATION — the jotedar lived in the village and the zamindar did not.** ⭐ **Conclude with the transfer of power:** when estates were auctioned, jotedars were **among the purchasers** — so the Permanent Settlement, designed to secure a loyal landed gentry, **helped hollow it out from below.**

4. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"In what way was the livelihood of the Paharias different from that of the Santhals?"** *(NCERT Exercise 7)*
   → **Contrast systematically — tool, land tenure, mobility, relation to forest, relation to market, relation to the state.** **Hoe vs plough; shifting vs settled; the whole region held as a community's territory vs a surveyed grant inside boundary pillars; forest as food/income/pasture vs forest as an obstacle; pulses and millets FOR CONSUMPTION vs commercial crops FOR THE MARKET; no revenue vs revenue.** ⭐ **Then refuse the easy moral:** the Santhals' "success" was temporary — heavy taxes, *dikus* seizing land, zamindars encroaching, and by the 1850s **revolt**. ⭐ **Best closing line:** *the two peoples were set against each other by a state that needed one of them to displace the other, and within a generation it had dispossessed both.*

5. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Why did the Santhals rebel against British rule?"** *(NCERT Exercise 4)*
   → **Name all three antagonists** — the state's **heavy taxes** on land the Santhals themselves had cleared, the ***dikus*'** high interest and seizure of land for unpaid debt, and **zamindars asserting control over the Damin**. ⭐ **Frame it as a broken bargain:** they were **invited**, given a demarcated homeland on condition they clear and plough it, and did so spectacularly (40 → 1,473 villages) — **and then lost the land they had made.** ⭐ **State the aim in NCERT's own words** — *"to create an ideal world for themselves **where they would rule**"* — and note that this is **a claim to sovereignty**, unlike the Deccan ryots' targeted attack on documents. ⭐ **Close with the sequel:** the Santhal Pargana, 5,500 sq miles with special laws — protective legislation as the standard colonial answer, and Ranga's 1946 speech as evidence of how well it held.

6. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"What explains the anger of the Deccan ryots against the moneylenders?"** *(NCERT Exercise 5)*
   → ⭐ **Lead with NCERT's own distinction, because it is the whole answer:** what infuriated them was *"**not simply** that they had got deeper into debt… **but that moneylenders were VIOLATING THE CUSTOMARY NORMS of the countryside**"* — above all the norm that **interest could not exceed the principal** (breached twenty-fold: Rs 2,000 on Rs 100). ⭐ **Then the specific grievances from the Sources:** goods sold on credit at **25–50% above cash prices**; **produce taken and never credited**; **receipts refused**; **deeds of hire** making a man pay Rs 4 a month for his own bullocks; and the **1859 Limitation Law turned into a three-yearly compounding device**. ⭐ **Then the trigger** — not the debt but **the refusal of new credit in 1865–75**, exactly as the revenue demand rose 50–100%. ⭐ **Close on the target:** they burnt **the account books and bonds**, because in a colonial economy **the document was the instrument of the debt.**

7. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"How did the American Civil War affect the lives of ryots in India?"** *(NCERT Exercise 8)*
   → **Trace the chain, then the reversal.** Britain's **three-quarters dependence on American cotton**; the pre-war hedge (**Cotton Supply Association 1857, Manchester Cotton Company 1859**); **1861** war and the collapse to **under 3%** of normal imports; the credit chain **Bombay export merchants → urban sahukars → rural moneylenders → the ryot at Rs 100 an acre**; **acreage doubled 1860–64**, **over 90% of Britain's imports Indian by 1862**. ⭐ **Then the sentence that carries the marks:** *"some rich peasants did gain, but for the large majority, cotton expansion meant heavier debt"* — because **the boom arrived as credit, not as cash**. ⭐ **Then 1865:** American revival, exports fall, **advances restricted and repayment demanded**, colliding with the **50–100% higher revenue settlement** → **1875**. ⭐ **The general lesson, worth stating:** *an export boom transmitted through debt leaves the producer with the obligations after the prices have gone.*

8. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"The colonial insistence on written contracts was a reform that made the peasant weaker." Examine.**
   → **Grant the intention fully:** the British distrusted *"transactions based on informal understanding"* and required terms *"clearly, unambiguously and categorically stated"* and **enforceable at law** — on its face, protection against arbitrary custom. ⭐ **Then show the reversal on three levels.** **(a) Literacy:** the ryot signed with a **thumb impression**, *"did not know what he was actually signing"*, and **"feared the written word."** **(b) Enforcement:** the customary ceiling on interest had been policed **by the village**; the bond was policed **by the court** — so the lender no longer needed his neighbours' approval, only a magistrate. **(c) Design:** Source 9's clause — *"in the absence of a receipt I shall not contend that the hire had been paid"* — interlocks with Source 8's complaint that **receipts were routinely refused.** ⭐ **Conclude:** *formalisation is neutral only between parties of comparable power; between unequal parties it converts custom's restraints into the stronger party's instruments.* **And note the 1859 Limitation Law as the same lesson in statute form.**

9. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Compare the Deccan Riots Commission's verdict with the evidence it collected."**
   → **A four-step demolition.** **(1) The framing:** it was *"specifically asked"* whether **the level of government revenue demand** caused the revolt — a framing that makes the state and the moneylender **alternatives**. **(2) The verdict:** the demand was not the cause; **the moneylenders were.** **(3) The evidence against the verdict, from the chapter itself:** *"revenue could rarely be paid without a loan from a moneylender"*; officials' own admission that the 1820s settlement was **"exorbitant, rigid, and the peasant economy on the verge of collapse"**; and the **50–100% increase** landing precisely as credit dried up. **(4) The general pattern:** NCERT's *"persistent reluctance… to admit that popular discontent was ever on account of government action."* ⭐ **Best closing line:** *the Commission did not falsify its evidence; it answered a question designed so that the honest answer would still exonerate the state.*

10. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Colonial officials produced two incompatible images of the same country, and both served the empire." Discuss with reference to the Rajmahal hills.**
    → **The administrator's hills** were *"dangerous and wild, peopled by turbulent tribes"* — a description that licensed **extermination in the 1770s** and **pacification in the 1780s**. **The artist's hills** — William Hodges, guest of **Augustus Cleveland**, painting **aquatints** in 1782 — were *"exotic and idyllic"*, in the tradition of **the picturesque** and **Romanticism**. ⭐ **Fig. 9.9 is the proof of technique:** the hills are *"nowhere actually above 2,000 feet"*, but a **low viewpoint and central framing** make them impassable — *no fact falsified, a vantage chosen.* ⭐ **Then generalise:** one image justified the violence, the other made the possession charming to the British public; **and the aquatint was a REPRODUCIBLE medium, made to circulate.** ⭐ **Link forward** to the *Illustrated London News* Santhal images (Figs. 9.10–9.14) and onward to [Theme 10 § 5](10_Theme10_Rebels_and_the_Raj_1857.md).

11. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Both the Permanent Settlement and the ryotwari system failed the cultivator, but in opposite ways." Assess.**
    → ⭐ **The symmetry is the answer.** **Permanent Settlement:** a **fixed** demand set in the depressed 1790s ruined the **zamindar** at first (auctions, the sunset law) and then, after **1810 when prices rose**, **ruined the STATE'S share** — which is precisely why it was *"rarely extended beyond Bengal."* **Ryotwari:** a demand **revised every 30 years and increased**, settled **directly on the ryot**, justified by **Ricardian** theory about rentiers — which removed the intermediary but **transferred the entire risk of harvest and price to the cultivator**, in cash, on a fixed date. ⭐ **The common outcome:** *both systems required a cash payment that did not vary with the harvest, and in both the gap was filled by a moneylender.* **Bengal produced the jotedar; the Deccan produced the sahukar. Different instruments, the same hinge.**

12. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Explain how the expansion of settled agriculture in eastern India destroyed a functioning political order."**
    → **Establish that an order existed:** zamindars paid **tribute**, traders paid a **toll**, and *"once the toll was paid, the Paharia chiefs **PROTECTED** the traders."* NCERT itself calls it **"this negotiated peace."** ⭐ **Then the three British motives** for clearance — enlarge land revenue, produce crops for export, establish a settled ordered society — and the belief beneath them, that forest people were *"savage, unruly, primitive, and **difficult to govern**."* ⭐ **Then the mechanism of collapse:** forests and pastures contracted → raids intensified → the raids were read as proof of savagery → justifying further clearance. **A circle.** ⭐ **And the co-option that failed:** chiefs who took Cleveland's allowance **lost authority**, becoming *"stipendiary chiefs"* — **the empire dissolved the very authority it was trying to purchase.**

## 🔑 Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Permanent Settlement** | The 1793 Bengal revenue settlement fixing the state's demand **in perpetuity**, made with the **zamindar** — who was therefore *"not a landowner in the village, but **a revenue collector of the state**"* |
| **Sunset law** | If the revenue was not paid **by sunset of the appointed day**, the zamindari was **liable to be auctioned** |
| **Jama** | The revenue **assessment** — the sum demanded. ⭐ Note that the Fifth Report's figures are in *jama*, i.e. **revenue attached to land, not acreage** |
| **Zamindar** | Under the Permanent Settlement, the **revenue-collecting intermediary** with a fixed obligation, an urban residence, and a **disbanded** military establishment |
| **Amla / amlah** | The zamindar's **revenue officials**, who did the actual collecting in the villages |
| **Ryot** | The **cultivator/peasant** — the party with whom the **ryotwari** settlement was made directly |
| **Jotedar** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ A **rich peasant** who accumulated large holdings, **controlled local trade and moneylending**, and **lived in the village** — the zamindar's chief rival. Most powerful in **North Bengal** |
| **Haoladar · gantidar · mandal** | Regional names for figures equivalent to the **jotedar** in other parts of Bengal |
| **Adhiyar / bargadar** | **Sharecroppers** who *"brought their own ploughs, laboured in the field, and handed over **half the produce**"* to the jotedar |
| **Lathiyal** | The zamindar's **club-wielding strongmen**, used to prevent auction-purchasers from taking possession |
| **Benami** | Literally "anonymous" — a purchase made **in a fictitious or another's name**, the device by which zamindars bought back their own auctioned estates |
| **Fifth Report** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The **fifth of a series of reports** on the East India Company's administration in India, submitted to the **British Parliament in 1813**; contains **zamindars' and ryots' petitions, collectors' reports and statistical tables** — and was **written inside a British political fight over the Company** |
| **Paharias** | The hill people of the **Rajmahal hills** — shifting cultivators with the **hoe**, food gatherers, hunters, charcoal producers and silkworm rearers, who *"considered **the entire region as their land**"* |
| **Mahua** | A **flower** collected from the forest by the Paharias **for food** |
| **Stipendiary chief** | ⭐ A chief **on a British allowance** — Augustus Cleveland's pacification device, which caused those who accepted it to *"**most often lose authority within the community**"* |
| **Damin-i-Koh** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The territory **demarcated by 1832** as **"the land of the Santhals"** — surveyed, mapped, **enclosed with boundary pillars**, with a condition to clear and cultivate **one-tenth within ten years** |
| **Diku** | The Santhal term for the **outsider / moneylender** who charged high interest and took over land for unpaid debt |
| **Santhal Pargana** | **5,500 square miles** carved out of **Bhagalpur and Birbhum** **after** the revolt of **1855–56**, with special laws, in the hope of conciliation |
| **Picturesque** | An aesthetic tradition, rooted in **Romanticism**, that sought nature as **an idyll uncorrupted by modern civilisation** — the convention within which **William Hodges** painted the Rajmahal hills |
| **Aquatint** | NCERT's definition: *"a picture produced by **cutting into a copper sheet with acid** and then printing it"* — a **reproducible** medium |
| **Sahukar** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Someone who acted as **BOTH a moneylender AND a trader**"* — hence control of **both** the interest rate and the price of goods |
| **Bahi khata** | The moneylender's **account book** — the object the Deccan ryots demanded and burnt |
| **Ryotwari** | The revenue system settled **directly with the ryot**, based on assessed soil income, with **resurvey every 30 years and increased rates** |
| **Rentier** | NCERT's gloss: *"a term used to designate people who **live on rental income from property**"* — the Ricardian objection to the Bengal zamindar |
| **Limitation Law (1859)** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Made loan bonds valid for **only three years**, intending to check interest accumulation — but produced a **three-yearly renewal in which principal + accrued interest became the new principal** |
| **Deed of hire** | The document by which a peasant **sold his carts and bullocks against a debt and hired back the very same animals** for a monthly payment |
| **Deccan Riots Report** | The **1878** report to the **British Parliament** containing statements of ryots, sahukars and eyewitnesses, statistics on revenue, prices and interest, and collectors' reports — which concluded that **the moneylenders, not the government demand, were to blame** |

### NCERT's own exercises — **answer these first**

**Answer in 100–150 words:**
1. Why was the **jotedar a powerful figure** in many areas of rural Bengal? → *(§ 1.4; Mains angle 3)*

4. Why did the **Santhals rebel** against British rule? → *(§ 2.2; Mains angle 5)*
5. What explains the **anger of the Deccan ryots against the moneylenders**? → *(§ 3.6; Mains angle 6)*

**Write a short essay (250–300 words):**
6. Why were many **zamindaris auctioned** after the Permanent Settlement? → *(Mains angle 2)*
7. In what way was the **livelihood of the Paharias different from that of the Santhals**? → *(Mains angle 4)*
8. How did the **American Civil War affect the lives of ryots** in India? → *(Mains angle 7)*
9. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ What are the **problems of using official sources** in writing about the history of peasants? → *(Mains angle 1 — **the chapter's own central question; do this one properly**)*

**Map work:**
10. *"On an outline map of the subcontinent, **mark out the areas described in this chapter**. Find out whether there were **other areas where the Permanent Settlement and the ryotwari system were prevalent** and plot these on the map as well."*
→ ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Worth doing, because the map IS the argument.** Mark: **Bengal** (Burdwan, Dinajpur — Permanent Settlement); the **Rajmahal hills** and **Damin-i-Koh**, with **Bhagalpur** and **Birbhum** for the Santhal Pargana; and the **Bombay Deccan** — **Poona (Supa)**, **Ahmednagar**. ⭐ *(Beyond NCERT, for the second half of the question: **Permanent Settlement / zamindari** — Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, and parts of the Madras and United Provinces; **RYOTWARI** — Madras and Bombay Presidencies, later Assam and Coorg; **MAHALWARI** — the North-Western Provinces, Punjab and parts of Central India.* ⭐ **The pattern to notice: the Permanent Settlement covers the EARLIEST conquests, ryotwari and mahalwari the LATER ones — because by then the Company had learnt what a fixed demand costs.)*

**Projects (choose one):**
11. Read one of **Francis Buchanan's district reports** on eastern India and collate what it says about rural society, *"highlighting the ways in which historians can use such texts."*
12. In your own region, **talk to older people in a rural community**, visit their fields, and find out what they produce, how they earn their livelihoods, what their parents did, and **how their lives have changed over the last 75 years.**

> 💡 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Project 12 is the one to do, and NCERT has hidden a lesson in it.** Every source in this chapter is **written**, and almost every one was written by an official. **Project 12 asks you to create an ORAL source** — which is precisely the fourth item on the chapter's own closing checklist (*"newspapers, unofficial accounts, legal records and, where possible, **oral sources**"*). ⭐ **Doing it teaches you what the Deccan Riots Commission could not record: what people say when nobody is assessing them.** *(And it is the direct methodological bridge to [Supplement A — Understanding Partition](13_Supplement_A_Understanding_Partition.md), the one chapter in the series built entirely on oral history.)*

### My additions — harder questions

13. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The Deccan Riots Report is the best source we have on the Deccan ryot, and it exists only because he rebelled."* Discuss what this implies for the history of people who leave no records of their own.
14. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"A permanently fixed revenue demand is a bet on prices, and both parties lost it."* Explain, using the 1790s and the years after 1810.
15. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Compare the **Santhal revolt of 1855–56** with the **Deccan riots of 1875** on: who was attacked, what was destroyed, what was demanded, and what political aim was expressed. **Account for the differences.**
16. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The Limitation Law of 1859 is the clearest case in the chapter of a protective statute becoming an instrument of oppression."* Examine — and state the general condition under which protective laws fail.
17. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Why did confinement **destroy** Paharia agriculture rather than merely reduce it? Answer with reference to the logic of shifting cultivation.
18. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Co-option destroyed the authority it was trying to buy."* Discuss with reference to **Augustus Cleveland's stipendiary chiefs**, and compare with any other instance of colonial co-option you know.
19. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Read **Source 3** and **Source 4** together. What does the difference in the *care* with which Buchanan records rocks and records people tell us about the purpose of his survey?
20. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The ryots of Supa understood this chapter's argument better than most historians."* Justify, with reference to what they chose to burn.
21. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Fig. 9.10 and Fig. 9.12 appeared in the same issue of the same magazine on 23 February 1856.** What changed between the two images, and what did not?
22. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Formalisation is neutral only between parties of comparable power."* Assess, using the bond, the deed of hire, and the thumb impression.
23. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Using **Source 8** and **Source 9** together, reconstruct the complete set of ways a sahukar could extract value from a ryot — and identify which of them would **not** show up in a stated interest rate.
24. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"The colonial state's standard explanation for Indian discontent was always an Indian intermediary."* Test this claim against **the Fifth Report** and **the Deccan Riots Report**, and say what it cost the historical record.
