## 🎬 The Framing — read this before anything else

NCERT opens by putting Gandhi in a line of national founders:

> *"In the history of nationalism a **single individual is often identified with the making of a nation**. Thus, for example, we associate **Garibaldi** with the making of **Italy**, **George Washington** with the **American War of Independence**, and **Ho Chi Minh** with the struggle to free **Vietnam** from colonial rule. In the same manner, Mahatma Gandhi has been regarded as the '**Father**' of the Indian nation."*

And then immediately qualifies it — and **this qualification is the whole chapter**:

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"However, like Washington or Ho Chi-Minh, Mahatma Gandhi's political career was **shaped and constrained by the society in which he lived**. For **individuals, even great ones, are made by history even as they make history**."*

```
   THE TWO HALVES OF THIS CHAPTER

   SECTIONS 1-5  :  WHAT HAPPENED               1915 -- 1948
        |
        |   Gandhi returns -> BHU -> Champaran/Kheda/Ahmedabad
        |   -> Rowlatt -> Non-cooperation -> Chauri Chaura
        |   -> Salt March -> Round Tables -> Quit India
        |   -> Partition -> 30 January 1948
        |
   SECTION 6     :  HOW WE KNOW IT              the historian's toolkit
        |
        |   6.1  speeches (public voice)  vs  letters (private script)
        |   6.2  autobiographies -- and their SILENCES
        |   6.3  the colonial police -- Fortnightly Reports
        |   6.4  newspapers -- English, Indian-language, foreign
        |
        v
   ** Every source is PARTIAL. The Home Department said nobody cared
      about the Salt March WHILE 60,000 people were being arrested. **
```

> 🎯 **Why the framing matters for your answer-writing.** Two candidates write on Gandhi. One writes *"Gandhi launched Non-Cooperation in 1921 and withdrew it after Chauri Chaura."* The other writes *"Gandhi's authority rested on symbols the poorest Indian could act on — a fistful of salt, a spinning wheel, a loincloth — but the colonial Home Department's own confidential reports insisted, fortnight after fortnight, that nobody was responding, even as 60,000 arrests were being made. The gap between the two is the measure of how badly the Raj understood what was happening to it."* **The second candidate has read Section 6.**

### 🧑‍⚖️ The analogy that fits this chapter: **four witnesses to the same event**

Gandhi trained as a lawyer, was tried as a criminal, and spent his life arguing in public. So take the chapter's evidence problem the way a court takes it. Suppose a single event — the Dandi March — and four accounts of it:

```
   THE EVENT:  Gandhi walks to the sea and picks up salt, April 1930

   WITNESS 1 -- GANDHI'S OWN SPEECH at Dandi, 5 April
        "the Government ... felt ashamed to arrest such an army"
        -> a PUBLIC voice, crafted to be heard, deliberately generous
           to the opponent because generosity was itself a tactic

   WITNESS 2 -- HIS PRIVATE LETTERS (and Nehru's, and Prasad's)
        "I suggested your name for the crown of thorns"
        -> a PRIVATE script -- irritation, mediation, tiredness,
           things nobody says from a platform

   WITNESS 3 -- THE POLICE, Fortnightly Reports (Confidential)
        "the meetings ... excite little or no interest"
        -> a SECRET record, and yet the least honest of the four,
           because it reports what senior officials WANTED to believe

   WITNESS 4 -- THE NEWSPAPERS
        TIME magazine: first "spindly frame", then "Saint and Statesman"
        -> a PUBLISHED account, shaped by the politics of its owner,
           and capable of changing its mind within one week

   ** None of them is lying outright. All four are incomplete. **
   ** The historian's job is not to pick the true one -- it is to
      understand WHY each says what it says. **
```

> ⚖️ The analogy is exact, not decorative: a court does not throw out a hostile witness, it asks *what interest shapes this testimony*. That is precisely what Section 6 teaches — and it is the same skill Theme 10 teaches with the 1857 sources.

## 1️⃣ A Leader Announces Himself

### 🚢 January 1915 — the return

> *"In January 1915, **Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** returned to his homeland after **two decades of residence abroad**."*

Most of those two decades were in **South Africa**, where he had gone as a **lawyer** and *"in time became a leader of the Indian community in that territory."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **NCERT quotes the historian Chandran Devanesan: South Africa was *"the making of the Mahatma."*** Learn this name and this phrase — it is a favourite quotation-attribution question.

**What, specifically, did South Africa give him? NCERT lists three things — memorise the list:**

```
   SOUTH AFRICA, 1893-1914 -- WHAT IT MADE

   1.  He "first forged the distinctive techniques of non-violent
       protest known as SATYAGRAHA"

   2.  He "first promoted HARMONY BETWEEN RELIGIONS"

   3.  He "first alerted upper-caste Indians to their DISCRIMINATORY
       TREATMENT of LOW CASTES AND WOMEN"

   ** Note what this list means: every single one of Gandhi's later
      Indian preoccupations was already fully formed BEFORE he
      landed at Bombay. India did not teach him these. **
```

### 🇮🇳 The India of 1915 versus the India of 1893

> *"The India that Mahatma Gandhi came back to in 1915 was **rather different from the one that he had left in 1893**. Although still a colony of the British, it was **far more active in a political sense**."*

| 1893 (when he left) | 1915 (when he returned) |
|---|---|
| Congress a small elite body | *"The Indian National Congress now had **branches in most major cities and towns**"* |
| Narrow social base | *"Through the **Swadeshi movement of 1905-07** it had **greatly broadened its appeal among the middle classes**"* |
| No towering popular leaders | *"That movement had **thrown up some towering leaders**"* |

### 🔺 "Lal, Bal and Pal" — and why the alliteration matters ⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
   THE MILITANTS -- "LAL, BAL AND PAL"

     LALA LAJPAT RAI ........ PUNJAB      (north-west)
     BAL GANGADHAR TILAK .... MAHARASHTRA (west)
     BIPIN CHANDRA PAL ...... BENGAL      (east)

   ** NCERT's point about the nickname is not that it is catchy.
      "the alliteration conveying the ALL-INDIA CHARACTER of their
      struggle, since their NATIVE PROVINCES WERE VERY DISTANT
      FROM ONE ANOTHER."  The name itself was a claim about reach. **

   THE MODERATES -- "preferred a more GRADUAL and PERSUASIVE approach"

     GOPAL KRISHNA GOKHALE ... "Gandhiji's acknowledged POLITICAL MENTOR"
     MOHAMMAD ALI JINNAH ..... at this date a MODERATE, and --
                               "like Gandhiji, a lawyer of GUJARATI
                                extraction TRAINED IN LONDON"
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The Jinnah detail is one of the most quietly examinable facts in the book.** In 1915 Jinnah is (a) a **Moderate**, (b) **inside the Congress**, (c) a **Gujarati**, and (d) a **London-trained lawyer** — i.e. Gandhi's near-twin in background and, at that date, his political ally. The chapter ends with the two men unable to agree and the country partitioned. **NCERT plants this deliberately.** *(Beyond the book: Jinnah was in 1916 the architect of the Lucknow Pact between Congress and the League, and was hailed as the "Ambassador of Hindu–Muslim Unity" — the same session at which Gandhi was approached by the Champaran peasant.)*

### 🎓 The BHU speech, February 1916 — ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**On Gokhale's advice**, Gandhi *"spent a year travelling around British India, getting to know the land and its peoples."* His **first major public appearance** was at the **opening of the Banaras Hindu University (BHU) in February 1916**.

**Who was in the audience:** *"the **princes and philanthropists** whose donations had contributed to the founding of the BHU"* and *"important leaders of the Congress, such as **Annie Besant**."*

**Where Gandhi stood in that room:** *"Compared to these dignitaries, Gandhiji was **relatively unknown**. He had been invited **on account of his work in South Africa, rather than his status within India**."*

**What he said — learn at least the bolded phrases:**

> *"The opening of the BHU… was **'certainly a most gorgeous show'**. But he worried about the contrast between the **'richly bedecked noblemen'** present and **'millions of the poor' Indians who were absent**."*
>
> *"**There is no salvation for India unless you strip yourself of this jewellery and hold it in trust for your countrymen in India.**"*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**There can be no spirit of self-government about us, if we take away or allow others to take away from the peasants almost the whole of the results of their labour. Our salvation can only come through the farmer. Neither the lawyers, nor the doctors, nor the rich landlords are going to secure it.**"*

**Why this was extraordinary in context:**

```
   THE OCCASION           :  celebration -- a NATIONALIST university,
                             "sustained by Indian money and Indian
                             initiative"
                                     |
   THE EXPECTED TONE      :  self-congratulation
                                     |
   WHAT GANDHI DID INSTEAD:  "remind those present of the PEASANTS
                             AND WORKERS who constituted a MAJORITY
                             of the Indian population, YET WERE
                             UNREPRESENTED IN THE AUDIENCE"
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The two-level reading NCERT gives — this is the single most quotable analytical sentence in Section 1:**

> *"Gandhiji's speech at Banaras in February 1916 was, **at one level, merely a statement of fact** — namely, that **Indian nationalism was an elite phenomenon, a creation of lawyers and doctors and landlords**. But, **at another level, it was also a statement of intent** — the **first public announcement of Gandhiji's own desire to make Indian nationalism more properly representative of the Indian people as a whole**."*

**And the opportunity came within ten months:** at the **annual Congress at Lucknow, December 1916**, *"he was approached by **a peasant from Champaran in Bihar**, who told him about the **harsh treatment of peasants by British indigo planters**."*

> 💡 *Beyond the book:* that peasant was **Rajkumar Shukla**, who followed Gandhi persistently until he agreed to come. NCERT leaves him anonymous — but the anonymity is itself the point: an unnamed cultivator redirected the course of Indian nationalism.

> 📌 **NCERT's own "Discuss…" box here:** *"Find out more about the national movement in India before 1915 and see whether Mahatma Gandhi's comments are justified."* → **Short answer: largely yes.** Pre-1915 Congress was dominated by lawyers, journalists, teachers and landholders; its annual sessions were in English; its methods were petitions and resolutions ("Political mendicancy", as the Extremists sneered). Even the Swadeshi movement of 1905–07, its broadest phase, was strongest among students and the urban middle class in Bengal. **But not entirely fair:** Swadeshi did reach artisans and weavers, and the 1907 Surat split was partly *about* how far to widen the base. *(This is a good 150-word answer if it comes up.)*

## 2️⃣ The Making and Unmaking of Non-cooperation

### 🌾 The three localised satyagrahas, 1917–1918 — ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ know all three cold

```
   1917  CHAMPARAN, BIHAR
         "seeking to obtain for the peasants SECURITY OF TENURE
          as well as the FREEDOM TO CULTIVATE THE CROPS OF THEIR CHOICE"
          -- i.e. against the indigo planters' forced-cultivation system
             |
   1918  AHMEDABAD, GUJARAT   (his HOME state)
         "he intervened in a LABOUR DISPUTE ... demanding BETTER
          WORKING CONDITIONS for the TEXTILE MILL WORKERS"
             |
   1918  KHEDA, GUJARAT
         "he joined PEASANTS ... in asking the state for the
          REMISSION OF TAXES following the FAILURE OF THEIR HARVEST"

   ** WHAT THE THREE DID FOR HIM:
      "These initiatives ... marked Gandhiji out as a NATIONALIST WITH
       A DEEP SYMPATHY FOR THE POOR."
   ** WHAT THEY DID NOT DO:
      "At the same time, these were all LOCALISED STRUGGLES."          **
```

> 🔴 **Prelims trap:** the three satyagrahas are often mis-paired.
> **Champaran = tenure + crop choice (indigo, Bihar, 1917)** · **Ahmedabad = workers' conditions (1918)** · **Kheda = tax remission after crop failure (1918)**.
> *(Beyond the book: Ahmedabad was where Gandhi undertook his **first fast** as a political weapon, and Kheda is where **Sardar Patel** first worked with him.)*

### ⚖️ 1919 — the Rowlatt Act: the issue the British handed him

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Then, in 1919, the **colonial rulers delivered into Gandhiji's lap an issue from which he could construct a much wider movement**."*

**The logic of the Act, in NCERT's own sequence:**

```
   DURING the Great War 1914-18, the British had instituted:
        - CENSORSHIP OF THE PRESS
        - DETENTION WITHOUT TRIAL
                        |
   These were WARTIME emergency powers -- expected to lapse.
                        |
   Instead, "on the recommendation of a committee chaired by
   SIR SIDNEY ROWLATT, THESE TOUGH MEASURES WERE CONTINUED."
                        |
                        v
   ** The grievance is not that the powers existed. It is that
      PEACETIME WAS BEING GOVERNED BY WARTIME RULES. **
```

**The response:** *"Gandhiji called for a **countrywide campaign against the 'Rowlatt Act'**. In towns across **North and West India**, **life came to a standstill**, as **shops shut down and schools closed** in response to the **bandh** call."*

### 💥 Why Punjab exploded — the sentence to remember ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The protests were **particularly intense in the Punjab**, where **many men had served on the British side in the War — expecting to be rewarded for their service. Instead they were given the Rowlatt Act**."*

That single sentence is the causal chain. Then:

- **Gandhi was detained** while proceeding to the Punjab; prominent local Congressmen were arrested.
- *"The situation in the province grew progressively more tense, reaching a **bloody climax in Amritsar in April 1919**, when a **British Brigadier ordered his troops to open fire on a nationalist meeting**."*
- ⭐ *"**More than four hundred people were killed** in what is known as the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre**."*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Figures trap — know that the number is contested.** NCERT Class 12 says *"more than four hundred"*. *(Beyond the book: the official **Hunter Committee** figure was **379**; Congress's own enquiry and later estimates ran to about **1,000**.)* The date was **13 April 1919** (Baisakhi) and the officer was **Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer** — **NCERT names neither here**, so if a question gives them, they come from outside this chapter. **Do not "correct" NCERT's four hundred; know it is a floor, not a count.**

### 🌟 The turning point

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"It was the **Rowlatt satyagraha that made Gandhiji a truly national leader**."*

**Not** Champaran. **Not** the BHU speech. **Rowlatt.** Because Champaran/Kheda/Ahmedabad were *localised*; Rowlatt was the first **countrywide** action.

### 🚫 Non-cooperation — what it actually asked of people

*"Emboldened by its success, Gandhiji called for a campaign of '**non-cooperation**' with British rule."*

```
   THE PROGRAMME -- what Indians were asked to STOP doing:

        stop attending SCHOOLS
        stop attending COLLEGES
        stop attending LAW COURTS
        do not PAY TAXES
              |
        summed up by Gandhi as a
        "RENUNCIATION OF (ALL) VOLUNTARY ASSOCIATION
         WITH THE (BRITISH) GOVERNMENT"
              |
   THE PROMISE:  "If non-cooperation was effectively carried out,
                  said Gandhiji, INDIA WOULD WIN SWARAJ WITHIN A YEAR."
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The word "voluntary" is doing all the work.** Gandhi's insight was that **colonial rule was not held up by force alone** — it needed Indians to attend its schools, plead in its courts, staff its offices and buy its cloth. **Withdraw the consent and the machine has nothing to run on.** This is why the movement was, as Louis Fischer put it, *"negative enough to be peaceful but positive enough to be effective."*

### 🕌 Khilafat — why Gandhi joined it ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"To further broaden the struggle he had **joined hands with the Khilafat Movement** that sought to restore the **Caliphate**, a symbol of **Pan-Islamism**."*

**NCERT's box — the three Khilafat demands. Learn all three:**

```
   THE KHILAFAT MOVEMENT (1919-1920)
   "a movement of Indian Muslims, led by MUHAMMAD ALI and SHAUKAT ALI"
   -- the "ALI BROTHERS" --  that demanded:

     1. The Turkish SULTAN or KHALIFA must retain control over the
        MUSLIM SACRED PLACES in the erstwhile Ottoman empire

     2. The JAZIRAT-UL-ARAB (Arabia, Syria, Iraq, Palestine) must
        remain under MUSLIM SOVEREIGNTY

     3. The Khalifa must be left with SUFFICIENT TERRITORY to enable
        him to DEFEND THE ISLAMIC FAITH

   "The Congress SUPPORTED the movement and Mahatma Gandhi sought to
    CONJOIN IT to the Non-cooperation Movement."
```

**Gandhi's calculation, stated plainly:** *"Gandhiji hoped that by **coupling non-cooperation with Khilafat**, India's **two major religious communities, Hindus and Muslims, could collectively bring an end to colonial rule**."*

> ⚠️⭐⭐⭐ **A genuine slip in the NCERT text — worth knowing so a wrongly-worded option doesn't trap you.** The main text says the Caliphate *"had recently been abolished by the Turkish ruler Kemal Attaturk"* — but the box dates the movement to **1919–1920**, and **Atatürk abolished the Caliphate in March 1924**, i.e. *four years after* the movement began. What the Khilafatists were actually agitating about in 1919–20 was the **harsh post-war treatment of the defeated Ottoman Sultan** (formalised in the **Treaty of Sèvres, 1920**). **The abolition of the Caliphate in 1924 is what killed the Khilafat movement, not what started it.** The box is right; the sentence in the main text is loose. **Trust the box.**

### 🌊 2.1 Knitting a popular movement — the surge, with figures ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"These movements certainly unleashed a **surge of popular action that was altogether unprecedented in colonial India**."*

```
   WHO ACTED, AND HOW

   STUDENTS ....... stopped going to government schools and colleges
   LAWYERS ........ refused to attend court
   WORKING CLASS .. went on strike in many towns and cities
                    ** OFFICIAL FIGURES, 1921 -- LEARN THESE THREE **
                       396 STRIKES
                       600,000 WORKERS involved
                       7 MILLION WORKDAYS lost
   COUNTRYSIDE .... "seething with discontent too"
       HILL TRIBES in NORTHERN ANDHRA ...... violated the FOREST LAWS
       FARMERS in AWADH .................... did not pay TAXES
       PEASANTS in KUMAUN .................. refused to CARRY LOADS
                                             for colonial officials
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Now the sentence that explains every mass movement in modern Indian history — memorise it:**

> *"These protest movements were **sometimes carried out in defiance of the local nationalist leadership**. **Peasants, workers, and others interpreted and acted upon the call to 'non-cooperate' with colonial rule in ways that best suited their interests, rather than conform to the dictates laid down from above.**"*

> 🔗 **This is the exact same argument as Class 10 Ch.2's "differing strands"** — and the three examples even match (Andhra hill tribes = the Gudem/Alluri Sitaram Raju rebellion; Awadh peasants = Baba Ramchandra's movement against *begar*). **Class 10 gives you the detail; Class 12 gives you the general principle.** Quote the principle, illustrate with the detail. That combination is a full-marks answer.

**Louis Fischer's formulation** (Gandhi's American biographer) — a superb quotation to deploy:

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Non-cooperation** became the name of an epoch in the life of India and of Gandhiji. **Non-cooperation was negative enough to be peaceful but positive enough to be effective. It entailed denial, renunciation, and self-discipline. It was training for self-rule.**"*

**The scale of the achievement, in one line:** *"As a consequence of the Non-Cooperation Movement the British Raj was **shaken to its foundations for the first time since the Revolt of 1857**."*

> 🔗 That line is a **direct bridge to Theme 10**. The book measures 1921 against 1857 — the two moments the Raj felt genuinely threatened.

### 🔥 Chauri Chaura, February 1922 — the ethics case study ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

```
   FEBRUARY 1922 -- CHAURI CHAURA, a HAMLET in the UNITED PROVINCES
                    (now Uttar Pradesh and Uttaranchal)

        "a group of PEASANTS attacked and TORCHED A POLICE STATION"
        "SEVERAL CONSTABLES PERISHED in the conflagration"
                             |
        "This act of violence prompted Gandhiji to
         CALL OFF THE MOVEMENT ALTOGETHER."
                             |
        Gandhi's justification, verbatim:
        ** "NO PROVOCATION CAN POSSIBLY JUSTIFY (THE) BRUTAL MURDER
            OF MEN WHO HAD BEEN RENDERED DEFENCELESS AND WHO HAD
            VIRTUALLY THROWN THEMSELVES ON THE MERCY OF THE MOB." **
```

> 🎓 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is a GS Paper IV (Ethics) case study, and you should be able to argue both sides.**
>
> **For the withdrawal:** the movement's *only* claim to moral authority was non-violence; a movement that kills defenceless men has become the thing it opposes. **Means and ends are inseparable** — Gandhi's core ethical position. Withdrawing at the peak proved the principle was not tactical.
>
> **Against the withdrawal:** the movement was at its height; hundreds of thousands had sacrificed careers and education; one incident by one crowd of one village was allowed to veto a national movement — a **collective punishment for an individual act**. Motilal Nehru, Lala Lajpat Rai and C.R. Das were among those bitterly critical. *(Subhas Bose later called it a "national calamity".)*
>
> **The mature position:** the withdrawal cost the movement momentum but bought Gandhian nationalism the credibility that made the **Salt March possible eight years later** — the Raj could never again claim that mass mobilisation meant mob rule. *(Note the detail NCERT plants earlier: Gandhi's train passed **through Chauri Chaura station** in February 1921 and a Marwari gentleman handed him a donation there. The place was already on his map.)*

> 💡 *Beyond the book, for precision:* **4 February 1922**, Gorakhpur district; **22 policemen** died; the movement was formally withdrawn by the **Bardoli Resolution of 12 February 1922**.

### ⚖️ The trial — Justice C.N. Broomfield, March 1922 ⭐⭐⭐

*"Gandhiji himself was **arrested in March 1922, and charged with sedition**."* The judge, **Justice C.N. Broomfield**, *"made a remarkable speech while pronouncing his sentence"*:

> *"It would be impossible to ignore the fact that **you are in a different category from any person I have ever tried or am likely to try**… that, **in the eyes of millions of your countrymen, you are a great patriot and a leader**. Even those who differ from you in politics **look upon you as a man of high ideals and of even saintly life**."*
>
> Sentence: *"**six years' imprisonment**"* — but: *"**If the course of events in India should make it possible for the Government to reduce the period and release you, no one will be better pleased than I.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why NCERT includes this.** It is a colonial judge, in a colonial court, applying colonial law — **conceding the moral case while enforcing the legal one.** For a Mains answer on the moral authority of the freedom struggle, or an Ethics answer on the conflict between legality and legitimacy, **this is a gift of a quotation.** *(Beyond the book: the trial was on **18 March 1922** at Ahmedabad, under **Section 124A** — the sedition section. Gandhi pleaded guilty and asked for the **highest penalty**. He was released in **February 1924** after an appendicitis operation.)*

### 👣 2.2 A people's leader — HOW the appeal was actually built ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"By 1922, Gandhiji had **transformed Indian nationalism**, thereby **redeeming the promise he made in his BHU speech of February 1916**. It was **no longer a movement of professionals and intellectuals**; now, **hundreds of thousands of peasants, workers and artisans also participated in it**."*

NCERT then gives **four distinct mechanisms**. Most students remember only the first. **Learn all four — a question asking "how did Gandhi identify with the common people" wants 1 and 2; a question asking "how did he transform the movement" wants all four.**

#### Mechanism 1 — Identification: dress, language, manual labour

> *"They appreciated the fact that he **dressed like them, lived like them, and spoke their language**. **Unlike other leaders he did not stand apart from the common folk, but empathised and even identified with them.**"*

```
   THE CONTRAST NCERT DRAWS

   OTHER NATIONALIST LEADERS  |  GANDHI
   ---------------------------|---------------------------------
   dressed FORMALLY           |  "a simple DHOTI or LOINCLOTH"
   a WESTERN SUIT             |
   or an Indian BANDGALA      |
                              |  spent part of EACH DAY working
                              |  on the CHARKHA (spinning wheel),
                              |  "and encouraged other nationalists
                              |   to do likewise"

   ** WHY SPINNING MATTERED SOCIALLY, in NCERT's words:
      "The act of spinning allowed Gandhiji to BREAK THE BOUNDARIES
       that prevailed within the TRADITIONAL CASTE SYSTEM,
       BETWEEN MENTAL LABOUR AND MANUAL LABOUR."                      **
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The 1921 change of dress — a dated fact, and examinable:** *"In **1921**, during a **tour of South India**, Gandhiji **shaved his head and began wearing a loincloth** in order to **identify with the poor**. His new appearance also came to **symbolise asceticism and abstinence** — qualities he celebrated **in opposition to the consumerist culture of the modern world**."*

**And the class point NCERT is careful to make:** *"Mahatma Gandhi was **by caste a merchant, and by profession a lawyer**; but his simple lifestyle and love of working with his hands allowed him to **empathise more fully with the labouring poor and for them, in turn, to empathise with him**."*

> 🔁 **The verb runs both ways — "empathise with them… for them to empathise with him."** That reciprocity is the answer to "how did he identify with the common people?"

#### Mechanism 2 — The charkha, and Gandhi's argument about machinery ⭐⭐⭐⭐

NCERT's box: *"Mahatma Gandhi was **profoundly critical of the modern age in which machines enslaved humans and displaced labour**. He saw the charkha as a **symbol of a human society that would not glorify machines and technology**. The spinning wheel, moreover, could **provide the poor with supplementary income and make them self-reliant**."*

**Gandhi in his own words — two extracts you should be able to paraphrase:**

> *Young India,* **13 November 1924**: *"**What I object to, is the craze for machinery as such.** The craze is for what they call **labour-saving machinery**. Men go on 'saving labour', till **thousands are without work and thrown on the open streets to die of starvation**. **I want to save time and labour, not for a fraction of mankind, but for all; I want the concentration of wealth, not in the hands of few, but in the hands of all.**"*

> *Young India,* **17 March 1927**: *"**Khaddar does not seek to destroy all machinery** but it does **regulate its use and check its weedy growth**. It uses machinery **for the service of the poorest in their own cottages**. **The wheel is itself an exquisite piece of machinery.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The second quotation destroys the standard exam distractor.** The lazy claim is *"Gandhi was anti-machine / anti-technology."* **He was not.** He objected to the **craze** for labour-saving machinery in a country whose scarcest resource was not labour but **employment**. His test was **distributional**: *for whom is the labour saved?* The charkha passes the test; the power-loom, in 1920s India, did not. **He calls the spinning wheel itself "an exquisite piece of machinery."** *(This is also a live GS-III argument — appropriate technology, employment-intensive growth, and the jobless-growth debate. Use it there.)*

#### Mechanism 3 — Rumour and the "Mahatma": Shahid Amin ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"In a fascinating study, the historian **Shahid Amin** has traced the **image of Mahatma Gandhi among the peasants of eastern Uttar Pradesh**, as conveyed by **reports and rumours in the local press**."*

**The Gorakhpur newspaper account of February 1921** (an eyewitness texture worth quoting):

> *"At Bhatni Gandhiji addressed the local public… There were **not less than 15,000 to 20,000 people** at Nunkhar, Deoria, Gauri Bazar, **Chauri Chaura** and Kusmhi (stations)… Mahatmaji was very pleased to witness the scene at Kusmhi, as **despite the fact that the station is in the middle of a jungle there were not less than 10,000 people** here. Some, **overcome with their love, were seen to be crying**… a sheet was spread and **currency notes and coins started raining**… Outside the Gorakhpur station the Mahatma was stood on a high carriage and **people had a good darshan of him** for a couple of minutes."*

**The three families of rumour NCERT records:**

```
   FAMILY A -- GANDHI AS AN AGENT OF A HIGHER AUTHORITY
     "he had been SENT BY THE KING to redress the grievances of the
      farmers, and that he had the power to OVERRULE ALL LOCAL OFFICIALS"

   FAMILY B -- GANDHI AS SUPERIOR TO THE RAJ ITSELF
     "Gandhiji's power was SUPERIOR TO THAT OF THE ENGLISH MONARCH,
      and that WITH HIS ARRIVAL THE COLONIAL RULERS WOULD FLEE the district"

   FAMILY C -- DIRE CONSEQUENCES FOR THOSE WHO OPPOSED HIM
     "villagers who criticised Gandhiji found their HOUSES MYSTERIOUSLY
      FALLING APART or their CROPS FAILING"
```

**Source 2 — "The miraculous and the unbelievable" — the specific stories (these are the ones quoted in exams and in classroom discussion):**

| Testing his power | Punishing opposition |
|---|---|
| **Sikandar Sahu** (Basti, **15 February**): would believe when the ***karah*** (boiling pan) of **sugarcane juice** in his *karkhana* **split in two** — *"Immediately the karah actually split into two from the middle."* | A gentleman of **Gorakhpur city** questioned the need to ply the charkha — **his house caught fire.** |
| A **cultivator in Azamgarh**: would believe if **sesamum sprouted on his field planted with wheat** — *"Next day all the wheat in that field became sesamum."* | **April 1921**, a village in UP: people **gambling**; one refused to stop and **abused Gandhi** — *"the next day his goat was bitten by four of his own dogs."* |

**The names he was known by:** *"**Gandhi baba**", "**Gandhi Maharaj**", or simply "**Mahatma**"* — and the role he played in the peasant imagination: *"a **saviour**, who would **rescue them from high taxes and oppressive officials** and **restore dignity and autonomy** to their lives."*

> 📌 **NCERT's own "Discuss…" box:** *"You have read about rumours in **Chapter 10** and seen that **the circulation of rumours tells us about the structure of the belief of a time**: they tell us about **the mind of the people who believe in the rumours and the circumstances that make this belief possible**. What do you think these rumours about Gandhiji reflect?"*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The model answer — and this is a very likely Mains-style question:**
> 1. **Rumours are not evidence of what happened; they are evidence of what people wanted and feared.** A rumour spreads only if it is *plausible within an existing world-view*.
> 2. Family A (**"sent by the King"**) reveals that peasants understood politics through a **framework of just kingship** — a distant, benevolent sovereign whose local agents are corrupt. **They were not yet imagining a republic; they were imagining a better king.**
> 3. Family B (**"superior to the English monarch"**) shows that same framework **turning against the Raj** — the source of legitimate authority has shifted from London to Gandhi.
> 4. Family C (**punishment miracles**) shows the **moral programme being enforced socially** — note *what* the punished men did: refused the charkha, gambled, drank liquor. **The rumours were doing the work of Gandhi's constructive programme in villages he never visited.**
> 5. **The deepest point:** the rumours are **not** evidence that Gandhi was manipulating anyone. He never claimed miraculous powers. They show that **a mass movement is co-authored** — the leader supplies the symbol, the people supply the meaning. Which loops straight back to *"individuals… are made by history even as they make history."*
>
> 🔗 **The Theme 10 link the box is pointing at:** in 1857, rumours of **bone-dust in flour**, of **chapatis** passing village to village, and the prophecy that the Raj would end **100 years after Plassey** did the same work — expressing a collective anxiety that could not be stated as policy.

#### Mechanism 4 — Careful, deliberate organisation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (the mechanism everyone forgets)

> *"While Mahatma Gandhi's mass appeal was undoubtedly genuine… it must **also be stressed that his success in broadening the basis of nationalism was based on CAREFUL ORGANISATION**."*

```
   THE ORGANISATIONAL WORK -- four moves

   1. NEW BRANCHES of the Congress set up in various parts of India

   2. "PRAJA MANDALS" established -- to promote the nationalist creed
      IN THE PRINCELY STATES
      ** important: British India was only 2/3 of the subcontinent.
         The Praja Mandals are how the movement entered the other third. **

   3. COMMUNICATION IN THE MOTHER TONGUE, "rather than in the
      language of the rulers, English"

   4. PROVINCIAL COMMITTEES REORGANISED ON LINGUISTIC REGIONS,
      "rather than on the ARTIFICIAL BOUNDARIES of British India"

   RESULT: "nationalism was taken to the FARTHEST CORNERS of the
            country and embraced by SOCIAL GROUPS PREVIOUSLY
            UNTOUCHED BY IT."
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Point 4 is a Polity/GS-II goldmine.** The Congress reorganised itself on **linguistic** lines in **1920**, three decades before the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**. The party that later agonised over linguistic states had already **conceded the principle inside its own structure** in order to mobilise. Use this in any answer on linguistic reorganisation, federalism, or the language question. *(Beyond the book: this was the **Nagpur Congress, December 1920**, which also created the Working Committee and admitted members for four annas.)*

#### 1924–1928: the social-reform interlude ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Released **February 1924**, Gandhi *"now chose to devote his attention to the **promotion of home-spun cloth (khadi)**, and the **abolition of untouchability**."*

## 3️⃣ The Salt Satyagraha — *A Case Study*

### 🔙 1928 — re-entering politics ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"For several years after the Non-cooperation Movement ended, Mahatma Gandhi **focused on his social reform work**. **In 1928, however, he began to think of re-entering politics.**"*

**Two things happened in 1928 that Gandhi *blessed but did not lead* — a precise and examinable distinction:**

| Event, 1928 | Gandhi's role — NCERT is exact |
|---|---|
| The all-India campaign against the **all-White Simon Commission**, *"sent from England to enquire into conditions in the colony"* | *"Gandhiji **did not himself participate** in this movement, **although he gave it his blessings**"* |
| The **peasant satyagraha in Bardoli** | *"as he also did"* — blessed, not led |

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Prelims trap:** a statement saying *"Gandhi led the anti-Simon agitation"* or *"Gandhi led the Bardoli satyagraha"* is **FALSE by NCERT's own wording.** *(Beyond the book: **Bardoli, 1928, was led by Vallabhbhai Patel**, and it is where the women of Bardoli gave him the title **"Sardar"**. The Simon Commission was appointed in **1927**, arrived **February 1928**, and was met with **"Simon Go Back"**; **Lala Lajpat Rai** was injured in the Lahore lathi-charge against it and died soon after.)*

### 🏛️ Lahore, December 1929 — two things, both examinable ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"In the end of **December 1929**, the Congress held its **annual session in the city of Lahore**. The meeting was **significant for two things**:"*

```
   1. THE ELECTION OF JAWAHARLAL NEHRU AS PRESIDENT
      "signifying the PASSING OF THE BATON OF LEADERSHIP
       TO THE YOUNGER GENERATION"

   2. THE PROCLAMATION OF COMMITMENT TO "PURNA SWARAJ"
      -- or COMPLETE INDEPENDENCE
      ** the goal changes: no longer dominion status, no longer
         reform within the empire -- COMPLETE INDEPENDENCE **
```

### 🇮🇳 26 January 1930 — the first "Independence Day" ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

*"On **26 January 1930**, '**Independence Day**' was observed, with the **national flag being hoisted** in different venues, and **patriotic songs** being sung."*

**Gandhi's instructions — note how much of this is about *simultaneity and construction*, not protest:**

> *"It would be good if the declaration [of Independence] is made by **whole villages, whole cities even**… It would be well if **all the meetings were held at the identical minute in all the places**."*

- The time of the meeting to be advertised *"in the traditional way, by the **beating of drums**"*
- Celebrations to **begin with the hoisting of the national flag**
- The rest of the day to be spent *"in doing some **constructive work**, whether it is **spinning**, or **service of 'untouchables'**, or **reunion of Hindus and Mussalmans**, or **prohibition work**, or even all these together, which is not impossible"*

**The pledge — a text worth knowing, because it is Lockean and it reappears in 1950:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"it was the **inalienable right of the Indian people, as of any other people, to have freedom and to enjoy the fruits of their toil**"*, and that *"**if any government deprives a people of these rights and oppresses them, the people have a further right to alter it or abolish it**."*

> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Carry this straight into Theme 12.** The reason the Constitution was brought into force on **26 January 1950** was to make the republic's birthday the same date as this pledge — the day India had *declared* freedom for itself twenty years before it was *granted*. **A one-line link like that is worth real marks in a Polity or History answer.** *(Note also the pledge's echo of the American Declaration of Independence's "right to alter or abolish".)*

### 🧂 3.1 Dandi — why salt? ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Mahatma Gandhi announced that he would **lead a march to break one of the most widely disliked laws in British India, which gave the state a monopoly in the manufacture and sale of salt**. His picking on the salt monopoly was **another illustration of Gandhiji's tactical wisdom**."*

**NCERT's own reasoning, in three clauses:**

```
   1. "in EVERY INDIAN HOUSEHOLD, SALT WAS INDISPENSABLE"
   2. "yet people were FORBIDDEN FROM MAKING SALT EVEN FOR
       DOMESTIC USE, compelling them to BUY IT FROM SHOPS
       AT A HIGH PRICE"
   3. "The state monopoly over salt was DEEPLY UNPOPULAR; by making
       it his target, Gandhiji hoped to MOBILISE A WIDER DISCONTENT
       against British rule."
```

> 🧂 **The analogy that actually fits.** Every political movement needs an issue people can *act on*, not merely agree with. A demand for dominion status is something you can **support**; a salt law is something you can **break**, at the seashore, with your hands, at no cost, in front of your neighbours — and **every household in India already had the grievance in its kitchen**. Caste, class, region, religion, literacy: none of them filtered who could participate. **Salt was the lowest possible threshold for entry into the national movement.** That, and not the revenue, is why it was chosen.

**Source 3 — Gandhi's own indictment. His "fourfold curse" is the most compact examinable list in the chapter:**

> *"The volume of information being gained daily shows **how wickedly the salt tax has been designed**. In order to prevent the use of salt that has not paid the tax **which is at times even fourteen times its value**, the **Government destroys the salt it cannot sell profitably**. Thus **it taxes the nation's vital necessity; it prevents the public from manufacturing it and destroys what nature manufactures without effort.** No adjective is strong enough for characterising this wicked **dog-in-the-manger policy**… **Maunds if not tons of salt are said to be destroyed on the Konkan coast.** The same tale comes from **Dandi**."*

```
   ** THE SALT MONOPOLY IS A "FOURFOLD CURSE" -- Gandhi's four counts **

   1. "It DEPRIVES THE PEOPLE OF A VALUABLE EASY VILLAGE INDUSTRY"
   2. "involves WANTON DESTRUCTION OF PROPERTY that nature
       produces in abundance"
   3. "the DESTRUCTION ITSELF MEANS MORE NATIONAL EXPENDITURE"
       -- i.e. the state PAYS salt officers TO DESTROY the salt
   4. "an unheard-of TAX OF MORE THAN 1,000 PER CENT is exacted
       from a STARVING PEOPLE"

   And the closing political line:
   "This tax has remained so long BECAUSE OF THE APATHY OF THE
    GENERAL PUBLIC. Now that it is sufficiently roused, THE TAX HAS
    TO GO. How soon it will be abolished depends upon the strength
    the people."
```

> 📌 **NCERT's question on Source 3:** *"Why was salt destroyed by the colonial government? Why did Mahatma Gandhi consider the salt tax more oppressive than other taxes?"*
> **Answer:** (a) Salt formed naturally on the coasts; if people could freely collect it, the **monopoly and its revenue collapsed**. Salt that could not be sold profitably was destroyed **to keep it out of people's reach** — the state paid officials specifically for this destruction. (b) More oppressive because it fell on an **absolute necessity of life** that **nature supplies free**, it was **regressive** (a starving family pays the same as a rich one), the rate was **over 1,000 per cent**, and it simultaneously **destroyed a village industry**. **An income tax takes a share of your surplus; the salt tax taxed survival itself.**

### 🚶 The march — 12 March to 6 April 1930

> *"Where **most Indians understood the significance of Gandhiji's challenge, the British Raj apparently did not.** Although Gandhiji had **given advance notice** of his 'Salt March' to the Viceroy **Lord Irwin**, **Irwin failed to grasp the significance of the action**."*

```
   12 MARCH 1930 ... "Gandhiji began WALKING from his ashram at
                      SABARMATI towards the ocean"
        |
        |  ** advance notice given to the Viceroy. He was not
        |     evading the state -- he was INVITING it to respond. **
        |
        |  meanwhile: "PARALLEL SALT MARCHES were being conducted
        |              in OTHER PARTS OF THE COUNTRY"
        v
   ~3 WEEKS LATER (6 April, at DANDI)
        "making a FISTFUL OF SALT as he did and thereby
         MAKING HIMSELF A CRIMINAL IN THE EYES OF THE LAW"
```

> 💡 *Beyond the book:* the march covered roughly **240 miles / 385 km** from **Sabarmati Ashram** to **Dandi** (coastal Navsari, Gujarat), begun with **78 chosen volunteers**. **Gandhi himself was arrested on 5 May 1930.** Other salt marches: **C. Rajagopalachari** from Tiruchirappalli to **Vedaranyam**, and **K. Kelappan** in Malabar.

**Source 4 — the Dandi speech, 5 April 1930 ("Tomorrow we shall break the salt tax law"):**

> *"When I left Sabarmati with my companions for this seaside hamlet of Dandi, **I was not certain in my mind that we would be allowed to reach this place**… If someone says that this **betrays imperfect faith on my part, I shall not deny the charge**… That I have reached here is in **no small measure due to the power of peace and non-violence**: that power is universally felt."*
>
> *"The Government may, if it wishes, **congratulate itself**… for **it could have arrested every one of us**. In saying that **it did not have the courage to arrest this army of peace, we praise it. It felt ashamed to arrest such an army.** **He is a civilised man who feels ashamed to do anything which his neighbours would disapprove.** The Government deserves to be congratulated on not arresting us, **even if it desisted only from fear of world opinion**."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**What if I and all the eminent leaders in Gujarat and in the rest of the country are arrested? This movement is based on the faith that WHEN A WHOLE NATION IS ROUSED AND ON THE MARCH NO LEADER IS NECESSARY.**"*

> 📌 **NCERT's question:** *"What does the speech tell us about how Gandhiji saw the colonial state?"*
> **Model answer — make four points:**
> 1. **He saw it as susceptible to shame, i.e. as a moral actor** — *"It felt ashamed to arrest such an army"*, *"He is a civilised man who feels ashamed…"*. Satyagraha is only coherent if the opponent has a conscience to appeal to.
> 2. **He saw it as susceptible to world opinion** — *"even if it desisted only from fear of world opinion"*. He knew the Raj's self-image as a liberal empire was a lever.
> 3. **He addressed it with deliberate courtesy** — *"deserves to be congratulated"* — which is itself a tactic: **praising restraint makes repression costlier.**
> 4. ⭐ **He judged it fallible and hesitant, not omnipotent** — he had genuinely expected arrest, and says so, admitting *"imperfect faith"*. **The candour is strategic: a leader who admits doubt is harder to portray as a manipulator.**
> **And the deepest point:** by declaring *"no leader is necessary"* he was **removing the state's best weapon** — if arresting Gandhi cannot stop the movement, arresting Gandhi is pointless.

### 🕵️ Two contemporary windows on the march — police and press

**(a) The police reports.** *"The progress of Gandhiji's march to the seashore can be traced from the **secret reports filed by the police officials deputed to monitor his movements**."* These reproduce his village speeches, in which he *"called upon **local officials to renounce government employment** and join the freedom struggle."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **At the village of Wasna**, Gandhi told the upper castes:

> *"**If you are out for Swaraj you must serve untouchables. You won't get Swaraj merely by the repeal of the salt taxes or other taxes. For Swaraj you must make amends for the wrongs which you did to the untouchables. For Swaraj, Hindus, Muslims, Parsis and Sikhs will have to unite. These are the steps towards Swaraj.**"*

> 🎯 **Note the irony NCERT is exploiting: the best surviving record of Gandhi's most radical social message comes from the police spies sent to incriminate him.** That is a Section 6 point delivered inside Section 3.

**What the spies reported:** *"Gandhiji's meetings were **very well attended, by villagers of all castes, and by women as well as men**… **thousands of volunteers were flocking to the nationalist cause**. Among them were **many officials, who had resigned from their posts**."* And the **District Superintendent of Police**, writing to government: *"**Mr Gandhi appeared calm and collected. He is gathering more strength as he proceeds.**"*

### 🤝 3.2 Dialogues — the three reasons the Salt March mattered ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"The Salt March was notable for **at least three reasons**."*

```

               TO WORLD ATTENTION. The march was widely covered by
               the EUROPEAN AND AMERICAN PRESS."

   SECOND  -- "it was the FIRST NATIONALIST ACTIVITY IN WHICH WOMEN
               PARTICIPATED IN LARGE NUMBERS."

                  "had PERSUADED GANDHIJI NOT TO RESTRICT THE PROTESTS
                   TO MEN ALONE." She herself "courted arrest by

   THIRD   -- "and PERHAPS MOST SIGNIFICANT, it was the Salt March
               which FORCED UPON THE BRITISH THE REALISATION THAT
               THEIR RAJ WOULD NOT LAST FOREVER, and that they would
```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Two things to be exact about.**
> **(1) Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay is credited by NCERT with *persuading Gandhi* to include women — women's mass entry into the movement was not simply granted from above, it was argued for.** Learn her name; she is a standard "contributors from different parts of the country" answer.
> **(2) NCERT calls the third reason the "perhaps most significant" one, and it is a cause-and-effect claim: the Salt March → British realisation → Round Table Conferences → GoI Act 1935.** Build your answer along that chain.

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The three challengers ARE the answer to NCERT's own exercise question 7 ("Why were the dialogues at the Round Table Conference inconclusive?").** The deeper answer: **the Congress's claim to speak for all of India was the very thing under dispute**, and the British had every incentive to keep it disputed — *if India was many communities rather than one nation, then the Raj was the necessary arbiter between them.* **The three-way challenge was not merely an obstacle to the talks; it was the British constitutional strategy.**

> 💡 *Beyond the book, for dates:* Gandhi–Irwin Pact = **5 March 1931**, endorsed at the **Karachi Congress (March 1931)** — the session that also passed the **Resolution on Fundamental Rights and Economic Policy**, which is a **direct ancestor of Part III of the Constitution** (⭐ carry this into Theme 12). Second RTC = **September–December 1931**. **Third RTC, November 1932, was boycotted by the Congress** — NCERT does not mention it here.

### ⚔️ Gandhi vs Ambedkar on separate electorates — argue BOTH sides ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is the most important debate in the chapter, and NCERT gives you both men's own words. **A UPSC answer that presents only one side is a weak answer.**

**Source 5 — GANDHI, at the Round Table Conference:**

> *"**Separate electorates to the 'Untouchables' will ensure them bondage in perpetuity**… **Do you want the 'Untouchables' to remain 'Untouchables' for ever? Well, the separate electorates would perpetuate the stigma.** What is needed is **destruction of 'Untouchability'**, and when you have done it, the **bar-sinister**, which has been imposed by an insolent 'superior' class upon an 'inferior' class **will be destroyed**. **When you have destroyed the bar-sinister to whom will you give the separate electorates?**"*

*(NCERT's caption adds his reasoning: he believed separate electorates *"would **prevent their integration into mainstream society and permanently segregate them from other caste Hindus**."*)*

**Source 6 — AMBEDKAR, replying:**

> *"Here is a class which is **undoubtedly not in a position to sustain itself in the struggle for existence**. **The religion, to which they are tied, instead of providing them an honourable place, brands them as lepers, not fit for ordinary intercourse.** **Economically, it is a class entirely dependent upon the high-caste Hindus for earning its daily bread with no independent way of living open to it.** Nor are all ways closed by reason of the social prejudices of the Hindus but **there is a definite attempt all through our Hindu Society to bolt every possible door so as not to allow the Depressed Classes any opportunity to rise in the scale of life.**"*
>
> *"In these circumstances, it would be granted by all fair-minded persons that **as the only path for a community so handicapped to succeed in the struggle for life against organised tyranny, some share of political power in order that it may protect itself is a paramount necessity**…"*

```
   THE STRUCTURE OF THE DISAGREEMENT  -- get this clear, it is subtle

   THEY AGREE ON:  untouchability is an evil and must go.
                   (Gandhi's whole 1924-28 programme was against it.)

   THEY DISAGREE ON:  WHAT KIND OF PROBLEM IT IS

   GANDHI'S FRAME .............. A MORAL AND SOCIAL problem
        -> the disease is the STIGMA
        -> a political device that MARKS the group as separate
           ENTRENCHES the very stigma you are trying to remove
        -> "bondage in perpetuity"; "perpetuate the stigma"
        -> cure = REFORM OF HINDU SOCIETY BY CASTE HINDUS

   AMBEDKAR'S FRAME ............ A PROBLEM OF POWER AND DEPENDENCE
        -> the disease is DEFENCELESSNESS, economic and political
        -> "entirely dependent upon the high-caste Hindus for
            earning its daily bread"
        -> reform promised by the oppressor cannot be relied on:
           "a definite attempt ALL THROUGH our Hindu Society TO
            BOLT EVERY POSSIBLE DOOR"
        -> cure = "SOME SHARE OF POLITICAL POWER IN ORDER THAT IT
                   MAY PROTECT ITSELF"

   ** THE HARD QUESTION UNDERNEATH:
      Can a group be liberated by the goodwill of those who
      oppress it -- or must it hold power of its own?           **
```

> 📌 **NCERT's "Discuss…" box asks you to write an imaginary dialogue between the two.** Do the exercise; it is excellent preparation for **GS-I (social empowerment), GS-II (representation and reservation) and Ethics.**
>
> ⚠️⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **CRITICAL GAP — this chapter does NOT tell you how the dispute ended.** It stops at the argument. The resolution is in **Class 10 Ch.2**: the British **Communal Award (1932)** granted separate electorates to the Depressed Classes; Gandhi began a **fast unto death** in Yerwada jail; the two men settled on the **POONA PACT (September 1932)**, which **gave up separate electorates in exchange for RESERVED SEATS within the general (joint) electorate**. **That compromise is the direct ancestor of SC/ST reservation of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies today (Articles 330 and 332).** **If you answer only from Class 12, you will leave the story unfinished — always append the Poona Pact.**

### 🏛️ 1935–1939 — from protest to office ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Lord Willingdon**, the new Viceroy, *"was **deeply unsympathetic to the Indian leader**."* His private letter to his sister is worth quoting for how the Raj privately saw Gandhi:

> *"It's a beautiful world **if it wasn't for Gandhi**… At the bottom of **every move he makes which he always says is inspired by God, one discovers the political manouevre**. I see the American Press is saying what a wonderful man he is… But the fact is that **we live in the midst of very unpractical, mystical, and superstitious folk who look upon Gandhi as something holy**."*

> 🎯 **Read that against the *Time* reversal and the Fortnightly Reports.** Willingdon's letter is a **private script** (Section 6.1) revealing what public despatches would never say: contempt for Indians as *"unpractical, mystical, and superstitious folk"*. **Three sources, three Gandhis: the saint, the manipulator, and the irrelevance.**

```
   1935 -- A NEW GOVERNMENT OF INDIA ACT
           "promised SOME FORM OF REPRESENTATIVE GOVERNMENT"
                          |
   1937 -- ELECTIONS "held on the basis of a RESTRICTED FRANCHISE"
           "the Congress won a COMPREHENSIVE VICTORY"
           ** "Now EIGHT OUT OF 11 PROVINCES had a Congress
               'PRIME MINISTER', working under the SUPERVISION
               OF A BRITISH GOVERNOR." **
                          |
   SEPT 1939 -- SECOND WORLD WAR BREAKS OUT
           "two years after the Congress ministries assumed office"
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Two phrases to notice, because they contain the whole ambiguity of 1937.** *"**restricted franchise**"* — this was not universal suffrage; only a small propertied fraction voted. And *"**working under the supervision of a British Governor**"* — the ministries held office, not power. **Yet those two years of provincial government are where the Congress learned to administer, and where the men who would write the Constitution learned what a governor's discretionary powers feel like from below.** ⭐ **That experience is a direct input into Theme 12** — and explains why the Constituent Assembly was so careful about the Governor's role.

### 🌍 The war, and the offer that was refused ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Mahatma Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru had **both been strongly critical of Hitler and the Nazis**. Accordingly, they **promised Congress support to the war effort if the British, in return, promised to grant India independence once hostilities ended**. **The offer was refused. In protest, the Congress ministries resigned in October 1939.**"*

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Get the moral shape of this right, because exam options twist it.** The Congress was **not** pro-Axis and **not** indifferent to fascism — NCERT says explicitly that Gandhi and Nehru were **strongly critical of Hitler**. They offered **conditional cooperation**. It was **refused**, and only then did the ministries resign. *(Beyond the book: the constitutional grievance was that the Viceroy, **Linlithgow**, declared India a belligerent **without consulting a single Indian legislature**. Through 1940–41 the Congress ran **individual satyagrahas**; **Vinoba Bhave** was the first individual satyagrahi and **Nehru** the second.)*

### 🔺 March 1940 — the struggle becomes three-cornered ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Meanwhile, in **March 1940**, the **Muslim League passed a resolution demanding a measure of autonomy for the Muslim-majority areas of the subcontinent**."*

```
   BEFORE MARCH 1940                AFTER MARCH 1940

        INDIANS                          CONGRESS
           vs                             /     \
       THE BRITISH                       /       \
                                 MUSLIM LEAGUE -- THE BRITISH

   ** NCERT's own words: "The political landscape was now becoming
      complicated: IT WAS NO LONGER INDIANS VERSUS THE BRITISH;
      rather, it had become A THREE-WAY STRUGGLE BETWEEN THE CONGRESS,

   ** This single sentence is the hinge of the last decade.

```

> 🔴 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Precision point:** NCERT deliberately describes it as *"a measure of **autonomy** for the **Muslim-majority areas**"* — **not** as a demand for a separate state called Pakistan. *(Beyond the book: this is the **Lahore Resolution, 23 March 1940**. The word "Pakistan" **does not appear in its text**; it speaks of "independent states" in the north-western and eastern zones. The press dubbed it the "Pakistan Resolution".)* **An option asserting that the League demanded "Pakistan" in March 1940 is technically wrong, though widely repeated.**

### 🇬🇧 Churchill and the Cripps Mission, 1942 ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"At this time **Britain had an all-party government, whose Labour members were sympathetic to Indian aspirations**, but whose Conservative Prime Minister, **Winston Churchill**, was a **diehard imperialist who insisted that he had not been appointed the King's First Minister in order to preside over the liquidation of the British Empire**."*

> *"In the **spring of 1942**, Churchill was persuaded to send one of his ministers, **Sir Stafford Cripps**, to India to try and forge a compromise with Gandhiji and the Congress."*

**Why the talks broke down — the precise sticking point NCERT gives:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"Talks broke down… **after the Congress insisted that if it was to help the British defend India from the Axis powers, then the Viceroy had first to appoint an INDIAN AS THE DEFENCE MEMBER of his Executive Council.**"*

> 🎯 **Note what the demand actually was: not independence, but *control of defence now*.** The Congress had learned in 1939 that promises for "after the war" were worthless; it wanted **power in hand, not on paper.** *(Beyond the book: Gandhi's celebrated dismissal of the Cripps offer as "a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank" is not in NCERT — attribute it carefully.)*

> 📌 **The internal Congress divide NCERT flags in a caption:** *"**Nehru and Patel represented two distinct political tendencies within the Congress — the socialist and the conservative. Mahatma Gandhi had to often mediate between these groups.**"* **This caption is the bridge to Source 7 in Section 6** — where you actually read Gandhi doing the mediating.

```

                          |
```

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The assessment sentence:**

> *"'**Quit India' was genuinely a mass movement, bringing into its ambit hundreds of thousands of ordinary Indians. It especially energised the young who, in very large numbers, left their colleges to go to jail.**"*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And immediately, the counter-fact — NCERT does not let you celebrate without cost:**

> *"**However, while the Congress leaders languished in jail, Jinnah and his colleagues in the Muslim League worked patiently at expanding their influence. It was in these years that the League began to make a mark in the Punjab and Sind, provinces where it had previously had scarcely any presence.**"*

> 🎯 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is the most important cause-and-effect claim in Section 4, and a favourite Mains angle.** Quit India was a genuine mass upsurge **and** it removed the entire Congress leadership from the political field for three years at exactly the moment the League was building its base in the two provinces that would decide Partition. **The two facts are not in tension — they are the same fact seen from two sides.** A good answer states both.

### 🏴 Satara, 1943 — the *prati sarkar* box ⭐⭐⭐⭐

NCERT gives this its own box, which means it is meant to be known in detail:

```

      NATIONAL MOVEMENT BY THE 1930s."

     PARALLEL GOVERNMENT = "PRATI SARKAR"
        with  SEBA DALS ...... volunteer corps
        and   TUFAN DALS ..... village units
        which "ran PEOPLE'S COURTS and organised CONSTRUCTIVE WORK"

   ITS SOCIAL BASE:
     "DOMINATED BY KUNBI PEASANTS and SUPPORTED BY DALITS"

   HOW LONG IT LASTED:
     "functioned TILL THE ELECTIONS OF 1946, DESPITE GOVERNMENT
      REPRESSION and, IN THE LATER STAGES, CONGRESS DISAPPROVAL"
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Three things make this box exam-worthy, and the third is the subtle one.**
> 1. **Vocabulary:** *prati sarkar*, *seba dal*, *tufan dal* — precise terms, exactly the kind Prelims likes.
> 2. **Social composition:** a nationalist parallel state built by **Kunbi peasants with Dalit support**, growing out of a **non-Brahman anti-caste movement**. **Nationalism here is fused with an anti-caste and anti-landlord programme** — it is not a purely political demand.
> 3. ⭐ **"…and, in the later stages, CONGRESS DISAPPROVAL."** The parallel government outlasted the national leadership's approval of it. **Once again: people acted on the national call "in ways that best suited their interests, rather than conform to the dictates laid down from above."** The Section 2 principle, alive in 1943.
> *(Beyond the book: the Satara prati sarkar is associated with **Nana Patil**. Other parallel governments: **Ballia** under Chittu Pandey and **Tamluk (Midnapore)** — the *Jatiya Sarkar*.)*

## 5️⃣ The Last Heroic Days — 1947–48

### 🕯️ 15 August 1947 — where Gandhi was ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"As it happened, Mahatma Gandhi was **not present at the festivities in the capital** on 15 August 1947. He was in **Calcutta**, but he **did not attend any function or hoist a flag** there either. **Gandhiji marked the day with a 24-HOUR FAST.**"*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**The freedom he had struggled so long for had come at an unacceptable price, with a nation divided and Hindus and Muslims at each other's throats.**"*

> 🎯 **If you learn one image from this chapter, learn this one.** On the day the movement he built achieved its object, its leader **was not there, hoisted no flag, and fasted.** For an essay on leadership, on the cost of victory, or on the gap between political success and moral failure, **nothing in the syllabus is stronger.**

### 🤲 September–October 1947 — "Gandhiji's finest hour"

His biographer **D.G. Tendulkar** records that Gandhi *"**went round hospitals and refugee camps giving consolation to distressed people**"*, and that he *"**appealed to the Sikhs, the Hindus and the Muslims to forget the past and not to dwell on their sufferings but to extend the right hand of fellowship to each other, and to determine to live in peace**…"*

> *"**Many scholars have written of the months after Independence as being Gandhiji's 'finest hour'.**"*

**In Delhi:** *"his meetings were **disrupted by refugees** who **objected to readings from the Koran**, or **shouted slogans asking why he did not speak of the sufferings of those Hindus and Sikhs still living in Pakistan**."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Tendulkar's answer to that charge is worth quoting in full because it captures Gandhi's entire moral method:**

> *"Gandhiji **was equally concerned with the sufferings of the minority community in Pakistan**. He would have liked to be able to go to their succour. **But with what face could he now go there, when he could not guarantee full redress to the Muslims in Delhi?**"*

> 🎓 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This is a first-class Ethics answer in one sentence: your standing to demand justice elsewhere depends on delivering it where you are.** He did not answer the refugees' charge by denying their suffering; he answered it by saying that **the only work available to him was the work at hand.**

### 🕊️ The Congress resolution on minorities ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"At the initiative of **Gandhiji and Nehru**, the Congress now passed a resolution on '**the rights of minorities**'. The party had **never accepted the 'two-nation theory'**: **forced against its will to accept Partition**, it still believed that '**India is a land of many religions and many races, and must remain so**'. Whatever be the situation in Pakistan, India would be '**a democratic secular State where all citizens enjoy full rights and are equally entitled to the protection of the State, irrespective of the religion to which they belong**'. The Congress wished to '**assure the minorities in India that it will continue to protect, to the best of its ability, their citizen rights against aggression**'."*

> 🔗 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **This resolution is the political commitment that Theme 12's Constituent Assembly then had to translate into text** — and Theme 12 shows how contested that translation was, especially on minority rights and separate electorates. **Quote this resolution in any answer on Indian secularism's origins:** note that it is framed as a promise made **at the moment of maximum provocation**, when the case for reciprocity ("whatever be the situation in Pakistan") would have been easiest to make. **The Congress explicitly refused to make Indian citizenship conditional on Pakistan's conduct.**

```
        |    for the 'EQUALITY OF ALL CLASSES AND CREEDS, NEVER THE
        |    DOMINATION AND SUPERIORITY OF THE MAJOR COMMUNITY OVER A
        |    MINOR, HOWEVER INSIGNIFICANT IT MAY BE IN NUMBERS OR
        |    INFLUENCE'."
        |
        |   And the hope: "that THOUGH GEOGRAPHICALLY AND POLITICALLY
        |    INDIA IS DIVIDED INTO TWO, AT HEART WE SHALL EVER BE
        |    FRIENDS AND BROTHERS helping and respecting one another
        |    and BE ONE FOR THE OUTSIDE WORLD."
        |
   30 JANUARY 1948 -- "At his DAILY PRAYER MEETING on the evening of
                       30 January, Gandhiji was SHOT DEAD by a young man.
                       The assassin, WHO SURRENDERED AFTERWARDS, was
                       NATHURAM GODSE."
```

**NCERT's summary of the paradox:** *"Gandhiji had **fought a lifelong battle for a free and united India**; and yet, **when the country was divided, he urged that the two parts respect and befriend one another**. **Other Indians were less forgiving.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"**Other Indians were less forgiving**"* is one of the most quietly devastating sentences in the NCERT corpus — **the assassination is presented as the logical extreme of an unforgivingness that was widespread**, not as the act of one deranged man.

### 🌍 The reception of his death ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Gandhiji's death led to an **extraordinary outpouring of grief**, with **rich tributes being paid to him from across the political spectrum in India**, and **moving appreciations coming from such international figures as George Orwell and Albert Einstein**."*

**And *Time* magazine — the same magazine that had mocked his "spindly frame" — completed its journey:**

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *"[Time] now **compared his martyrdom to that of Abraham Lincoln**: it was **a bigoted American who had killed Lincoln for believing that human beings were equal regardless of their race or skin colour**; and it was **a bigoted Hindu who had killed Gandhiji for believing that friendship was possible, indeed necessary, between Indians of different faiths**. In this respect, as Time wrote, '**The world knew that it had, in a sense too deep, too simple for the world to understand, connived at his (Gandhiji's) death as it had connived at Lincoln's.**'"*

> 🎯 **Note the verb: "connived."** The claim is that a world which tolerates bigotry is complicit in what bigotry eventually does. **That is a usable line for an Essay on tolerance, on the responsibility of bystanders, or on hate speech.**

> 💡 *Beyond the book:* the popular print reproduced in **Fig 11.15** shows Gandhi **deified** — Nehru and Patel, *"representing two strands within the Congress"*, stand on either side of the pyre while Gandhi **blesses them both from a heavenly realm**. **NCERT's caption tells you how to read the image:** *"In popular representations, Mahatma Gandhi was deified, and shown as the **unifying force** within the national movement."* Compare **Fig 11.17**, the **"tree of nationalism"**, in which *"Mahatma Gandhi appears as the **looming central figure surrounded by small images of other leaders and sages**."* ⭐ **Both images are themselves historical sources — they tell you how people wished to see the movement, which is exactly Section 6's lesson applied to pictures.**

## 6️⃣ Knowing Gandhi — the historian's toolkit ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> ⚠️ **DO NOT SKIP THIS SECTION.** It looks like an appendix. It is the reason the chapter exists, it supplies **two of the chapter's nine exercise questions**, and it is the part that most students cannot write about.

> *"There are **many different kinds of sources** from which we can reconstruct the political career of Gandhiji and the history of the nationalist movement."*

```
   FOUR SOURCE TYPES -- and the SPECIFIC LIMITATION of each

   6.1  WRITINGS AND SPEECHES  ..... public voice vs private script
        strength : the person's own words, at the time
        limit    : ** the public/private distinction "OFTEN BREAKS DOWN" **

   6.2  AUTOBIOGRAPHIES ............ rich in human detail
        strength : motive, feeling, self-understanding
        limit    : ** RETROSPECTIVE, written FROM MEMORY,
                      and framed -- look for the SILENCES **

   6.3  GOVERNMENT / POLICE RECORDS  the state watching its subjects
        strength : dense, dated, local, contemporaneous
        limit    : ** report what higher officials "SAW, OR WANTED
                      TO BELIEVE" **

   6.4  NEWSPAPERS ................. contemporary and wide-reaching
        strength : reported daily, in many languages
        limit    : ** "should NOT be seen as UNPREJUDICED" --
                      shaped by the owners' politics **
```

### 📝 6.1 Public voice and private scripts

> *"One important source is the **writings and speeches of Mahatma Gandhi and his contemporaries, including both his associates and his political adversaries**. Within these writings we need to **distinguish between those that were meant for the public and those that were not**."*

| | Reveals |
|---|---|
| **Speeches** | *"allow us to hear the **public voice** of an individual"* |
| **Private letters** | *"give us a glimpse of his or her **private thoughts**"* — *"we see people expressing their **anger and pain, their dismay and anxiety, their hopes and frustrations** in ways in which they may not express themselves in public statements"* |

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **But NCERT immediately complicates it, and this complication is the examinable insight:**

> *"But we must remember that **this private-public distinction often breaks down**. Many letters are written to individuals, and are therefore personal, **but they are also meant for the public**. **The language of the letters is often shaped by the awareness that they may one day be published.** Conversely, **the fear that a letter may get into print often prevents people from expressing their opinion freely** in personal letters."*

**Two proofs that the distinction breaks down — both named in the text:**
- *"Mahatma Gandhi **regularly published in his journal, *Harijan*, letters that others wrote to him**."*
- *"Nehru edited a collection of letters written to him during the national movement and published ***A Bunch of Old Letters***."* *(1958)*

> 🎯 **The point in one line:** a letter written by a public figure is **never fully private**, because the writer knows it may be read by history. **Yet it is still less guarded than a speech.** So the historian gets not "the truth" but **a second, differently-distorted view** — and it is the *difference between the two views* that is informative.

### ✉️ Source 7 — "One event through letters": the Congress crisis of 1936 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**This is the single best illustration in the chapter of what private letters do, and it is also the best evidence for what Gandhi's actual day-to-day role in the Congress was.**

**The background NCERT gives:**

```
   1920s ..... Nehru "increasingly influenced by SOCIALISM"
   1928 ...... returns from Europe "deeply impressed with the SOVIET UNION"
        |      begins working closely with the socialists --
        |      JAYAPRAKASH NARAYAN, NARENDRA DEV, N.G. RANGA and others
        |
        |      "a RIFT developed between the SOCIALISTS and the
        |       CONSERVATIVES within the Congress"
        |
   1936 ...... Nehru becomes CONGRESS PRESIDENT; "spoke passionately
        |      AGAINST FASCISM, and upheld the DEMANDS OF WORKERS
        |      AND PEASANTS"
        |
        |      -> "Worried by Nehru's socialist rhetoric, the
        |          CONSERVATIVES, led by RAJENDRA PRASAD and SARDAR
        |          PATEL, THREATENED TO RESIGN from the Working
        |          Committee, and some prominent INDUSTRIALISTS IN
        |          BOMBAY issued a statement ATTACKING NEHRU."
        |
        v
   "Both Prasad and Nehru turned to Mahatma Gandhi and met him at his
    ashram at WARDHA. THE LATTER ACTED AS THE MEDIATOR, AS HE OFTEN DID,
    RESTRAINING NEHRU'S RADICALISM and PERSUADING PRASAD AND OTHERS TO
    SEE THE SIGNIFICANCE OF NEHRU'S LEADERSHIP."
```

**The three letters — read what each one *reveals* that no speech would:**

**① Rajendra Prasad → Nehru, Wardha, 1 July 1936** *(the grievance of the old guard)*

> *"We understand that you have **felt much hurt** by the course of action taken by us… **It was never our intention either to embarrass you or to hurt you**… we have decided to **withdraw it and our resignation**…"*
>
> *"We have felt that in all your utterances as published in the Press you have been speaking **not so much on the general Congress programme as on a topic which has not been accepted by the Congress** [socialism], and in doing so you have been **acting more as the mouthpiece of the minority of our colleagues… than the mouthpiece of the majority which we expected you as Congress President to do**."*
>
> *"There is **regular continuous campaign against us treating us as persons whose time is over, who represent and stand for ideas that are worn out**… we have felt that **a great injustice has been and is being done to us by others, and we are not receiving the protection we are entitled from you as our colleague and as our President**…"*

**② Nehru → Gandhi ("My Dear Bapu"), Allahabad, 5 July 1936** *(exhaustion and wounded pride)*

> *"I have been **feeling weak in body and troubled in mind**… **meetings of the Working Committee exhaust me greatly; they have a devitalising effect on me and I have almost the feeling of being older in years after every fresh experience**…"*
>
> *"I read again Rajendra Babu's letter to me… and his **formidable indictment of me**… For however tenderly the fact may be stated, **it amounts to this that I am an intolerable nuisance and the very qualities I possess — a measure of ability, energy, earnestness, some personality which has a vague appeal — become dangerous for they are harnessed to the wrong chariot (socialism)**."*
>
> *"Those views are **not casual. They are part of me**, and though I might change them or vary them in future, **so long as I hold them I must give expression to them**. Because I attached importance to **a larger unity** I tried to express them **in the mildest way possible and more as an invitation to thought than as fixed conclusions**… **I was even told that my laying stress always on the poverty and unemployment in India was unwise**…"*

**③ Gandhi → Nehru, Segaon, 15 July 1936** *(the mediator at work — and note the tone)*

> *"**Your letter is touching. You feel the most injured party.** The fact is that **your colleagues have lacked your courage and frankness**… I have always pleaded with them to speak to you freely and fearlessly. But having lacked the courage, whenever they have spoken they have done it clumsily and you have felt irritated. **I tell you they have dreaded you, because of your irritability and impatience with them. They have chafed under your rebukes and magisterial manner and above all your arrogation of what has appeared to them your infallibility and superior knowledge.** They feel you have treated them with scant courtesy and **never defended them from socialist ridicule**…"*
>
> *"I have looked at the whole affair as a **tragi-comedy**. I would therefore like you to **look at the whole thing in a lighter vein**."*
>
> ⭐ *"**I suggested your name for the crown of thorns (Presidentship of the Congress). Keep it on, though the head be bruised.** Resume your humour at the committee meetings. That is your most usual role, not that of care-worn, irritable man ready to burst on the slightest occasion."*
>
> *"How I wish you could telegraph me that on finishing my letter you felt as merry as you were on that **new year's day in Lahore when you were reported to have danced around the tricolour flag**. **You must give your throat a chance.**"*

> 📌 **NCERT's three questions on Source 7 — model answers:**
>
> **(a) *What do the letters tell us about the way Congress ideals developed over time?***
> That the Congress was **never ideologically single**. By 1936 it contained a socialist wing (Nehru, JP Narayan, Narendra Dev, N.G. Ranga) committed to workers' and peasants' demands and to anti-fascism, and a conservative wing (Prasad, Patel) backed by **Bombay industrialists**. Prasad's own complaint — that Nehru was speaking on *"a topic which has not been accepted by the Congress"* — shows that **socialism was being pressed on the organisation from within, and had not yet been absorbed**. ⭐ The Congress's ideals developed **not by conversion but by containment**: rival positions were held together inside one body rather than resolved.
>
> **(b) *What do they reveal about the role of Mahatma Gandhi within the national movement?***
> That by 1936 his authority was **moral and arbitral rather than executive**. He held no office; both sides came to him at Wardha; he **restrained Nehru without disowning him and reassured Prasad without abandoning Nehru**. His method in the letter is instructive: **he does not adjudicate the ideological question at all.** He addresses **temperament** (*"irritability and impatience"*), reframes the crisis as a *"tragi-comedy"*, uses humour, invokes affection, and tells Nehru to **stay on**. ⭐ **He kept the Congress together by refusing to let a disagreement about ideas become a rupture between people.**
>
> **(c) *Do such letters give us any special insight into the working of the Congress, and into the nature of the national movement?***
> **Yes, and specifically what public sources cannot give.** Official resolutions record decisions; these letters record **how the decision was reached** — the threatened resignations, the wounded feelings, the industrialists' pressure, the exhaustion. They show that **the movement was a coalition held together by continuous private negotiation**, and that its unity was **an achievement, not a natural fact**. ⚠️ **But apply Section 6.1's caution:** Nehru **himself edited and published** these letters in 1958, when he was Prime Minister. **We are reading a selection made by one of the participants, long afterwards** — and we cannot know what was left out.
>
> 🎯 **That last caution is what turns a good answer into an excellent one.** Always ask *who preserved this source, and why.*

### 📖 6.2 Framing a picture — autobiographies and their silences ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Autobiographies similarly give us **an account of the past that is often rich in human detail**. But here again we have to be careful of the way we read and interpret autobiographies."*

```
   WHAT AN AUTOBIOGRAPHY ACTUALLY IS

   "RETROSPECTIVE accounts written VERY OFTEN FROM MEMORY"
                          |
   they tell us:  - "what the author COULD RECOLLECT"
                  - "what he or she SAW AS IMPORTANT"
                  - "or was KEEN ON RECOUNTING"
                  - "or HOW A PERSON WANTED HIS OR HER LIFE TO BE
                     VIEWED BY OTHERS"
                          |
   ** "WRITING AN AUTOBIOGRAPHY IS A WAY OF FRAMING A PICTURE
       OF YOURSELF." **
                          |
   THEREFORE the historian's method:
   ** "we have to try and see WHAT THE AUTHOR DOES NOT TELL US;
       we need to understand THE REASONS FOR THAT SILENCE --
       those WILFUL OR UNWITTING ACTS OF FORGETTING." **
```

> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **"Wilful or unwitting acts of forgetting" is the phrase to carry into the exam hall.** It distinguishes **suppression** (wilful) from **the ordinary editing that memory performs on everyone** (unwitting) — and the historian must allow for both without accusing the author of either. **This is also directly transferable to Theme 10 (the 1857 sources) and to any question about oral history or memoir evidence.**

### 👮 6.3 Through police eyes — the Fortnightly Reports ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"Another vital source is **government records**, for **the colonial rulers kept close tabs on those they regarded as critical of the government**. The letters and reports written by policemen and other officials **were secret at the time; but now can be accessed in archives**."*

**The specific series NCERT uses:** *"the **fortnightly reports** that were prepared by the **Home Department** from the **early twentieth century**."*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **The two-layer distortion — this is the analytically hardest and most rewarding idea in Section 6:**

> *"These reports were **based on police information from the localities**, **but often expressed what the higher officials saw, or wanted to believe**. **While noticing the possibility of sedition and rebellion, they liked to assure themselves that these fears were unwarranted.**"*

```
   HOW THE DISTORTION ENTERS -- two layers, not one

   LAYER 1 : the LOCAL POLICEMAN
             reports what he observes -- but has an interest in not
             appearing to have lost control of his district
                          |
   LAYER 2 : the SENIOR OFFICIAL in the Home Department
             compiles the reports -- and has an interest in not
             appearing to preside over a crumbling Raj
                          |
                          v
   RESULT for the SALT MARCH period:
   "the Home Department was UNWILLING TO ACCEPT that Mahatma Gandhi's
    actions had EVOKED ANY ENTHUSIASTIC RESPONSE FROM THE MASSES.
    The march was seen as ** A DRAMA, AN ANTIC, A DESPERATE EFFORT **
    to mobilise people who were UNWILLING TO RISE against the British
    and were BUSY WITH THEIR DAILY SCHEDULES, HAPPY UNDER THE RAJ."

   ** MEANWHILE, IN THE SAME WEEKS, NEARLY 60,000 INDIANS WERE
      BEING ARRESTED. **
```

**Source 8 — the Fortnightly Reports themselves. Read them for the *tone*, then note how the tone changes:**

**FIRST HALF OF MARCH 1930 — the dismissive register:**

| Province | What the report says | What it is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **Gujarat/general** | *"The peasantry is **for the moment engaged in harvesting a good rabi**; students are **pre-occupied with their impending examinations**"* | ⭐ Explaining away non-participation **before it has been tested** |
| **Central Provinces & Berar** | *"The arrest of Mr. Vallabh Bhai Patel caused **little excitement, except in Congress circles**, but a meeting… was attended by **a crowd of over 3000 people at Nagpur**"* | ⭐⭐ **The sentence contradicts itself** — "little excitement" and "over 3000 people" in one breath |
| **Bengal** | *"beyond **forming councils** no active steps have been taken"*; *"the meetings… **excite little or no interest and leave no profound impression**"* | Minimising — **yet immediately adds:** *"It is noticeable, however, that **ladies are attending these meetings in increasing numbers**"* ⭐ *(the officer records the very thing that made the Salt March historic, and files it as an aside)* |
| **Bihar & Orissa** | *"There is **still little to report**"* — but *"a good deal of talk about a campaign to **withhold payment of the chaukidari tax**"*; *"The **arrest of Gandhi is being foretold freely**"* | Anxiety leaking through the reassurance |
| **Madras** | *"General opinion inclines to regard his march as **theatrical** and his programme as **impracticable**, **but as he is held in such personal reverence by the Hindu public generally**, the possibility of arrest… **viewed with considerable misgiving**"* | ⭐⭐⭐ **The clearest single admission: they cannot afford to arrest him and cannot afford not to** |
| **Bombay** | *Kesari* (militant): *"**If it arrests Gandhi it will incur the discontent of the nation; if it does not do that, the movement of civil disobedience will go on spreading… if the Government punishes Mr. Gandhi the nation will have won a victory, and if it lets him alone it will have won a still greater victory.**"* / *Vividh Vritt* (moderate): the movement is futile, **but "repression would defeat its purpose"** | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ *Kesari* states **the satyagrahi's trap** perfectly |

**SECOND HALF OF MARCH 1930 — the hedging:**

> **Bengal:** *"Interest has continued to centre round Gandhi's march… But there is **little enthusiasm** for the form of civil disobedience favoured by Gandhi… **Generally people are waiting to see what happens to Gandhi and the probability is that if any action is taken against him, A SPARK WILL BE SET TO MUCH INFLAMMABLE MATERIAL IN BENGAL. But the prospect of any serious conflagration is at present slight.**"*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Read that sentence twice. It says there is inflammable material everywhere, a spark is likely, and there is nothing to worry about — all in one breath. That is the entire psychology of the colonial archive in a single line.**

> **Central Provinces:** *"most of the schools and colleges were **deserted** on the 12th March"*; the likely lines of attack are *"the **boycott of liquor shops** and the **infringement of forest laws**"*.
> **Punjab:** anticipated breaking of the salt law in **Jhelum**, revival of the **non-payment of water-tax in Multan**, a **National Flag** movement at **Gujranwala**.
> **United Provinces:** *"Political activity has **undoubtedly intensified**… The Congress party feels that it must do something spectacular to sustain public interest."*

**FIRST HALF OF APRIL 1930 — the dam breaks:**

> **United Provinces:** *"**Events have moved rapidly** during the fortnight… the **Salt Act has been openly defied at Agra, Cawnpore, Benaras, Allahabad, Lucknow, Meerut, Rae Bareli, Farukhabad, Etawah, Ballia and Mainpuri.**"* — **eleven towns named in one sentence.** And: *"**Pt. Jawaharlal Nehru was arrested at Cheoki railway station early on the morning of April 14**… sentenced to **six months simple imprisonment**."*
> **Bihar & Orissa:** *"**spectacular, but small-scale**, attempts at illicit salt manufacture"* ⭐ *(note the effort to keep the adjective "small-scale" attached)*
> **Central Provinces:** *"In Jubbalpore **Seth Govinddass** has attempted to manufacture chemical salt **at a cost many times in excess of the market price of clean salt**."* ⭐⭐ *(the official thinks this proves the movement is absurd; it actually proves the opposite — **people were spending more to make salt than to buy it, because the point was never the salt**)*
> **Madras:** *"**Considerable opposition** was shown at **Vizagapatam** to the Police when they attempted to seize salt made by boiling sea water, but elsewhere resistance… has been **half hearted**."*
> **Bengal:** illicit salt in **24-Parganas** and **Midnapore**; *"**Very little salt has actually been manufactured** and most of it has been **confiscated and the utensils in which it was manufactured destroyed**."*

> 📌 **NCERT's four questions on Source 8 — model answers. These are near-certain Mains-style prompts:**
>
> **(1) *How does the nature of the source affect what is being said?***
> These are **confidential internal reports**, so they are **not propaganda for the public** — which makes their minimising **more revealing, not less**. Because they were written *upward*, each officer had an interest in appearing **in control**. Hence the systematic pattern: the movement is *"theatrical"*, *"impracticable"*, exciting *"little or no interest"*, its acts *"small-scale"* and resistance *"half hearted"* — while the same paragraphs record **3,000 at Nagpur, deserted colleges, eleven UP towns defying the Salt Act, and rising attendance by women.** ⭐ **The adjectives are the officials' opinion; the nouns and numbers are the evidence. Read past the adjectives.**
>
> **(2) *Why was the Home Department continuously reporting on what people thought about the possibility of Gandhi's arrest?***
> Because **the arrest decision was the government's central strategic problem**, and it needed to forecast the consequences. *Kesari* had stated the dilemma publicly: **arrest him and the nation is inflamed; leave him and civil disobedience spreads.** Gandhi had **deliberately created this dilemma** — his Dandi speech of 5 April says the government *"could have arrested every one of us"*, congratulates it for not doing so, and then removes the incentive entirely: *"**when a whole nation is roused and on the march no leader is necessary**."* ⭐ **The reports are the Raj trying to price an action whose price Gandhi had already made unpayable.**
>
> **(3) *Why do you think Mahatma Gandhi was not arrested [during the march]?***
> Because arresting him **before** the law was broken would have (a) made him a martyr over an act he had not yet committed, (b) confirmed the nationalist claim that the Raj feared him, (c) inflamed Bengal and Punjab where the reports themselves admit *"much inflammable material"*, and (d) drawn **hostile world opinion** — the European and American press were already covering the march. The Raj preferred to let the march appear to fizzle. **It miscalculated: the walk itself, not the arrest, was the event.** *(He was in fact arrested afterwards — on 5 May 1930.)*
>
> **(4) *Why did the Home Department continue to say the march was evoking no response?***
> Partly **self-deception**, partly **institutional self-protection**, partly **ideology**. The Raj's legitimacy rested on the belief that Indians were *"busy with their daily schedules, happy under the Raj"* and that agitation was the work of a small disaffected class. **To admit a mass response was to admit that the premise of British rule was false.** ⭐ **Officials were not simply lying to London; they were protecting a story they needed to believe about themselves.**

### 📰 6.4 From newspapers ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> *"One more important source is **contemporary newspapers, published in English as well as in the different Indian languages**, which tracked Mahatma Gandhi's movements and reported on his activities, and also **represented what ordinary Indians thought of him**."*

**The caution:**

> *"Newspaper accounts, however, **should not be seen as unprejudiced**. They were **published by people who had their own political opinions and world views**. These ideas **shaped what was published and the way events were reported**. **The accounts that were published in a London newspaper would be different from the report in an Indian nationalist paper.**"*
>
> *"**Every statement made in these cannot be accepted literally as representing what was happening on the ground.** They often **reflect the fears and anxieties of officials who were unable to control a movement and were anxious about its spread.** They **did not know whether to arrest Mahatma Gandhi or what an arrest would mean.**"*

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **And then the closing sentence of the whole chapter — the one line to carry out of Section 6:**

> *"**The more the colonial state kept a watch on the public and its activities, the more it worried about the basis of its rule.**"*

```
   THE PARADOX OF SURVEILLANCE -- why this line is so good

   A CONFIDENT state does not need to file a report every fortnight
   on what peasants in Basti are saying about a spinning wheel.
                          |
   The VOLUME of the archive is itself EVIDENCE OF ANXIETY.
                          |
   ** So the colonial records are useful to the historian TWICE:
      once for WHAT THEY SAY (facts, dates, places, arrests)
      and once for WHAT THEIR EXISTENCE PROVES
      (a regime that had stopped feeling secure).                    **

   -> This is why the historian reads the Fortnightly Reports for the
      SALT MARCH not to learn whether people responded -- we know
      they did -- but to learn HOW BADLY THE RAJ NEEDED TO BELIEVE
      THEY HADN'T.
```

> 📌 **NCERT's question on Fig 11.16** (*police clashing with Congress volunteers in Bombay during Civil Disobedience*): *"Can you see any conflict between this image and what was reported in the Fortnightly Reports of the police?"* → **Yes, directly.** The reports insist on *"little or no interest"*, *"half hearted"* resistance and *"small-scale"* attempts, while the photograph shows **organised volunteers in sufficient numbers to require a police charge**. **The image is contemporaneous evidence that contradicts the written record produced by the very force in the picture.** ⭐ **This is the cleanest possible demonstration of why a historian never relies on a single source-type.**

### 🔴 Prelims traps — the claim vs the truth

| ❌ The tempting claim | ✅ The truth |

### 🟢 Mains angles

1. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ ***"Individuals, even great ones, are made by history even as they make history."* Discuss with reference to Mahatma Gandhi.**
   → The chapter's own thesis. **Made history:** transformed nationalism from *"a movement of professionals and intellectuals"* into a mass movement; invented a repertoire (satyagraha, khadi, salt) that made participation possible for the illiterate and the poor; held together a coalition from Birla to the Awadh tenant. **Made by history:** he returned to an India **already** politicised by Swadeshi with an existing Congress network; the **British handed him the Rowlatt issue**; his authority was constructed as much by **peasant rumour** as by his own acts; and his **greatest failure — Partition — was produced by forces (the 1946 mandate, the three-cornered struggle) he could not bend.**

2. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **In what way did Mahatma Gandhi transform the nature of the national movement?** *(NCERT Q8)*
   → Four mechanisms, in this order: **identification** (dress, language, manual labour, the 1921 loincloth); **symbols with a low threshold of participation** (charkha, salt, khadi); **the moral programme** (non-violence, untouchability, Hindu–Muslim harmony — *"to be worthy of freedom"*); and ⭐ **deliberate organisation** (branches, Praja Mandals in the princely states, mother-tongue communication, linguistic provincial committees). Result: *"no longer a movement of professionals and intellectuals."* **Include the honest qualification:** the breadth was purchased by leaving the peasant's and the industrialist's *"perhaps opposed"* interests unreconciled.

3. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why did the salt laws become an important issue of struggle?** *(NCERT Q3)*
   → **Universality** (indispensable in every household, across caste, class, region, faith) + **absurdity of the prohibition** (forbidden to make what nature gives free) + **Gandhi's fourfold curse** (destroys a village industry; wanton destruction of natural property; the destruction itself costs the exchequer; a tax of **over 1,000%** on a starving people) + **actionability** (a law any person could break, publicly, at no cost). **Close with the consequences:** ~60,000 arrests, world attention, women's mass entry, and the British realisation that the Raj *"would not last forever."*

4. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **What do private letters and autobiographies tell us about an individual? How are these sources different from official accounts?** *(NCERT Q9)*
   → **Letters:** anger, pain, dismay, hopes, frustrations — *but* the private/public distinction *"often breaks down"* because writers know they may be published (*Harijan*; *A Bunch of Old Letters*). **Autobiographies:** rich human detail, motive and self-understanding — *but* retrospective, from memory, and a way of *"framing a picture of yourself"*; read for **silences**. **Official accounts:** dense, dated and local — *but* record what senior officials *"wanted to believe"*, and are **written upward within a hierarchy that punishes admissions of lost control.** ⭐ **The strongest closing point: no source is transparent; the historian's method is to read them against each other. The Fortnightly Reports and Fig 11.16 are the demonstration.**

5. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **How was Mahatma Gandhi perceived by the peasants?** *(NCERT Q2)*
   → As **"Gandhi baba", "Gandhi Maharaj", "Mahatma"** — *"a **saviour** who would rescue them from high taxes and oppressive officials and restore **dignity and autonomy**."* Use **Shahid Amin**, the three rumour families, and the Gorakhpur newspaper scene (15,000–20,000 at wayside stations, 10,000 at a jungle halt, *darshan*, notes and coins raining). ⭐ **Then add the analytical layer:** the rumours reveal a peasant political imagination working through **just kingship** rather than republican citizenship, and they **enforced the constructive programme** (charkha, liquor, gambling) in villages Gandhi never reached.

6. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why are newspapers an important source for the study of the national movement?** *(NCERT Q4)*
   → Contemporaneous; published in **English and Indian languages**; tracked movements day by day; **represented what ordinary Indians thought**; and captured **foreign opinion** (*Time*). ⚠️ **But** *"should not be seen as unprejudiced"* — the same magazine called Gandhi a *"spindly frame"* and, **one week later**, a *"Saint and Statesman"*; *Kesari* and *Vividh Vritt* reported the same march to opposite conclusions. **Their bias is itself evidence — of the range of contemporary opinion.**

7. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **How was non-cooperation a form of protest?** *(NCERT Q6)*
   → It attacked **consent, not the state's capacity**: a *"renunciation of all voluntary association"* — schools, courts, taxes, titles, foreign cloth. Quote **Louis Fischer**: *"negative enough to be peaceful but positive enough to be effective… **training for self-rule**."* **Show the scale** (396 strikes, 600,000 workers, 7 million workdays; Andhra forest laws, Awadh taxes, Kumaun *begar*) and **the loss of control** (*"in defiance of the local nationalist leadership"*), which is what made **Chauri Chaura** — and the withdrawal — inevitable in principle.

8. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Why were the dialogues at the Round Table Conference inconclusive?** *(NCERT Q7)*
   → **1st RTC:** held without Gandhi — *"an exercise in futility."* **2nd RTC:** the Congress's claim to represent all India was challenged by **three** parties (League, Princes, Ambedkar). ⭐ **The structural answer:** the conferences were built on the premise that India was **an assembly of communities and interests requiring British arbitration**, while the Congress came as **the representative of a nation**. **Those two premises cannot produce an agreement.** Add Willingdon's private contempt as evidence that the British side was not negotiating in good faith.

9. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Examine the Gandhi–Ambedkar debate on separate electorates for the Depressed Classes.**
   → Present **both** positions from Sources 5 and 6 in their own terms (stigma-and-integration vs power-and-self-protection), state the **underlying question** (can a group be liberated by the goodwill of its oppressors?), then **complete the story with the Communal Award and the Poona Pact (1932)** and trace the line to **reserved seats under Articles 330/332**. ⭐ **Conclude even-handedly:** the Poona Pact preserved a single electorate — Gandhi's core concern — while conceding guaranteed representation — Ambedkar's core concern; both men regarded it as a compromise rather than a victory.

10. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **Assess the Quit India Movement.**
    → **Achievements:** genuinely mass, energised the young, produced **parallel governments** (Satara, Medinipur), required **over a year** of force to suppress, and made clear the Raj could no longer govern with consent. **Costs:** the entire Congress leadership was removed from the field for three years, during which **the League built its base in Punjab and Sind** — *"provinces where it had previously had scarcely any presence."* ⭐ **The judgement:** Quit India made independence certain and Partition likelier.

11. ⭐⭐⭐⭐ **[Ethics] Was Gandhi right to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement after Chauri Chaura?**
    → Use the two-sided treatment in §2 above. Frame it as a **means–ends** question, note the **collective-punishment objection**, and resolve it by pointing out that the withdrawal **purchased the moral capital that made 1930 possible**. Quote him directly: *"No provocation can possibly justify the brutal murder of men who had been rendered defenceless."*

12. ⭐⭐⭐ **[Essay/GS-IV] "The freedom he had struggled so long for had come at an unacceptable price."**
    → 15 August 1947: no function, no flag, a 24-hour fast in Calcutta. Then the *"finest hour"* — hospitals, refugee camps, the Delhi meetings disrupted by refugees, and Tendulkar's line: *"with what face could he now go there, when he could not guarantee full redress to the Muslims in Delhi?"* **A meditation on the gap between political victory and moral defeat.**

## 🔑 Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Satyagraha** | The technique of **non-violent protest** Gandhi *"first forged"* in South Africa — literally "insistence on truth"; **active**, not passive, resistance |
| **Bandh** | A general shutdown — shops closed, schools closed — the form the Rowlatt protest took |
| **Khilafat** | The movement (**1919–20**) led by the **Ali brothers** to preserve the Turkish **Khalifa's** control over Muslim sacred places, the *jazirat-ul-Arab*, and sufficient territory to defend the faith |
| ***Jazirat-ul-Arab*** | **Arabia, Syria, Iraq, Palestine** — the region the Khilafatists demanded remain under Muslim sovereignty |
| **Pan-Islamism** | The idea of the political solidarity of Muslims worldwide, of which the Caliphate was a symbol |
| **Non-cooperation** | *"A renunciation of (all) voluntary association with the (British) Government"* — boycott of schools, colleges, courts, and refusal to pay taxes |
| **Charkha** | The **spinning wheel**; symbol of a society *"that would not glorify machines"*, a source of **supplementary income and self-reliance**, and a breaker of the **mental/manual labour** divide |
| **Khadi / khaddar** | **Home-spun, hand-woven cloth**; wearing it was the economic leg of the constructive programme |
| **Purna Swaraj** | **Complete independence** — proclaimed as the Congress goal at **Lahore, December 1929** |
| **Praja Mandal** | Bodies established to spread the nationalist creed **in the princely states** |
| **Bar-sinister** | An old heraldic mark of illegitimacy — Gandhi's metaphor for the **stigma** of untouchability *"imposed by an insolent 'superior' class upon an 'inferior' class"* |
| **Separate electorates** | An electoral arrangement in which a community votes **only for its own candidates in its own seats**; demanded by Ambedkar for the Depressed Classes, opposed by Gandhi |
| **Depressed Classes** | The term of the period for the communities now designated **Scheduled Castes** |
| ***Prati sarkar*** | The **parallel government** set up in **Satara**, 1943 |
| ***Seba dal*** | **Volunteer corps** of the Satara *prati sarkar* |
| ***Tufan dal*** | **Village units** of the Satara *prati sarkar* |
| **Kunbi** | The peasant caste that dominated the Satara *prati sarkar* |
| **Chaukidari tax** | The village-watchman tax; a Bihar/Orissa target for non-payment in 1930 |
| **Fortnightly Reports** | **Confidential** summaries prepared by the **Home Department** from police information in the localities — a central source, and a deeply skewed one |
| **Direct Action Day** | **16 August 1946**, called by Jinnah to press the demand for Pakistan; triggered the Calcutta riots |
| **Cabinet Mission** | The **summer 1946** British mission that failed to secure Congress–League agreement on a **federal system** |
| ***Harijan*** | Gandhi's journal, in which he **published letters written to him** |
| ***A Bunch of Old Letters*** | Nehru's **edited collection (1958)** of letters written to him during the national movement |
| ***Maund*** | A traditional unit of weight — used in Gandhi's account of the salt destroyed on the Konkan coast |
