## 🎬 Start Here: 12 August 1765

> On **12 August 1765**, the Mughal emperor appointed the East India Company as the **Diwan of Bengal**.

⭐ **A detail worth noticing** — NCERT points it out deliberately:

> *"The actual event most probably took place in **Robert Clive's tent**, with a few Englishmen and Indians as witnesses. But in the painting, the event is shown as a **majestic occasion, taking place in a grand setting**. The painter was **commissioned by Clive** to record the memorable events in Clive's life."*

> 🎯 **A tent redrawn as a throne room.** This is Chapter 1's lesson about images working immediately: the picture is not evidence of the ceremony but evidence of **how Clive wanted it remembered.**

### The Company's new problem

```
   As DIWAN, the Company became the CHIEF FINANCIAL ADMINISTRATOR
   of the territory under its control.

   NOW IT HAD TO:
     - administer the land
     - organise its revenue resources
     - in a way that would yield enough revenue to meet its
       GROWING EXPENSES
     - AND, as a trading company, ensure it could BUY the products
       it needed and SELL what it wanted

   BUT ALSO -- and this is the political constraint:
   "Being an ALIEN POWER, it needed to PACIFY those who in the past
    had ruled the countryside and enjoyed authority and prestige.
    Those who had held local power HAD TO BE CONTROLLED BUT THEY
    COULD NOT BE ENTIRELY ELIMINATED."
```

> ⭐ **That last constraint explains the Permanent Settlement.** The Company could not simply sweep away the existing rural elite — it needed them as collectors and as a buffer. **Every settlement in this chapter is an attempt to solve "extract maximum revenue without provoking rebellion."**

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐ The Bengal Crisis and the Famine of 1770

> The Company **"had become the Diwan, but it still saw itself primarily as a TRADER. It wanted a large revenue income but was UNWILLING TO SET UP ANY REGULAR SYSTEM of assessment and collection."**

```
   THE STRATEGY: increase revenue as much as possible, and buy
   fine cotton and silk cloth as CHEAPLY as possible

   *** Within FIVE YEARS, the value of goods bought by the Company
       in Bengal DOUBLED. ***

   (Before 1765 those goods were paid for with gold and silver
    from Britain; now Bengal's own revenue financed them -- Ch.2)
                              |
                              v
   THE CONSEQUENCES, in NCERT's words:

     - "ARTISANS WERE DESERTING VILLAGES since they were being
        FORCED TO SELL THEIR GOODS TO THE COMPANY AT LOW PRICES"
     - "PEASANTS were unable to pay the dues demanded from them"
     - "ARTISANAL PRODUCTION WAS IN DECLINE, and agricultural
        cultivation showed SIGNS OF COLLAPSE"
                              |
                              v
   *** THEN IN 1770, A TERRIBLE FAMINE KILLED TEN MILLION PEOPLE
       IN BENGAL. ABOUT ONE-THIRD OF THE POPULATION WAS WIPED OUT. ***
```

> ⭐⭐⭐ **The Bengal Famine of 1770 is one of the most important facts in Modern Indian history, and the numbers are asked directly: TEN MILLION dead, ONE-THIRD of the population.**
>
> 🎯 **And note the sequence NCERT constructs.** The famine follows immediately after a description of **maximum extraction with no system of assessment**, **forced sales at low prices**, and **peasants unable to pay**. The book does not say "the Company caused the famine" — but it places the two paragraphs side by side, and the argument is unmistakable. **A drought became a catastrophe because the revenue demand did not fall when the harvest did.**
>
> 💻 A system running at maximum extraction has **no headroom**. Any shock becomes a total failure because all the slack has already been consumed.

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The Permanent Settlement, 1793

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Year** | **1793** |
| ⭐⭐ **Governor-General** | **CHARLES CORNWALLIS** |
| **Preceded by** | *"**Two decades of debate** on the question"* |
| **The reasoning** | *"If the economy was in ruins, could the Company be certain of its revenue income?"* Officials felt **investment in land had to be encouraged and agriculture improved** |

### ⭐⭐⭐ The terms

```
   [1] The RAJAS and TALUQDARS were recognised as *** ZAMINDARS ***

   [2] They were asked to COLLECT RENT FROM THE PEASANTS
       and PAY REVENUE TO THE COMPANY

   [3] *** THE AMOUNT TO BE PAID WAS FIXED PERMANENTLY --
       "it was not to be increased ever in future" ***
```

**⭐ The intended logic:**

> This would **ensure a regular flow of revenue** into the Company's coffers **and at the same time encourage the zamindars to invest in improving the land** — since the revenue demand would not be increased, **the zamindar would keep the benefit of any increased production.**

> 💻 **The design intent was to create an incentive to invest.** Fix the state's claim permanently, and every extra rupee of output accrues to the improver. **In theory, a rational landlord would now develop his estate.** It is not a stupid idea. It simply did not work.

### ⚠️ ⭐⭐⭐ Why it failed — three failures, three victims

```
   FAILURE 1 -- FOR THE ZAMINDARS (early years)
   "The revenue that had been fixed was SO HIGH that the zamindars
    found it DIFFICULT TO PAY."
   "Anyone who FAILED TO PAY the revenue LOST HIS ZAMINDARI."
   *** "NUMEROUS ZAMINDARIS WERE SOLD OFF AT AUCTIONS
       organised by the Company." ***

   FAILURE 2 -- FOR THE COMPANY (after c. 1800)
   "By the first decade of the nineteenth century... PRICES IN THE
    MARKET ROSE and CULTIVATION SLOWLY EXPANDED."
   -> "an INCREASE IN THE INCOME OF THE ZAMINDARS but
       *** NO GAIN FOR THE COMPANY, since it could not increase
           a revenue demand that had been FIXED PERMANENTLY. ***"

   FAILURE 3 -- THE INVESTMENT NEVER CAME
   "Even then the zamindars did NOT have an interest in improving
    the land."
     - some had LOST their lands in the early years
     - others "now saw the possibility of EARNING WITHOUT THE
       TROUBLE AND RISK OF INVESTMENT"
   *** "As long as the zamindars could GIVE OUT THE LAND TO TENANTS
       AND GET RENT, they were NOT INTERESTED IN IMPROVING it." ***
```

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is the answer to "critically examine the Permanent Settlement", and the shape of it is elegant:**
>
> **It failed twice, in opposite directions.** In the first decade the demand was **too high** and ruined the zamindars. After 1800 it was **too low** and cheated the Company. **The one thing it never did was produce the agricultural investment that justified it.**
>
> 💻 **The incentive was real but the easier path was rent-seeking.** Why take the risk of improving land when you can simply lease it out and collect? **The scheme assumed the landlord would behave like an entrepreneur; he behaved like a rentier.**

### ⭐⭐ And the cultivator — the worst outcome

> *"On the other hand, in the villages, the cultivator found the system **EXTREMELY OPPRESSIVE**. The **rent he paid to the zamindar was HIGH** and **his right on the land was INSECURE**. To pay the rent he had to often **TAKE A LOAN FROM THE MONEYLENDER**, and when he failed to pay the rent, **he was EVICTED from the land he had cultivated for generations.**"*

> ⭐ **Note the three-part trap: high rent → debt → eviction.** This is the origin of the **rural indebtedness** that defines the Indian countryside right into the twentieth century, and it is why moneylenders become a target in later peasant movements.
>
> ⭐ **The under-tenants were worse off still.** In 1806 **H. T. Colebrook** described how powerful ryots in Bengal did not cultivate but **leased their land to under-tenants at very high rents**, so that these under-tenants *"depressed by an excessive rent in kind, and by usurious returns for the cattle, seed, and subsistence advanced to them, **can never extricate themselves from debt**... they cannot labour in spirit, while they earn a scanty subsistence **without hope of bettering their situation**."*

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The Mahalwari Settlement, 1822

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Who** | **HOLT MACKENZIE**, an Englishman |
| ⭐⭐ **Year in effect** | **1822** |
| ⭐⭐ **Where** | The **North Western Provinces of the Bengal Presidency** — *"most of this area is now in **Uttar Pradesh**"* |
| ⭐ **His premise** | *"He felt that the **VILLAGE was an important social institution** in north Indian society and **needed to be PRESERVED**."* |

**⭐⭐ The method:**

```
   Under his directions, COLLECTORS went from VILLAGE TO VILLAGE:
        - INSPECTING the land
        - MEASURING the fields
        - RECORDING the customs and rights of different groups
                            |
                            v
   The estimated revenue of EACH PLOT within a village was ADDED UP
   to calculate the revenue that each village (*** MAHAL ***) had to pay
                            |
                            v
   *** THIS DEMAND WAS TO BE REVISED PERIODICALLY,
       NOT PERMANENTLY FIXED. ***
                            |
                            v
   *** The charge of COLLECTING the revenue and paying it to the
       Company was given to THE VILLAGE HEADMAN,
       RATHER THAN THE ZAMINDAR. ***
```

> ⭐⭐ **Two deliberate reversals of the Permanent Settlement — and both are exam points:**
> 1. **Periodic revision instead of permanent fixing** → the Company would not be locked out of rising prices again
> 2. **The village headman instead of the zamindar** → no intermediary landlord class created
>
> ⭐ ***MAHAL*** = *"in British revenue records, a **revenue estate** which may be a **village or a group of villages**."* Learn this definition.

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The Ryotwari (Munro) System

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Who devised it** | Tried on a small scale by ⭐ **CAPTAIN ALEXANDER READ**, in areas taken over after the wars with **Tipu Sultan**; ⭐ **subsequently developed by THOMAS MUNRO** |
| ⭐⭐ **Where** | **Gradually extended all over SOUTH INDIA**. *(Munro was **Governor of Madras, 1819–26**)* |
| ⭐⭐ **The premise** | *"Read and Munro felt that **in the south there were NO TRADITIONAL ZAMINDARS**."* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The terms** | *"The settlement had to be made **DIRECTLY WITH THE CULTIVATORS (RYOTS)** who had tilled the land for generations. Their fields had to be **carefully and SEPARATELY SURVEYED** before the revenue assessment was made."* |
| ⭐ **The ideology** | *"Munro thought that the British should act as **PATERNAL FATHER FIGURES protecting the ryots** under their charge."* |

> 🎯 **The paternalism line is worth quoting.** It captures the self-image of the colonial administrator perfectly — and it sits very awkwardly beside what actually happened next.

### ⚠️ ⭐⭐ "All was not well" — the common failure

> *"Within a few years after the new systems were imposed, it was clear that **all was not well** with them. Driven by the desire to increase the income from land, **revenue officials FIXED TOO HIGH A REVENUE DEMAND**. **Peasants were unable to pay, RYOTS FLED THE COUNTRYSIDE, and VILLAGES BECAME DESERTED in many regions.** Optimistic officials had imagined that the new systems would **transform the peasants into rich enterprising farmers. BUT THIS DID NOT HAPPEN.**"*

> ⭐⭐ **This is the single most important sentence in the chapter for a Mains answer.** All three systems — permanent, periodic, and direct-with-the-cultivator — **failed in the same way and for the same reason: the demand was set too high**, because the purpose was always revenue maximisation, not agricultural improvement. **The institutional design varied; the extractive intent did not.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The Three Settlements — Comparison Table

**Memorise this. It is the highest-yield table in the book.**

| | **PERMANENT SETTLEMENT** | **MAHALWARI** | **RYOTWARI** |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Year** | **1793** | **1822** | Early 19th c. (Read → Munro) |
| ⭐ **Associated with** | **Cornwallis** | **Holt Mackenzie** | **Alexander Read**, **Thomas Munro** |
| ⭐ **Where** | **Bengal** (Bengal Presidency) | **North Western Provinces** (mostly UP) | **South India** (Madras Presidency) |
| ⭐⭐ **Revenue paid by** | **ZAMINDARS** (rajas and taluqdars) | **The VILLAGE (mahal)**, through the **village headman** | **The RYOT (cultivator) directly** |
| ⭐⭐ **Demand fixed?** | **PERMANENTLY — never to be increased** | **REVISED PERIODICALLY** | Periodically revised |
| **Unit of assessment** | The zamindari estate | The **mahal** — a village or group of villages | The **individual field**, separately surveyed |
| **Rationale** | Encourage zamindars to invest | **Preserve the village** as a social institution | **No traditional zamindars** in the south; deal with the tiller |
| ⚠️ **Outcome** | Zamindaris **auctioned off**; Company **locked out of rising prices**; **no investment**; cultivators **evicted and indebted** | Demand still **too high** | Demand still **too high**; **ryots fled**, villages **deserted** |

> 🧠 **Memory hook: "Corn-Perm-Bengal · Mack-Mahal-UP · Munro-Ryot-South."**
> And the paying party: **zamindar / village / cultivator.**

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐ Crops for Europe

> *"The British also realised that the countryside could not only yield revenue, it could also **grow the crops that EUROPE REQUIRED**."*

By the late eighteenth century the Company was expanding **opium** and **indigo**. In the century and a half that followed, the British **"persuaded or forced"** cultivators to produce:

| Crop | Region |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Jute** | **Bengal** |
| ⭐ **Tea** | **Assam** |
| ⭐ **Sugarcane** | **United Provinces** (now Uttar Pradesh) |
| ⭐ **Wheat** | **Punjab** |
| ⭐ **Cotton** | **Maharashtra and Punjab** |
| ⭐ **Rice** | **Madras** |

> 🧠 **Learn these six pairings — they are a stock matching question.** And note the phrase **"persuaded or forced"**: NCERT is signalling that this was the **commercialisation of agriculture under compulsion**, not a market response.
>
> ⭐ **The consequence worth stating in Mains:** land shifted from **food for the cultivator** to **cash crops for export**, which raised vulnerability to famine and tied the Indian peasant to distant price movements he could not see or influence.

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐ Why Europe Wanted Indian Indigo

**A genuinely interesting story of global economics — and NCERT tells it well.**

```
   THE COLOUR PROBLEM

   INDIGO grows primarily in the TROPICS.
   By the 13th century Indian indigo was used by cloth
   manufacturers in ITALY, FRANCE and BRITAIN.
                            |
   BUT: "only SMALL AMOUNTS of Indian indigo reached the European
    market and ITS PRICE WAS VERY HIGH."
                            |
                            v
   So European manufacturers depended on *** WOAD *** --
   a plant of the TEMPERATE zones, easily available in Europe,
   grown in northern Italy, southern France, parts of Germany
   and Britain.
                            |
                            v
   *** WOAD PRODUCERS, worried by the competition, PRESSURISED
       THEIR GOVERNMENTS TO BAN THE IMPORT OF INDIGO. ***
                            |
                            v
   BUT CLOTH DYERS PREFERRED INDIGO:
     - indigo produced a RICH BLUE colour
     - the dye from woad was PALE AND DULL
                            |
                            v
   By the 17th century European cloth producers persuaded their
   governments to RELAX the ban.
                            |
                            v
   Plantations sprang up: the FRENCH in ST DOMINGUE (Caribbean),
   the PORTUGUESE in BRAZIL, the ENGLISH in JAMAICA,
   the SPANISH in VENEZUELA, and across NORTH AMERICA.
```

### ⭐⭐ The supply collapse of the 1780s — and India's opening

| Development | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Demand rose** | *"Britain began to **INDUSTRIALISE**, and its **cotton production expanded dramatically**, creating an **enormous new demand for cloth dyes**"* |
| ⭐ **Supply collapsed** | *"Between **1783 and 1789**, the production of indigo in the world **FELL BY HALF**"* |
| ⭐ **Why** | In **St Domingue**, the **African slaves who worked the plantations rose in rebellion in 1791**, burning the plantations and killing the planters; **France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1792**. These events **collapsed the Caribbean indigo plantations** |

**⭐⭐ The result for India:**

```
   "From the last decades of the 18th century, indigo cultivation
    in BENGAL EXPANDED RAPIDLY and Bengal indigo came to
    DOMINATE THE WORLD MARKET."

        1788  ->  only about *** 30 PER CENT *** of the indigo
                  imported into Britain was from India
        1810  ->  the proportion had gone up to
                  *** 95 PER CENT ***
```

> ⭐⭐ **The 30% → 95% figures are directly asked.** And notice the causal chain, which is superb Mains material: **a slave revolt in the Caribbean in 1791 → the collapse of Atlantic indigo → forced indigo cultivation on Bengali peasants.** Colonial economies were a single connected system; a rebellion in Haiti reshaped agriculture in Bengal.

**Who became planters:** *"commercial agents and officials of the Company began investing in indigo production. Over the years **many Company officials left their jobs** to look after their indigo business. Attracted by the prospect of high profits, **numerous Scotsmen and Englishmen came to India and became planters**. Those who had no money could get **loans from the Company and the banks**."*

## 7️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ *Nij* vs *Ryoti* — How Indigo Was Cultivated

**A guaranteed comparison question.**

| | ⭐ ***NIJ* CULTIVATION** | ⭐ ***RYOTI* CULTIVATION** |
|---|---|---|
| **Whose land** | Land the **planter directly controlled** — bought, or rented from zamindars | ⭐ **The ryot's own land** |
| **Who works it** | **Directly employed hired labourers** | The **ryot and his family** |
| **Share of production** | ⭐ **Less than 25%** of indigo land | ⭐ **The rest — the great majority** |

### ⚠️ ⭐⭐ Why *nij* could not expand — four obstacles

```
   [1] LAND
       Indigo needed FERTILE land -- and fertile land was
       ALREADY DENSELY POPULATED. Only "small plots scattered
       over the landscape" could be acquired, but planters needed
       "LARGE AREAS IN COMPACT BLOCKS".
       Attempts to lease land around the factory and EVICT the
       peasants "ALWAYS LED TO CONFLICTS AND TENSION".

   [2] LABOUR
       "A large plantation required a VAST NUMBER OF HANDS...
        and labour was needed PRECISELY AT A TIME WHEN PEASANTS
        WERE USUALLY BUSY WITH THEIR RICE CULTIVATION."

   [3] PLOUGHS AND BULLOCKS
       *** One BIGHA of indigo required TWO PLOUGHS.
           A planter with 1,000 bighas would need 2,000 PLOUGHS. ***
       Investment in purchase and maintenance was a big problem.

   [4] TIMING AGAIN
       Peasants' own ploughs and bullocks were busy on their rice
       fields "exactly at the time that the indigo planters
       needed them".
```

> ⭐ **The 1 bigha : 2 ploughs ratio (so 1,000 bighas : 2,000 ploughs) is a favourite detail.** *(A **bigha** was standardised by the British in Bengal to about **one-third of an acre**.)*
>
> 🎯 **Notice that obstacles [2] and [4] are the same conflict:** indigo and rice **compete for the same labour, ploughs and bullocks at the same moment in the year.** Indigo could only expand by **taking those resources away from food production** — which is exactly why the peasant resisted.

### ⭐⭐⭐ The *ryoti* system — the debt trap

```
   [1] Planters FORCED the ryots to sign a contract, an
       agreement (*** SATTA ***). At times they pressurised the
       VILLAGE HEADMEN to sign on behalf of the ryots.

   [2] Those who signed got *** CASH ADVANCES at low rates of
       interest *** to produce indigo.

   [3] BUT the loan committed the ryot to cultivating indigo on
       *** AT LEAST 25 PER CENT of the area under his holding. ***

   [4] The planter provided the SEED and the DRILL; the cultivators
       PREPARED THE SOIL, SOWED THE SEED and LOOKED AFTER THE CROP.

   [5] When the crop was delivered after harvest,
       *** A NEW LOAN WAS GIVEN, AND THE CYCLE STARTED ALL OVER AGAIN. ***

              "The price they got for the indigo they produced was
               VERY LOW and *** THE CYCLE OF LOANS NEVER ENDED. ***"
```

**⭐⭐ And two further injuries:**

| Problem | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Best land taken** | *"The planters usually insisted that indigo be cultivated on the **BEST SOILS**, in which peasants preferred to cultivate **RICE**."* |
| ⭐⭐ **Soil exhaustion** | *"Indigo had **DEEP ROOTS** and it **EXHAUSTED THE SOIL RAPIDLY**. **After an indigo harvest the land COULD NOT BE SOWN WITH RICE.**"* |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **That last line is the crux of the whole grievance, and it is the answer to "why were ryots reluctant to grow indigo?"**
>
> It was not merely that indigo paid badly. **Growing indigo destroyed the field's ability to grow food.** The peasant was being paid a low price, in a loan he could never repay, to **permanently damage his own means of subsistence.**
>
> 💻 **The contract extracted a one-way, irreversible cost from the counterparty** while the planter bore none of it — and the loan structure made refusal impossible. **A debt instrument used as a coercion mechanism.**

### 🏭 How indigo was produced — the three vats ⭐

```
   [1] *** FERMENTING (STEEPER) VAT ***
       Leaves stripped from the plant are SOAKED IN WARM WATER
       for several hours. As they ferment the liquid BOILS AND
       BUBBLES. Rotten leaves removed; liquid drained into...

   [2] *** BEATER VAT ***
       The solution is CONTINUOUSLY STIRRED AND BEATEN WITH PADDLES.
       As it turns GREEN and then BLUE, LIME WATER is added.
       Indigo separates out in FLAKES; a MUDDY SEDIMENT settles
       at the bottom; CLEAR LIQUID rises to the surface.
       The liquid is drained off and the sediment -- the indigo
       pulp -- transferred to...

   [3] *** SETTLING VAT ***
       Then PRESSED and DRIED for sale.
```

> ⭐ **The human cost of step 2:** the vat-beaters *"had to remain in **WAIST-DEEP WATER FOR OVER EIGHT HOURS** to beat the indigo solution."* Women usually carried the plant to the vats.

## 8️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The "Blue Rebellion", March 1859

### What happened

> *"In **March 1859**, **thousands of ryots in Bengal REFUSED TO GROW INDIGO**."*

| The action |
|---|
| Ryots **refused to pay rents** to the planters |
| **Attacked indigo factories armed with swords and spears, bows and arrows** |
| ⭐ **"WOMEN TURNED UP TO FIGHT with pots, pans and kitchen implements"** |
| Those who worked for the planters were **socially boycotted** |
| The ***gomasthas*** — agents of planters — who came to collect rent **were beaten up** |
| Ryots swore they would **no longer take advances** nor be bullied by the planters' ***lathiyals*** — the **lathi-wielding strongmen** maintained by the planters |

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why 1859? — NCERT's own question, and the answer

> *"Clearly, the indigo system was intensely oppressive. **But those who are oppressed DO NOT ALWAYS RISE UP IN REBELLION. They do so only at times.**"*

**Three enabling conditions:**

| # | Condition |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **1. Local elite support** | *"The indigo ryots felt they had the support of the **LOCAL ZAMINDARS AND VILLAGE HEADMEN**."* Headmen who had been **forced to sign indigo contracts mobilised the peasants** and **fought pitched battles with the lathiyals**. In other places **zamindars went around villages urging the ryots to resist** — they were **unhappy at the increasing power of the planters** and angry at being **forced to give them land on long leases** |
| ⭐⭐ **2. Belief that government would back them** | *"The indigo peasants also imagined that **the British government would SUPPORT them**."* ⭐ **After the Revolt of 1857, the government was particularly worried about the possibility of another popular rebellion.** When news spread of unrest, **the Lieutenant Governor toured the region in the winter of 1859**, and the ryots read the tour as **sympathy** |
| ⭐⭐ **3. The Ashley Eden notice** | At **Barasat**, the magistrate ⭐ **ASHLEY EDEN** issued a notice stating **ryots would not be compelled to accept indigo contracts**. ⭐ *"Word went around that **QUEEN VICTORIA had declared that indigo need not be sown**."* Eden was trying to **placate the peasants and control an explosive situation, but his action was read as SUPPORT FOR THE REBELLION"* |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is a beautifully constructed explanation and outstanding Mains material.** Oppression is necessary but not sufficient for revolt. **What changed in 1859 was the perception of ALLIES and of IMPUNITY** — the peasants believed the zamindars were with them and that the state would not crush them. **1857 had made the government cautious, and that caution was read as permission.**
>
> 🎯 **The rumour that "Queen Victoria had declared indigo need not be sown" is especially telling** — a technical magistrate's notice transmuted, as it travelled, into a royal sanction. **Rumour as a mobilising force** is a theme you will meet again in Chapter 5 (the chapatis and the greased cartridges).

### 📰 And the intellectuals

> *"As the rebellion spread, **intellectuals from CALCUTTA rushed to the indigo districts**. They wrote of **the misery of the ryots, the tyranny of the planters, and the horrors of the indigo system**."*

*(The most famous product of this was Dinabandhu Mitra's play* **Nil Darpan** *— not named in the rationalised text, but standard knowledge and worth carrying.)*

### ⚖️ ⭐⭐ The Indigo Commission

| Government response | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Force** | *"The government brought in the **MILITARY to protect the planters** from assault"* |
| ⭐⭐ **Inquiry** | *"...and set up the **INDIGO COMMISSION** to enquire into the system of indigo production"* |

**⭐⭐ The Commission's findings:**

```
   [1] It HELD THE PLANTERS GUILTY
   [2] It CRITICISED THEM for the COERCIVE METHODS they used
   [3] It declared that *** INDIGO PRODUCTION WAS NOT PROFITABLE
       FOR RYOTS ***
   [4] It asked the ryots to FULFIL THEIR EXISTING CONTRACTS
       but told them they could *** REFUSE TO PRODUCE INDIGO
       IN FUTURE ***
```

**⭐ And the peasant's own voice — Hadji Mulla before the Commission, 5 June 1860:**

> **Seton Karr:** *"Are you now willing to sow indigo; and if not, on what fresh terms would you be willing to do it?"*
> **Hadji Mulla:** *"I am not willing to sow, and **I don't know that any fresh terms would satisfy me**."*
> **Mr Sale:** *"Would you not be willing to sow at a rupee a bundle?"*
> **Hadji Mulla:** *"No I would not; rather than sow indigo **I will go to another country; I WOULD RATHER BEG THAN SOW INDIGO.**"*

> ⭐⭐ **Quote this.** It is a rare instance of a peasant speaking **in his own words, in an official record** — precisely the voice Chapter 1 said the archive normally omits. And his answer refuses the entire premise of the question: **there is no price at which this is acceptable.**

>
> 🔗 **Chapter 3 → Chapter 8.** When you write about Champaran in the national movement, **begin here**: it was not a random first campaign, it was the culmination of a **150-year-old struggle over a single crop.**

## 🔁 2-MINUTE RECAP

1. **12 August 1765** — the Company became **Diwan**, and therefore had to administer land and revenue, while being an **alien power that could control but not eliminate the old rural elite**.
2. It maximised revenue with **no proper system of assessment**, forced artisans to sell cheap, and within five years **doubled** its purchases. Then came the **famine of 1770 — ten million dead, one-third of Bengal**.
3. ⭐ **Permanent Settlement, 1793, Cornwallis:** rajas and taluqdars became **zamindars**, collected rent, paid a **permanently fixed** revenue — meant to encourage **investment**.
4. ⭐ **It failed three ways:** the demand was **too high** at first (zamindaris **auctioned off**); after 1800 prices rose and the Company **could not raise a fixed demand**; and **zamindars never invested**, preferring rent without risk. **The cultivator paid high rent, borrowed, and was evicted.**
5. ⭐ **Mahalwari, 1822, Holt Mackenzie, North Western Provinces:** the **village (mahal)** was the unit, the **village headman** collected, and the demand was **periodically revised** — both deliberate reversals of 1793.
6. ⭐ **Ryotwari, Read then Munro, South India:** **no traditional zamindars** there, so settle **directly with the ryot**, with fields **separately surveyed**; Munro cast the British as **paternal father figures**.
7. ⚠️ **All three failed the same way** — officials **fixed too high a demand**, **ryots fled**, and villages were **deserted**. The peasants never became "rich enterprising farmers".
8. **Crops for Europe:** jute (Bengal), tea (Assam), sugarcane (UP), wheat (Punjab), cotton (Maharashtra and Punjab), rice (Madras) — grown because cultivators were **"persuaded or forced"**.
9. **Indigo:** Europe had only **woad**, which was **pale and dull**; woad growers had lobbied to **ban** indigo. Industrialising Britain's demand soared just as world production **halved (1783–89)** after the **St Domingue slave revolt of 1791**. India's share of British indigo imports went from **30% (1788) to 95% (1810)**.
10. ⭐ ***Nij*** (planter's own land, hired labour) stayed **under 25%** because land, labour, ploughs and bullocks all clashed with the **rice** cycle. ***Ryoti*** dominated: a ***satta***, a **cash advance**, a commitment to plant indigo on **25% of the holding**, a **low price**, and **a new loan every year**.
11. ⭐⭐ **Indigo took the best soil and, with its deep roots, exhausted it — after an indigo harvest the land could not grow rice.**
12. ⭐ **March 1859 — the Blue Rebellion.** Ryots refused to sow, attacked factories, beat the ***gomasthas***; **women fought with pots and pans**. They rose because they believed **zamindars and headmen backed them** and that **the government, nervous after 1857, would not crush them** — a belief confirmed in their eyes by **Ashley Eden's notice at Barasat**, rumoured to be **Queen Victoria's order**.
13. ⭐ **The Indigo Commission held the planters guilty**, declared indigo **unprofitable for ryots**, and allowed refusal in future. **Hadji Mulla: "I would rather beg than sow indigo."**
14. ⭐⭐ **Production collapsed in Bengal — and moved to Bihar.** ⭐ **Gandhi's visit in 1917 began the Champaran movement**, the first satyagraha in India.
