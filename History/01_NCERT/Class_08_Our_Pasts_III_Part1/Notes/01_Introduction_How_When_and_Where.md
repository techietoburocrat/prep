# Chapter 1 — Introduction: How, When and Where

> **The one-line point:** The British wrote down **everything** — and that is both the historian's greatest resource and their biggest trap. This chapter teaches you to read colonial records **against the grain**, and it demolishes **James Mill's** periodisation for good.

## 🎬 Start Here: When Did Indians Start Drinking Tea?

NCERT opens with a deceptively simple question. **You cannot answer it with a date.**

> *"People in India did not begin drinking tea one fine day; they developed a taste for it over time. **There can be no one clear date for a process such as this.**"*

And the same is true of the things this book is actually about:

```
   THINGS THAT HAVE NO SINGLE DATE

     - the establishment of British rule
     - the start of the national movement
     - changes within the economy and society

   "All these things happened over a STRETCH of time.
    We can only refer to a SPAN of time, an APPROXIMATE
    PERIOD over which particular changes became visible."
```

### ⭐⭐ So why is history still associated with dates?

> Because *"there was a time when history was an account of **battles and big events**. It was about **rulers and their policies**."* Historians wrote the year a king was crowned, married, fought, died, and was succeeded.

**But NCERT's key methodological point is about *selection*:**

> ⭐ **"The dates we select... are not important on their own. They become vital because we focus on a particular set of events as important. IF OUR FOCUS OF STUDY CHANGES, IF WE BEGIN TO LOOK AT NEW ISSUES, A NEW SET OF DATES WILL APPEAR SIGNIFICANT."**

**The worked example NCERT gives:**

```
   BRITISH HISTORIES OF INDIA were organised around the
   rule of each GOVERNOR-GENERAL:

   Warren Hastings -> Wellesley -> Bentinck -> Dalhousie ->
   Canning -> Lawrence -> Lytton -> Ripon -> Curzon ->
   Harding -> Irwin ... ending with the last Viceroy,
   Lord MOUNTBATTEN

   "All the dates in these history books were linked to these
    PERSONALITIES -- to their activities, policies and
    achievements. It was as if there was NOTHING OUTSIDE
    THEIR LIVES that was important for us to know."
                            |
                            v
   "In the histories that revolve around the life of British
    Governor-Generals, THE ACTIVITIES OF INDIANS SIMPLY DO
    NOT FIT, THEY HAVE NO SPACE."
```

> 💻 **The chapter structure determined what could be recorded.** Organise the narrative around eleven officials and there is **no field in the schema** for a peasant revolt, a weaver's decline or a reform movement. **Changing the questions requires changing the framework, not just adding facts.**
>
> ⭐ **Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General in 1773** — a fact worth carrying.

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ James Mill and the Problem of Periodisation

**The most examinable section of the chapter.**

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Who** | ⭐ **JAMES MILL**, a **Scottish economist and political philosopher** |
| ⭐ **What and when** | In **1817** he published a massive **three-volume** work, ***A History of British India*** |
| ⭐⭐ **His periodisation** | He divided Indian history into three periods: **HINDU, MUSLIM and BRITISH** |
| **Its reception** | *"This periodisation came to be widely accepted."* |

### ⭐⭐ Mill's actual argument — know it, because the critique depends on it

> - He thought **"all Asian societies were at a lower level of civilisation than Europe"**
> - Before the British came, *"**Hindu and Muslim despots** ruled the country"*
> - *"**Religious intolerance, caste taboos and superstitious practices** dominated social life"*
> - ⭐ **British rule, Mill felt, could CIVILISE India** — by introducing **European manners, arts, institutions and laws**
> - ⭐ He therefore **suggested the British should conquer ALL the territories of India** "to ensure the enlightenment and happiness of the Indian people"
> - *"For **India was not capable of progress without British help**."*

```
   IN THIS IDEA OF HISTORY:

   British rule        =  ALL the forces of PROGRESS and CIVILISATION
   The period before   =  ONE OF DARKNESS
```

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is the intellectual foundation of the "civilising mission"** — the claim that conquest was a service. Mill's book was a **textbook for East India Company officials**, so this was not private opinion; **it was the training material of the colonial administration.**

### ⭐⭐ The objections — NCERT's own questions, answered

| # | Objection |
|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐ **Can we refer to any period as "Hindu" or "Muslim"?** *"Did not a variety of faiths exist simultaneously in these periods?"* |
| **2** | ⭐ **Why characterise an age only through the religion of the rulers?** *"To do so is to suggest that **the lives and practices of the others do not really matter**."* |
| **3** | ⭐ **"Even rulers in ancient India did not all share the same faith."** |
| **4** | The scheme was built to justify conquest — it makes British rule the **necessary** cure for a diseased past |

> 🔗 **You met this critique from the medieval side in Class 7 Ch.1.** Here you get **who made it, when, in what book, and why** — the full case. **Learn Mill's name and the year 1817.**

### ⭐⭐ And the problem with "ancient / medieval / modern" too

> Moving away from the British classification, historians usually divide Indian history into **ancient, medieval and modern**. ⭐ **"This division too has its problems."**

| The problem |
|---|
| It is **borrowed from the West**, where **"modern"** was associated with **science, reason, democracy, liberty and equality** |
| **"Medieval"** described a society where those features **did not exist** |
| ⭐⭐ **But under British rule "people did not have equality, freedom or liberty. NOR WAS THE PERIOD ONE OF ECONOMIC GROWTH AND PROGRESS."** |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **Hence: "Many historians therefore refer to this period as COLONIAL."**
>
> 🎯 **This is a superb, precise point and directly usable.** Calling 1750–1947 "modern" **imports a claim of progress that the evidence contradicts**. "Colonial" describes the period by **its actual defining relationship** — subjugation — rather than by a borrowed and flattering label. **Periodisation is never neutral** (Class 7 Ch.1), and here is the proof applied to our own textbooks.

### ⭐⭐ What "colonisation" means — NCERT's definition

> **"When the subjugation of one country by another leads to these kinds of POLITICAL, ECONOMIC, SOCIAL and CULTURAL changes, we refer to the process as COLONISATION."**

**The specific changes listed:** the British **subjugated local nawabs and rajas**, **established control over the economy and society**, **collected revenue to meet all their expenses**, **bought the goods they wanted at low prices**, **produced crops they needed for export**, and changed **values and tastes, customs and practices**.

### ⭐ Why "Our PastS" — plural

> **"You will, however, find that ALL CLASSES AND GROUPS DID NOT EXPERIENCE THESE CHANGES IN THE SAME WAY. That is why the book is called Our Pasts IN THE PLURAL."**

> 🎯 **A one-line title explaining an entire methodological stance.** There is no single Indian experience of colonialism — the zamindar, the weaver, the tribal, the widow and the sepoy each lived a different one. **Quote this when a question asks about the "impact of British rule".**

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ How Do We Know? — The Colonial Archive

### 📋 Administration produces records

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **The belief** | *"The British believed that **the act of writing was important**. Every instruction, plan, policy decision, agreement, investigation had to be **clearly written up**"* — so things could be **properly studied and debated** |
| **The culture it produced** | ⭐ *"An administrative culture of **memos, notings and reports**"* |
| ⭐ **Record rooms** | Attached to **all** administrative institutions — the **village tahsildar's office, the collectorate, the commissioner's office, the provincial secretariats, the lawcourts** |
| **Specialised institutions** | ⭐ **Archives and museums** established to preserve important records |
| ⭐ **Calligraphists** | In the **early nineteenth century** documents were **copied out and beautifully written by calligraphists** — specialists in the art of beautiful writing |
| ⭐ **Printing** | **By the middle of the nineteenth century**, with the spread of printing, **multiple copies were printed as proceedings of each government department** |
| **The National Archives** | ⭐ Came up in the **1920s**; when New Delhi was built, the **National Museum and National Archives were both located close to the Viceregal Palace** — *"this location reflects the importance these institutions had in British imagination"* |

> 🎯 **That last detail is worth pausing on.** The archive was placed **beside the seat of power**, physically. **Knowledge and rule were understood to belong together** — which is exactly the argument of the next section.
>
> 💻 The colonial state ran on **mandatory written logging of every decision, replicated and centrally retained**. It is an information system of remarkable completeness for its era — and like any log, it records **what its designers chose to instrument**.

### 🗺️ ⭐⭐ Surveys become important

> ⭐ **"The British believed that a country had to be PROPERLY KNOWN before it could be EFFECTIVELY ADMINISTERED."**

| Survey | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mapping** | By the **early nineteenth century**, detailed surveys were mapping the **entire country** |
| ⭐ **Revenue surveys** | In the villages — recording **topography, soil quality, flora, fauna, local histories and cropping patterns** |
| ⭐⭐ **CENSUS** | **From the end of the nineteenth century, held EVERY TEN YEARS**; prepared **detailed records of the number of people in all provinces**, noting **caste, religion and occupation** |
| **Other surveys** | ⭐ **Botanical, zoological, archaeological, anthropological, forest** surveys |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **The census deserves special attention, because its consequences were enormous and it is heavily asked in GS1 Indian Society.**
>
> Counting people **by caste and religion** did not merely *record* Indian society — it **reshaped it**. Fluid, locally variable jati identities (Class 7 Ch.1 and Ch.5!) were **fixed into countable, all-India categories**. Communities began to petition for higher classification. **Caste became a national political fact partly because the state made it a national statistical fact.**
>
> 💻 **The act of measurement altered the thing measured.** Defining the schema forced a messy reality into rigid enumerable categories — and people then organised around the categories.

### ⭐ The "map as domination" point

**James Rennel** was asked by **Robert Clive** to produce maps of Hindustan. NCERT notes he was *"an enthusiastic supporter of British conquest of India"* who **"saw preparation of maps as essential to the process of domination."**

The frontispiece to his 1782 map shows **Brahmans offering the Shastras to Britannia** — the image *"tries to suggest that Indians **willingly gave over their ancient texts** to Britannia... as if asking her to become the **protector of Indian culture**."*

> 🎯 **A picture doing political work.** It converts **conquest into voluntary entrustment**. NCERT's instruction — *"images need to be carefully studied for they project the viewpoint of those who create them"* — applies to the famous 1857 illustration too, captioned "Mutinous sepoys share the loot", in which *"the rebels appear as greedy, vicious and brutal."*

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ What Official Records Do NOT Tell

**The heart of the chapter.**

> **"From this vast corpus of records we can get to know a lot, but we must remember that these are OFFICIAL records. They tell us WHAT THE OFFICIALS THOUGHT, WHAT THEY WERE INTERESTED IN, and WHAT THEY WISHED TO PRESERVE FOR POSTERITY. These records do not always help us understand what OTHER PEOPLE in the country FELT, and WHAT LAY BEHIND THEIR ACTIONS."**

### The alternative sources

```
   WHEN WE SEARCH ELSEWHERE, "we find them in plenty,
   though they are MORE DIFFICULT TO GET than official records":

     - DIARIES of people
     - accounts of PILGRIMS and TRAVELLERS
     - AUTOBIOGRAPHIES of important personalities
     - POPULAR BOOKLETS sold in local bazaars
     - NEWSPAPERS (as printing spread, issues were debated in public)
     - writings of LEADERS and REFORMERS
     - POETS and NOVELISTS expressing their feelings
```

### ⚠️ ⭐⭐ But even these have a limit — the crucial caveat

> **"All these sources, however, were produced by THOSE WHO WERE LITERATE. From these, we will NOT be able to understand how history was experienced and lived by the TRIBALS and the PEASANTS, the WORKERS IN THE MINES or the POOR ON THE STREETS. Getting to know their lives is a MORE DIFFICULT TASK."**
>
> ⭐ And then, encouragingly: **"Yet this CAN be done, if we make a little bit of effort."**

> 🎯 **This is the same "silence of the sources" lesson you met in Class 6 Ch.1 and Class 7 Ch.5 — but sharpened.** In ancient India the record was thin. Here the record is **enormous** — and that abundance is itself a danger, because it **feels comprehensive while systematically omitting the majority**.
>
> ⭐⭐ **The sophisticated formulation for a Mains answer:** *"The colonial archive is not merely incomplete; it is **structured by the priorities of the state that produced it**. Reading it 'against the grain' — for what officials feared, misunderstood or dismissed — is what allows the historian to recover the experience of those it does not describe."*
>
> 💻 **A rich log is not a neutral one.** High volume creates false confidence in coverage.

### ⭐ The two contrasting sources NCERT prints

| Source 1 — **Home Department reports, 1946** | Source 2 — ***Hindustan Times*, 22 March 1946** |
|---|---|
| On the **Royal Indian Navy mutiny**: *"Bombay: Arrangements have been made for the Army to take over ships... **Karachi: 301 mutineers are under arrest**... All establishments are under military guard. **Vizagapatnam: The position is completely under control and no violence has occurred.**"* | On a **police strike in Delhi**: *"More than 2000 policemen... refused to take their food... as a protest against their **low salaries and the bad quality of food**... One of the strikers said: **'The food supplied to us... is not fit for human consumption. Even cattle would not eat the chappattis and dal which we have to eat.'**"* |
| **Voice:** administrative. Counts arrests, reports control restored. **No reason for the mutiny is given.** | **Voice:** the striker's own. **Gives the grievance and the reason.** |

> ⭐⭐ **NCERT's own activity asks you to compare them, and the contrast is the lesson of the chapter.** The official report tells you **that** order was restored; the newspaper tells you **why people revolted**. **The archive records the state's response; other sources record the cause.**

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Processes have no single date** — nobody began drinking tea, or being colonised, on a particular morning.
2. **The dates we treat as important depend on the questions we ask.** British histories organised around eleven Governors-General left **no room for Indians at all**.
3. ⭐ **James Mill, 1817, *A History of British India*, three volumes** — divided Indian history into **Hindu, Muslim, British**, held Asian societies to be less civilised, and argued **British rule would civilise India**.
4. **The objections:** many faiths coexisted; characterising an age by the ruler's religion implies **everyone else does not matter**; even ancient rulers differed in faith; and the scheme exists to **justify conquest**.
5. ⭐ **"Ancient/medieval/modern" is also borrowed and misleading** — under British rule there was **no equality, liberty or economic growth**, so many historians say **COLONIAL**.
6. **Colonisation** = subjugation producing **political, economic, social and cultural** change. **Our Pasts is plural because groups experienced it differently.**
7. **The British wrote everything down** — memos, notings, reports — kept in **record rooms** everywhere, copied by **calligraphists** and later **printed**; the **National Archives** came up in the **1920s** beside the Viceregal Palace.
8. ⭐ **"A country had to be properly known before it could be effectively administered"** — hence mapping, revenue surveys, and the **ten-yearly census** recording **caste, religion and occupation**.
9. ⭐⭐ **Official records tell us what officials thought and wished preserved** — not what others felt or why they acted.
10. **Alternative sources** (diaries, travellers, autobiographies, bazaar booklets, newspapers, reformers, poets) help — **but all were produced by the literate**, so tribals, peasants, miners and the urban poor remain hardest to reach.
