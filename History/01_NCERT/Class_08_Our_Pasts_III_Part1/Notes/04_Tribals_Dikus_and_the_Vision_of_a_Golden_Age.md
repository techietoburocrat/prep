# Chapter 4 — Tribals, Dikus and the Vision of a Golden Age

> **The one-line point:** Colonial rule needed **settled, countable, taxable subjects**. Tribal India was **mobile, forest-based and outside the revenue net** — so it was reclassified, restricted and dispossessed. **Birsa Munda's answer was a golden age and a Munda Raj.**

## 🎬 Start Here: 1895, Chottanagpur

> *"In **1895**, a man named **BIRSA** was seen roaming the forests and villages of **Chottanagpur in Jharkhand**. People said he had **miraculous powers** — he could cure all diseases and multiply grain. Birsa himself declared that **God had appointed him to save his people from trouble, free them from the slavery of DIKUS (outsiders)**. Soon thousands began following Birsa, believing that he was ***bhagwan*** and had come to solve all their problems."*

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| **His people** | Born into a family of ⭐ **MUNDAS**, a tribal group of **Chottanagpur** |
| **His following** | Included other tribals of the region — ⭐ **SANTHALS and ORAONS** |
| ⭐ **The grievance** | *"Their **familiar ways of life** seemed to be disappearing, their **livelihoods** were under threat, and their **religion** appeared to be in danger."* |

> ⭐ ***DIKU*** = **outsider**. The chapter's whole argument is about **who became an outsider on land that had been tribal**, and how.
>
> 🔗 **Class 7 Ch.5 recap NCERT gives:** most tribes had **customs and rituals very different from those laid down by Brahmans**, and **lacked the sharp social divisions of caste society** — all members shared **common ties of kinship**. ⚠️ **But NCERT adds a caveat worth keeping:** *"this did not mean that there were **no social and economic differences within tribes**."*

## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐ How Tribal Groups Lived — Four Livelihoods

### 🌾 Some were *jhum* (shifting) cultivators ⭐⭐

```
   THE JHUM CYCLE, step by step

   [1] Done on SMALL PATCHES of land, MOSTLY IN FORESTS
   [2] Cultivators CUT THE TREETOPS to allow SUNLIGHT to reach
       the ground
   [3] BURNT the vegetation on the land to clear it
   [4] SPREAD THE ASH from the firing -- it CONTAINED POTASH --
       to FERTILISE the soil
   [5] Used the AXE to cut trees and the HOE to scratch the soil
   [6] *** BROADCAST the seeds *** -- i.e. SCATTERED them on the
       field INSTEAD OF PLOUGHING the land and sowing
   [7] Once the crop was harvested, they MOVED TO ANOTHER FIELD
   [8] A field cultivated once was LEFT *** FALLOW *** for
       several years
```

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Broadcasting** | **Scattering** seeds instead of ploughing and sowing |
| ⭐ **Fallow** | A field **left uncultivated for a while so the soil recovers fertility** |
| ⭐ ***Bewar*** | The term used in **Madhya Pradesh** for shifting cultivation |

**Where:** *"Shifting cultivators were found in the **hilly and forested tracts of NORTH-EAST and CENTRAL India**."*

> ⭐⭐ **The critical dependency, and the reason the forest laws were devastating:**
>
> *"The lives of these tribal people depended on **FREE MOVEMENT WITHIN FORESTS** and on **being able to use the land and forests for growing their crops**. **That is the ONLY way they could practise shifting cultivation.**"*
>
> **Restrict movement and you do not merely inconvenience them — you abolish their agriculture.**

### 🏹 Some were hunters and gatherers ⭐

**The KHONDS** of the forests of **Orissa**:

| Practice |
|---|
| Went out on ⭐ **collective hunts** and **divided the meat amongst themselves** |
| Ate **fruits and roots** from the forest |
| ⭐ Cooked with **oil extracted from the seeds of the *SAL* and *MAHUA*** |
| Used many **forest shrubs and herbs for medicinal purposes** |
| **Sold forest produce** in local markets |
| ⭐ Local **weavers and leather workers** turned to them for ⭐ ***KUSUM* and *PALASH* flowers to colour cloth and leather** |

> ⭐ **That last line matters:** tribal forest produce was an **input to settled village crafts**. The forest and the plains were **economically interdependent**, not separate worlds.
>
> ⭐ ***Sal*** = a tree; ***mahua*** = **a flower that is eaten or used to make alcohol.**

**⭐ The Baigas' refusal:** when forest produce shrank, tribal people had to wander in search of wage work — *"But many of them — like the **BAIGAS of central India** — were **reluctant to do work for others**. The Baigas saw themselves as **people of the forest, who could only live on the produce of the forest.** ⭐ **It was BELOW THE DIGNITY of a Baiga to become a labourer.**"*

> 🎯 **This is not stubbornness; it is an identity claim.** For a GS1 answer on tribal marginalisation, it shows that the loss was **not only economic but of self-definition** — becoming a wage labourer meant ceasing to be who you were.

> ⭐⭐ **This is the specific content of the word *diku*.** Not "non-tribal" in the abstract — **the trader and the moneylender**, the two figures through whom the outside world extracted value.

### 🐄 Some herded animals ⭐

| Group | Where | Animals |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Van Gujjars** | **Punjab hills** | Cattle |
| ⭐ **Labadis** | **Andhra Pradesh** | Cattle |
| ⭐ **Gaddis** | **Kulu** | Sheep (shepherds) |
| ⭐ **Bakarwals** | **Kashmir** | Goats |

> 🧠 **Learn these four pairings** — a stock matching question. *(The Gaddis also appeared in Class 7 Ch.5, in the western Himalaya.)*

### 🚜 Some took to settled cultivation

Even before the nineteenth century many had **settled down**, using the **plough** and gradually acquiring **rights over land**.

> ⭐ **In many cases, like the MUNDAS of Chottanagpur, the LAND BELONGED TO THE CLAN AS A WHOLE.** All members were regarded as **descendants of the original settlers who had first cleared the land**, so **all of them had rights on it.**
>
> ⚠️ **But:** *"very often some people within the clan **acquired more power than others**, some became **chiefs** and others followers. **Powerful men often rented out their land instead of cultivating it themselves.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐ **Hold on to "the land belonged to the clan as a whole."** This is the exact principle colonial land settlement destroyed — because settlement required **individual owners**, and there was no column in the form for a clan. **It is also the principle that PESA and the Forest Rights Act later tried to restore.** *(Compare the Ahom rule in Class 7 Ch.5: the king could not take land without the community's consent.)*

**⭐ And the colonial hierarchy of judgement:**

> *"British officials saw **settled tribal groups like the GONDS and SANTHALS as MORE CIVILISED** than hunter-gatherers or shifting cultivators. **Those who lived in the forests were considered to be WILD AND SAVAGE: they needed to be SETTLED AND CIVILISED.**"*

> 🎯 **Settledness was equated with civilisation** — and, not coincidentally, with **taxability**. The moral judgement and the fiscal interest pointed the same way.

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ What Happened to Tribal Chiefs

| **BEFORE the British** | **UNDER British rule** |
|---|---|
| **Important people** enjoying **economic power** | ⭐ **Allowed to keep their land titles** over a cluster of villages and to **rent out lands** |
| Had the **right to administer and control their territories** | ⭐ **Lost much of their ADMINISTRATIVE POWER** |
| In some places had **their own police** | ⭐ **Forced to follow laws made by British officials** |
| **Decided the local rules of land and forest management** | ⭐ Had to **PAY TRIBUTE to the British** |
| Held **authority among their own people** | ⭐ Had to **DISCIPLINE THE TRIBAL GROUPS ON BEHALF OF THE BRITISH** |
| Fulfilled **traditional functions** | ⭐ **"They LOST THE AUTHORITY they had earlier enjoyed amongst their people, and were UNABLE TO FULFIL THEIR TRADITIONAL FUNCTIONS."** |

> ⭐⭐ **The chief was kept as an economic figure and hollowed out as a political one** — retained as a rent-collector and enforcer, stripped of the functions that had made him legitimate. **He became an agent of the state among his own people**, which destroyed exactly the authority the state wanted to use.
>
> 💻 **The role was preserved and its permissions revoked.** He kept the title and lost the capability — and with it the consent that made the title work.

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐ The Attack on Shifting Cultivation

```
   WHY THE BRITISH WANTED THEM SETTLED

   "The British were UNCOMFORTABLE with groups who MOVED ABOUT
    and did NOT HAVE A FIXED HOME."
                            |
        +-------------------+-------------------+
        v                                       v
   *** "SETTLED PEASANTS were EASIER TO      *** "The British also wanted
        CONTROL AND ADMINISTER than people        a REGULAR REVENUE SOURCE
        who were always ON THE MOVE." ***         for the state." ***
                            |
                            v
   SO THEY INTRODUCED LAND SETTLEMENTS:
     - MEASURED the land
     - DEFINED the rights of each INDIVIDUAL to that land
     - FIXED the revenue demand for the state
     - Some peasants declared LANDOWNERS, others TENANTS
```

### ⚠️ ⭐⭐ Why it failed — and the one British retreat in the chapter

> *"The British effort to settle jhum cultivators was **NOT VERY SUCCESSFUL**. **Settled plough cultivation is NOT EASY in areas where WATER IS SCARCE and the SOIL IS DRY.** In fact, **jhum cultivators who took to plough cultivation OFTEN SUFFERED, since their fields did not produce good yields.**"*
>
> ⭐⭐ *"So the jhum cultivators in **north-east India INSISTED on continuing with their traditional practice. FACING WIDESPREAD PROTESTS, THE BRITISH HAD TO ULTIMATELY ALLOW THEM THE RIGHT TO CARRY ON SHIFTING CULTIVATION IN SOME PARTS OF THE FOREST.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐ **This is an important and often-missed point: resistance WORKED here.** Sustained protest forced the colonial state to **concede a legal right**. When a Mains question asks whether tribal resistance achieved anything, this is your first piece of evidence — the second being Birsa's land laws at the end of the chapter.
>
> 🎯 **And note the technical argument:** *jhum* was not primitive backwardness. It was **an adaptation to dry soil and scarce water**, and plough agriculture in those conditions **produced worse yields**. **The "civilising" policy was agronomically wrong.**

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Forest Laws and Their Impact

> *"The life of tribal groups was **directly connected to the forest**. So **changes in forest laws had a considerable effect** on tribal lives."*

```
   [1] "The British EXTENDED THEIR CONTROL OVER ALL FORESTS and
        declared that *** FORESTS WERE STATE PROPERTY. ***"

   [2] Some forests were classified as *** RESERVED FORESTS ***
       -- "for they produced TIMBER WHICH THE BRITISH WANTED"

   [3] In these forests people were *** NOT ALLOWED TO: ***
         - MOVE FREELY
         - practise JHUM CULTIVATION
         - COLLECT FRUITS
         - HUNT ANIMALS

   [4] "How were jhum cultivators to survive in such a situation?
        *** MANY WERE THEREFORE FORCED TO MOVE TO OTHER AREAS
            IN SEARCH OF WORK AND LIVELIHOOD. ***"
```

### 🪤 ⭐⭐⭐ The forest villages device — a genuinely cynical solution

```
   THE BRITISH PROBLEM:

   "Once the British STOPPED the tribal people from LIVING INSIDE
    FORESTS, they faced a problem.
    *** From where would the Forest Department get its LABOUR
        to CUT TREES for RAILWAY SLEEPERS and to TRANSPORT LOGS? ***"
                            |
                            v
   THEIR SOLUTION:

   "They decided that they would GIVE JHUM CULTIVATORS SMALL
    PATCHES OF LAND IN THE FORESTS and ALLOW THEM TO CULTIVATE
    THESE -- *** ON THE CONDITION *** that those who lived in the
    villages would have to *** PROVIDE LABOUR TO THE FOREST
    DEPARTMENT and LOOK AFTER THE FORESTS. ***"
                            |
                            v
   "So in many regions, the Forest Department established
    *** FOREST VILLAGES *** to ensure a *** REGULAR SUPPLY OF
        CHEAP LABOUR. ***"
```

> ⭐⭐⭐ **Look at the structure of this, because it is the sharpest thing in the chapter.**
>
> **Step 1: take away their right to live in the forest.** **Step 2: give a fraction of it back — as a wage.** The very access that had been theirs by custom was **reissued as payment for labour the state needed.**
>
> **They were made dependent on the state for what they had previously simply had.** That is the mechanism of colonial forest policy in one paragraph, and it is superb Mains material for GS1 and for Environment.
>
> ⭐ ***Sleeper*** = **the horizontal planks of wood on which railway lines are laid.** The railways — the great symbol of colonial "modernisation" — **ran on timber cut by dispossessed forest dwellers.**

**⭐⭐ Resistance to the forest laws:**

> *"Many tribal groups **reacted against the colonial forest laws**. They **disobeyed the new rules, continued with practices that were declared illegal, and at times rose in OPEN REBELLION**. Such was the revolt of ⭐ **SONGRAM SANGMA in 1906 in ASSAM**, and the ⭐ **FOREST SATYAGRAHA of the 1930s in the CENTRAL PROVINCES**."*

**⭐ And the Baiga song Verrier Elwin recorded in the 1930s** — worth quoting:

> *"In this land of the English how hard it is to live... In the village sits the **landlord**, In the gate sits the **Kotwar**, In the garden sits the **Patwari**, In the field sits the **government**... **To pay cattle tax we have to sell cow / To pay forest tax we have to sell buffalo / To pay land tax we have to sell bullock / HOW ARE WE TO GET OUR FOOD?**"*

> 🎯 **Four figures occupying four spaces — village, gate, garden, field — so that there is nowhere left to stand.** And the second verse states the trap exactly: **each tax must be paid by selling the animal that produces the food.** This is a tribal voice in the historical record, and it is precisely the sort of source Chapter 1 said was hardest to recover.

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The Problem with Trade — the Silk Case

**The best worked example of tribal exploitation in the book.**

```
   THE SILK COCOON CHAIN, Hazaribagh (present-day JHARKHAND)

   The SANTHALS reared cocoons
                |
                v
   Traders' AGENTS gave LOANS to the tribal people and
   COLLECTED the cocoons
                |
                v
   *** The growers were paid Rs 3 to Rs 4 for a THOUSAND cocoons ***
                |
                v
   These were exported to BURDWAN or GAYA
                |
                v
   *** ...where they were sold at FIVE TIMES THE PRICE ***
                |
                v
   The MIDDLEMEN -- "so called because they ARRANGED DEALS between
   the EXPORTERS and the SILK GROWERS" -- *** MADE HUGE PROFITS. ***
   *** "The silk growers earned VERY LITTLE." ***
                |
                v
   "Understandably, many tribal groups saw the MARKET and the
    TRADERS as their MAIN ENEMIES."
```

> ⭐⭐ **The "five times the price" figure is asked directly.** And the analytical point is worth stating in an answer: the Santhals **bore all the production risk and captured none of the value**, because they were **tied by debt to a single buyer** and had no access to the market where the price was actually set.
>
> 🔗 **Exactly the *ryoti* indigo structure from Chapter 3** — advance, obligation, low price, permanent debt. **The same instrument, applied to a different community and commodity.**

### ⛏️ The search for work

> From the **late nineteenth century**, **tea plantations** came up and **mining became an important industry**. ⭐ **Tribals were recruited in large numbers to work at the TEA PLANTATIONS OF ASSAM and the COAL MINES OF JHARKHAND.**
>
> ⭐⭐ *"They were recruited through **CONTRACTORS** who paid them **miserably low wages**, and **PREVENTED THEM FROM RETURNING HOME**."*

**⭐ The scale and the cost:** in the **1920s, about 50 per cent of the miners** in the **Jharia and Raniganj** coal mines of Bihar **were tribals**. Work deep in the dark, suffocating mines was *"not only back-breaking and dangerous, it was often literally killing"* — ⭐ **over 2,000 workers died every year in India's coal mines in the 1920s.**

> ⭐ **"Prevented them from returning home" is indentured labour in all but name.** This is the destination of dispossession: a forest-dweller who could not live in the forest ended up **a mine worker who could not leave.**

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Tribal Revolts — Learn These Dates

> *"Through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, tribal groups in different parts of the country **rebelled against the changes in laws, the restrictions on their practices, the new taxes they had to pay, and the exploitation by traders and moneylenders**."*

| Revolt | Year | Where |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **KOLS rebelled** | **1831–32** | Chottanagpur |
| ⭐⭐ **SANTHALS rose in revolt** | **1855** | (Santhal Pargana) |
| ⭐ **SONGRAM SANGMA's revolt** | **1906** | **Assam** |
| ⭐⭐ **BASTAR Rebellion** | **1910** | **Central India** |
| ⭐ **Forest Satyagraha** | **1930s** | **Central Provinces** |
| ⭐⭐ **WARLI Revolt** | **1940** | **Maharashtra** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **BIRSA MUNDA's movement** | **1895–1900** | Chottanagpur |

> 🧠 **A stock Prelims format is match-the-revolt-to-the-year or to the region. Learn all seven.**
>
> ⭐ **And note the span: 1831 to 1940 — over a century of continuous tribal resistance.** This is the "resistance was continuous, not occasional" thread from the index. **The countryside was in revolt long before, and long after, the Congress existed.**

## 7️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ Birsa Munda

### The making of a leader

| Influence | What he absorbed |
|---|---|
| **Birth** | Born in the ⭐ **mid-1870s**, *"the son of a poor father"*; grew up around the forests of **Bohonda**, grazing sheep, playing the flute, dancing in the local *akhara*. **Forced by poverty, his father had to move from place to place looking for work** |
| ⭐ **The sirdars** | As an adolescent he **heard tales of the Munda uprisings of the past** and saw the ***sirdars* (leaders) urging the people to revolt**. They spoke of **a golden age when the Mundas had been free of the oppression of *dikus***, and said the **ancestral right of the community would be restored**. They saw themselves as **descendants of the original settlers, fighting for their land (*MULK KI LARAI*)** |
| ⭐ **The missionary school** | *"He heard it said that it was possible for the Mundas to **attain the Kingdom of Heaven and REGAIN THEIR LOST RIGHTS** — if they became good Christians and gave up their 'bad practices'"* |
| ⭐ **A Vaishnav preacher** | He **wore the sacred thread** and **began to value the importance of purity and piety** |

> ⭐⭐ **Birsa's movement is a synthesis, and NCERT is careful about this.** He drew on **Munda tradition, Christian millenarianism and Vaishnav asceticism** — and then **turned against the missionaries and landlords**, seeing them as *"outside forces that were ruining the Munda way of life."*
>
> 🎯 **He took the promise of the missionaries — that lost rights could be regained — and detached it from the missionaries.** That is a sophisticated observation for a Mains answer on tribal millenarian movements.

### 🌅 ⭐⭐⭐ The vision of a golden age

> In **1895**, Birsa urged his followers to **recover their glorious past**. He talked of a golden age — a ⭐ ***SATYUG*, the age of truth** — when the Mundas:
> - **constructed embankments**
> - **tapped natural springs**
> - **planted trees and orchards**
> - **practised cultivation to earn their living**
> - **did not kill their brethren and relatives**
> - **lived honestly**
>
> ⭐ *"Birsa also wanted people to **once again work on their land, SETTLE DOWN and CULTIVATE their fields**."*

**⭐ The reform side:** his movement *"was aimed at **reforming tribal society**."* He urged the Mundas to **give up drinking liquor, clean their village, and stop believing in witchcraft and sorcery.**

> 🎯 **NCERT's own question — "why do you think such a vision appealed to the people of the region?" — has a real answer.**
>
> The golden age was **not nostalgia for hunting and wandering**; it described **embankments, orchards, honest cultivation and settled land**. It offered **dignity and security in a familiar idiom** — a past that was recognisably theirs, and a future in which the land was theirs again. **A vision of restoration is more mobilising than a vision of change**, because it claims what is being demanded was **already rightfully yours**.

### ⚔️ ⭐⭐⭐ The political aim — what actually alarmed the British

> *"What worried British officials most was the **POLITICAL AIM** of the Birsa movement, for it wanted to **DRIVE OUT MISSIONARIES, MONEYLENDERS, LANDLORDS, AND THE GOVERNMENT** and **SET UP A MUNDA RAJ WITH BIRSA AT ITS HEAD**."*

**⭐ And why each was named:**

| Target | The grievance |
|---|---|
| **The government** | *"The **land policies** of the British were **destroying their traditional land system**"* |
| **Landlords and moneylenders** | *"were **taking over their land**"* |
| **Missionaries** | *"were **criticising their traditional culture**"* |

### The course of the movement

```
   1895 -- As the movement spread, British officials decided to act:
           *** ARRESTED BIRSA, convicted him of RIOTING,
               and JAILED HIM FOR TWO YEARS ***
                            |
                            v
   1897 -- RELEASED. He began TOURING THE VILLAGES to gather support,
           "using TRADITIONAL SYMBOLS AND LANGUAGE to rouse people,
            urging them to *** DESTROY "RAVANA" (dikus and the
            Europeans) *** and establish a kingdom under his leadership"
                            |
                            v
           His followers targeted the SYMBOLS of diku and European power:
             - ATTACKED POLICE STATIONS and CHURCHES
             - RAIDED the property of MONEYLENDERS and ZAMINDARS
             - *** RAISED THE WHITE FLAG as a symbol of BIRSA RAJ ***
                            |
                            v
   1900 -- *** BIRSA DIED OF CHOLERA and the movement faded out. ***
```

> ⭐ **The use of "Ravana" is worth noting** — a **familiar mythological idiom** repurposed for anti-colonial mobilisation, exactly as the *satyug* was. **Resistance spoke in the language people already had.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why it mattered — the two outcomes NCERT names

> *"However, the movement was **significant in at least TWO ways**:*
>
> ⭐⭐ ***FIRST** — it **forced the colonial government to INTRODUCE LAWS so that the LAND OF THE TRIBALS COULD NOT BE EASILY TAKEN OVER BY DIKUS**.*
>
> ⭐⭐ ***SECOND** — it showed once again that **the tribal people had the CAPACITY TO PROTEST AGAINST INJUSTICE and express their anger against colonial rule. They did this in their own specific way, INVENTING THEIR OWN RITUALS AND SYMBOLS OF STRUGGLE.**"*

> ⭐⭐⭐ **The first outcome is the direct ancestor of a whole line of Indian law**, and this is the connection to make in a GS1 or GS2 answer:
>
> **Birsa's movement → the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) → the Fifth and Sixth Schedules of the Constitution → PESA (1996) → the Forest Rights Act (2006).**
>
> The principle running through all of them is the one this chapter opened with: **tribal land belongs to the community and cannot be freely alienated to outsiders.** *(Compare the Ahom rule in Class 7 Ch.5 — the king could not take land without the community's consent.)*
>
> ⭐ **And the second outcome is NCERT's answer to a persistent condescension** — that tribal people were passive victims of history. **They resisted continuously, and on their own terms, with their own symbols.**

**Traps:**
- **"Jhum cultivators plough the land and sow seeds"** → **FALSE.** They **broadcast** seeds without ploughing *(NCERT true/false)*
- **"The British wanted to preserve the tribal way of life"** → **FALSE** *(NCERT true/false)*
- **"Tribal chiefs lost their land titles under British rule"** → **FALSE.** They **kept the titles** and lost the **administrative power**
- **"Reserved Forests were set aside to protect tribal rights"** → **FALSE.** They were reserved for **timber the British wanted**
- **"The British never conceded anything to shifting cultivators"** → **FALSE.** They **allowed jhum in some parts of the forest** after protests
- **"Birsa's movement had only religious aims"** → **FALSE.** Its **political aim** was a **Munda Raj**, driving out government, landlords, moneylenders and missionaries

### Mains

- ⭐⭐⭐ **Indian Society (GS1):** *"Colonial forest and land policy transformed tribal communities from independent forest-dwellers into dependent labour."* — use **Reserved Forests + forest villages + the silk chain + mine recruitment**
- ⭐⭐⭐ **Polity (GS2):** trace **community land rights** from Munda clan ownership → Birsa's laws → **Fifth Schedule → PESA 1996 → Forest Rights Act 2006**
- ⭐⭐ **Environment:** *jhum* as an **ecological adaptation**, not backwardness; the agronomic failure of imposed plough cultivation
- ⭐⭐ *"Tribal resistance was continuous and occasionally effective."* — the jhum concession and Birsa's land laws as evidence
- ⭐ **Historiography:** Verrier Elwin's songs as a rare tribal voice in the record

## 🔁 2-MINUTE RECAP

1. ***Dikus*** = outsiders — in practice the **traders and moneylenders** through whom tribal people met the market, and for whom *"market and commerce often meant **debt and poverty**."*
2. **Four livelihoods:** ***jhum*** cultivators (cut, burn, **ash for potash**, **broadcast**, move, leave **fallow**), **hunter-gatherers** (Khonds of Orissa — *sal*, *mahua*, *kusum*, *palash*), **herders** (Van Gujjars, Labadis, Gaddis, Bakarwals), and **settled cultivators** (Mundas, where **land belonged to the clan as a whole**).
3. **The Baigas refused wage labour** — it was **below their dignity** as people of the forest.
4. **Tribal chiefs kept their land titles but lost administrative power**, paid **tribute**, and had to **discipline their own people** for the British — destroying their authority.
5. The British wanted shifting cultivators **settled** because settled peasants were **easier to control and taxable**. ⭐ **It failed** — plough cultivation gave poor yields on dry soil, and after **widespread protests the British allowed jhum in some parts of the forest.**
6. ⭐⭐ **Forests were declared state property**; **Reserved Forests** were closed to movement, jhum, fruit-gathering and hunting. Then, needing labour for **railway sleepers**, the state created ⭐ **forest villages** — giving back small patches of forest **in exchange for labour**.
7. **Resistance to forest laws:** **Songram Sangma, 1906, Assam**; the **Forest Satyagraha of the 1930s** in the Central Provinces. **Elwin's Baiga song:** *"To pay forest tax we have to sell buffalo... how are we to get our food?"*
8. ⭐⭐ **The silk chain:** Santhals of **Hazaribagh** paid **Rs 3–4 per thousand cocoons**, resold in **Burdwan or Gaya at five times the price** — the **middlemen made huge profits**.
9. **Dispossession ended in migration:** **tea plantations of Assam** and **coal mines of Jharkhand**, recruited by **contractors** who paid miserably and **prevented them from returning home**. In the **1920s half the miners at Jharia and Raniganj were tribals**, and **over 2,000 died yearly**.
10. ⭐⭐ **Revolts:** **Kols 1831–32**, **Santhals 1855**, **Songram Sangma 1906**, **Bastar 1910**, **Forest Satyagraha 1930s**, **Warli 1940** — and **Birsa, 1895–1900**.
11. ⭐⭐ **Birsa** drew on **Munda tradition, missionary promise and Vaishnav piety**, then turned on the missionaries. He preached a ***satyug*** of **embankments, orchards and honest cultivation**, and a return to **settled land**.
12. ⭐⭐⭐ **His political aim was a MUNDA RAJ** — driving out **missionaries, moneylenders, landlords and the government**. **Arrested 1895, released 1897**, his followers attacked police stations and churches under a **white flag**; he **died of cholera in 1900**.
13. ⭐⭐⭐ **Two outcomes:** the government was **forced to legislate against the easy transfer of tribal land**, and tribal people demonstrated their **capacity to protest, in their own idiom**. That legal principle runs down to **PESA and the Forest Rights Act**.
