## 🎬 Start Here: What "First City" Actually Means

You've read about villages in Chapter 2 — a few hundred people, mud huts, everyone farming.

Now picture this instead:

**A city of 35,000–40,000 people. Streets laid out on a grid, crossing at right angles. Every house connected to a covered drain running beneath the street, with inspection holes for cleaning. Bricks in a fixed 1:2:4 ratio, identical from Gujarat to Afghanistan — a distance of over 1,500 km. A public water tank made watertight with gypsum mortar and bitumen. Standardised stone weights in a binary sequence.**

Now the part that should genuinely unsettle you: **this was 4,700 years ago.** Many Indian towns did not have covered sewerage in 1947.

```
   ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  WHO ELSE WAS BUILDING CITIES AT THIS MOMENT? (~2600 BCE)         │
   ├───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │                                                                   │
   │   🇮🇶 MESOPOTAMIA  — Sumer, Ur, Uruk (Tigris–Euphrates)          │
   │   🇪🇬 EGYPT        — Old Kingdom, the Great Pyramids             │
   │   🇮🇳 HARAPPA      — Indus valley                                │
   │   🇨🇳 CHINA        — a bit later (Shang, ~1600 BCE)              │
   │                                                                   │
   │   THE HARAPPAN WAS THE LARGEST OF ALL OF THEM BY AREA             │
   │   — roughly 1.5 million sq km, ~1,500 km east-to-west             │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**And here's the twist that defines the whole chapter:** Egypt gave us pyramids and pharaohs' names. Mesopotamia gave us kings and epics. **Harappa gave us drains, weights and grids — and not one single ruler's name.** We cannot read a word they wrote.

## 1️⃣ Discovery — How We Found Them

This is a genuinely good story, and the names are asked.

```
  TIMELINE OF DISCOVERY
  ═════════════════════

  1850s ──► British engineers building the Lahore–Multan RAILWAY
            find a mound of high-quality ancient bricks at Harappa.
            They GRIND THEM UP and use them as track ballast.
            An unknown quantity of the city is gone forever. 😞

  1875  ──► Alexander Cunningham (first Director-General of the ASI)
            reports a seal from Harappa. He cannot place it, and
            wrongly assumes it is foreign.

  1921  ──► DAYA RAM SAHNI excavates HARAPPA (Punjab, Pakistan)

  1922  ──► R. D. BANERJI excavates MOHENJODARO (Sindh, Pakistan)

  1924  ──► JOHN MARSHALL (ASI Director-General) announces to the world
            that a previously unknown civilisation has been found.
            India's history is instantly pushed back ~2,000 years.

  Post-1947 ─► Partition puts Harappa and Mohenjodaro in Pakistan.
            Indian archaeologists search for sites in India and find
            LOTHAL, KALIBANGAN, DHOLAVIRA, RAKHIGARHI, BANAWALI...

  2014-2025 ─► RAKHIGARHI (Haryana) emerges as possibly the LARGEST
            Harappan site; ancient DNA studies from here become
            central to the Aryan migration debate.

  2021  ──► DHOLAVIRA inscribed as a UNESCO WORLD HERITAGE SITE
```

### The naming question — "Harappan" or "Indus Valley"?

| Name | Why used | Why it's imperfect |
|---|---|---|
| **Harappan Civilisation** | Archaeological convention: name a culture after the **first site excavated** — Harappa (1921) | Harappa wasn't the capital or the biggest site |
| **Indus Valley Civilisation** | Most early sites lie along the Indus and its tributaries | **Misleading** — many sites (Lothal, Dholavira, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi) are *not* in the Indus valley. Over half lie along the dried-up **Ghaggar-Hakra** river |
| **Indus-Saraswati Civilisation** | Used by those identifying Ghaggar-Hakra with the Rigvedic Saraswati | Contested; the identification is debated |

> 🎯 **Prelims-worthy nuance:** "Harappan Civilisation" is the **more accurate** term precisely because the civilisation extends well beyond the Indus valley. NCERT uses "Harappan."

### The rules of Harappan urban planning

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Two-part division** | **Citadel** (west, smaller, higher, walled) + **Lower Town** (east, larger, lower, walled). Both usually walled |
| **Grid street plan** | Streets and lanes cross at **right angles**, dividing the city into rectangular blocks |
| **Baked bricks** | **Standardised** and remarkably well-made; laid in an **interlocking pattern** for strength |
| **Brick ratio** | **1 : 2 : 4** (thickness : width : length) — the *same ratio* across the entire civilisation |
| **Drains first** | **Drains were laid out BEFORE the houses were built** ← the killer detail |
| **Wells** | Many houses had their **own well**; Mohenjodaro had ~700 wells |
| **No temples or palaces** | No structure has been *conclusively* identified as either |

> ⭐ **"Drains were planned before houses" is the single most quotable line in this chapter.** Think about what it requires: someone surveyed the site, designed a network, dug and built it, and only then allowed construction — and everyone complied. **That is a state, or something very like one.**
>
> 💻 You'd call it *infrastructure-as-code, provisioned before deployment.* You cannot retrofit a gradient-correct sewer network under an existing city. It must be designed first. So must a civilisation.

## 3️⃣ The Drainage System — Their Greatest Achievement

Take this seriously. It is what makes the Harappans exceptional.

```
        CROSS-SECTION OF A HARAPPAN STREET
   ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════

    HOUSE                 STREET                  HOUSE
    ┌──────┐   ┌─────────────────────────────┐   ┌──────┐
    │      │   │                             │   │      │
    │ 🚿   │   │      (walking surface)      │   │  🚿  │
    │bath  │   │                             │   │ bath │
    │      │   └──┬───────────────────────┬──┘   │      │
    └───┬──┘      │  ▓▓▓ MANHOLE ▓▓▓      │      └──┬───┘
        │         │  (inspection cover,   │             │
        │ house   │   removable for       │  house      │
        │ drain   │   CLEANING)           │  drain      │
        ▼         ▼                       ▼         ▼
    ════╬═════════╬═══════════════════════╬═════════╬════
        ╚═════════▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓╬═════════╝
             MAIN COVERED DRAIN under the street
             • brick-lined  • sloped for GRAVITY flow
             • covered with loose stone slabs
             • soak pits / sumps at intervals to
               collect solid waste before it clogged
                                                        │
                          ▼
                  out beyond the city
```

### Why this is genuinely remarkable

| Feature | What it required |
|---|---|
| **Every house connected** | Universal coverage — not a privilege of the rich. Suggests a *civic* system, not an elite one |
| **Covered drains** | Understanding that open sewage causes disease and smell |
| **Gradient/slope** | Careful **surveying** — water must flow by gravity across the whole city |
| **Manholes / inspection holes** | Designed for **maintenance**, not just construction. Someone was responsible for ongoing cleaning |
| **Soak pits / sumps** | Solid waste settled out before it could block the line |
| **Standard brick sizes** | Manufacturing standards, and a supply chain to enforce them |

> 💻 **They designed for observability and maintenance.** Manholes are access ports; soak pits are a filtering stage that prevents downstream failure. This isn't just building a thing — it's building a thing that can be *operated* for generations. Most ancient engineering doesn't do this.

**The organisational implication (say this in a Mains answer):** a system like this cannot be built or maintained by individual households acting alone. It requires **surveying, standardisation, coordinated construction, and continuous municipal maintenance** — in other words, a functioning **civic authority** with the power to plan and enforce.

## 4️⃣ The Star Structures

### 🛁 The Great Bath (Mohenjodaro)

**The most famous Harappan structure.**

```
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │  Rooms on all sides (changing rooms?)         │
        │  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐  │
        │  │            COLONNADED CORRIDOR          │  │
        │  │   ┌───────────────────────────────┐     │  │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ steps      TANK      steps▓▓│    │  │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ ┌───────────────────────┐ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ │ ≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈ │ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ │ ≈≈≈  ~12m × 7m  ≈≈≈≈≈ │ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ │ ≈≈≈  ~2.4m deep ≈≈≈≈≈ │ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ │ ≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈≈ │ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   │ ▓▓ └───────────────────────┘ ▓▓│   │   │
        │  │   └───────────────────────────────┘     │  │
        │  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘  │
        │   Steps lead down on NORTH and SOUTH sides    │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
              Located on the CITADEL at MOHENJODARO
```

| Detail | Value |
|---|---|
| **Location** | **Citadel**, Mohenjodaro |
| **Size** | ~**12 m long × 7 m wide × 2.4 m deep** |
| **Waterproofing** | Bricks set on edge with **gypsum mortar**; a layer of **bitumen (natural tar)** lining the sides and floor |
| **Water supply** | From a **well** in an adjacent room |
| **Drainage** | A large **corbelled drain** in one corner emptied the tank |
| **Surroundings** | Corridor on all four sides; rooms on three sides — one with a well |
| **Purpose** | Probably **ritual bathing** on special occasions — a public/ceremonial function |

> 🎯 **The Great Bath is your evidence for "ritual purity mattered."** But be careful: we're *inferring* ritual from architecture. It's the leading hypothesis, not a proven fact — and Prelims respects that distinction.

> 🧠 **Why it's astonishing engineering:** they solved waterproofing 4,700 years ago using gypsum mortar plus bitumen — a two-layer system. That's a genuine materials-science achievement.

### 🌾 The Granary / Warehouse

Found at **Mohenjodaro** and **Harappa** (Harappa's is near the river, with circular brick platforms nearby, possibly for threshing grain).

- Large structures on the **citadel**, with **ventilation ducts** and raised platforms (keeps grain dry, deters rodents)
- **⚠️ Terminology caution:** these were identified as "granaries" by early excavators, but the identification is **disputed** — no significant grain residue was found in Mohenjodaro's. Many archaeologists now call them "warehouses" or "great halls." NCERT still uses "granary."

> 💡 **Why a granary on the citadel matters:** collecting and storing grain centrally implies **taxation or tribute** — someone gathered surplus from farmers and stored it under guard. This is the direct continuation of Chapter 2's "storage → surplus → the state" chain.

### 🔥 Fire Altars

Found at **Kalibangan** (Rajasthan) and **Lothal** (Gujarat).

- Rows of raised brick platforms with ash, charcoal and terracotta cakes
- At Kalibangan, found both on the citadel *and* inside private houses
- Interpreted as evidence of **fire rituals / sacrifice**

> 🎯 **Why this is significant for a bigger debate:** fire ritual is central to *Vedic* religion (Chapter 4). Fire altars at Harappan sites are used by some scholars to argue for continuity between Harappan and Vedic culture. **Contested, but very much asked.**

### ⚓ The Dockyard (Lothal) — ⭐⭐⭐

**Lothal, Gujarat** — one of the most important sites for India.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **The dockyard** | A large **brick-lined basin**, ~**214 m × 36 m**, connected to the Bhogavo river/Gulf of Khambhat by a channel |
| **What it did** | Ships and boats came in; a **sluice gate** and spill channel maintained water level at low tide |
| **Why it matters** | The **world's earliest known dockyard** (as identified). Lothal was a **port and trading hub** |
| **Also at Lothal** | A **bead-making factory** (with kiln, raw material, finished and unfinished beads), a **warehouse**, **fire altars**, a Persian Gulf seal, and **rice** cultivation |
| **Layout note** | Unlike most sites, Lothal's citadel and lower town were enclosed within **one wall** |

> 🎯 **Lothal = trade + beads + dockyard + rice + fire altar.** Four or five separate "famous for" tags on one site. Very high frequency in Prelims.

### 🏟️ Dholavira (Gujarat, Rann of Kutch) — ⭐⭐⭐

**UNESCO World Heritage Site (2021)** — which makes it current-affairs-relevant too.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Unique layout** | Divided into **THREE parts** (not two): **Citadel, Middle Town, Lower Town** — each with massive stone walls |
| **The signboard** ⭐ | A large inscription of ~**10 large Harappan signs**, made of white gypsum inlay on wood, found near the citadel gate — **the world's earliest known "signboard"** |
| **Water management** ⭐ | An extraordinary system of **stone-cut reservoirs**, check dams and channels to harvest every drop of monsoon runoff — Dholavira sits in an arid zone between two seasonal streams (Mansar and Manhar) |
| **Building material** | **STONE** masonry (most Harappan sites are brick) — local stone was abundant |
| **A "stadium"** | A large open ground with terraced seating — possibly for ceremonies or public gatherings |

> 🌍 **Modern relevance:** Dholavira is regularly cited in water-conservation and rainwater-harvesting discussions. **Use it in Mains/Essay answers on water security** — an ancient Indian city in a desert that survived on engineered rainwater harvesting. That's a strong, specific example.

## 6️⃣ Life, Craft and Technology

### 🌾 Agriculture

| Crops grown | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Wheat, barley** | The staples |
| **Pulses** (lentil, chickpea) | Widespread |
| **Sesame, mustard** | Oil seeds |
| **Rice** | At **Lothal** and **Rangpur** (Gujarat) — relatively rare |
| **Cotton** ⭐ | The Harappans were among the **world's first cotton growers**. Greeks later called it *"Sindon"* — from Sindh |
| **Millets** | In Gujarat sites |

**Farming technology:**

- **The plough** — evidence from two sites:
  - **Kalibangan**: an actual **ploughed field** with two sets of furrows **crossing at right angles**, showing two crops grown together ⭐
  - **Banawali**: a **terracotta model of a plough** ⭐
- Ploughs were of **wood**, so the ploughs themselves haven't survived — only the field marks and the model
- **Irrigation** was needed (the region is semi-arid): canals found at **Shortughai**, and water reservoirs at **Dholavira**

**Animals reared:** cattle (very important), sheep, goat, buffalo, pig. Also **dogs** and **cats** (footprints of both, and a famous brick with a dog chasing a cat).

> 🐎 **THE HORSE QUESTION — know this precisely.**
> Horse remains at Harappan sites are **rare and disputed** (Surkotada is the main claim, and specialists disagree on whether the bones are horse or wild ass/onager). There is **no depiction of a horse on any Harappan seal.** By contrast, the horse is *central* to Rigvedic society (Ch.4).
> **UPSC has asked this (2013).** The safe formulation: *"Harappans did not use horse-drawn chariots in warfare; evidence for the horse is scanty and contested."*

### 🏺 Crafts and materials

| Craft | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Bead-making** ⭐ | **Carnelian** (a beautiful red stone), agate, jasper, crystal, quartz, steatite, faience, shell, terracotta, gold. **Chanhudaro** and **Lothal** were bead factories |
| **Faience** ⭐ | An **artificial (man-made) material** — ground sand/quartz mixed with colour and gum, shaped and fired to a glaze. Used for beads, bangles, small vessels. **The Harappans manufactured a material that does not occur in nature** |
| **Shell working** | **Nageshwar** and **Balakot** — bangles, ladles, inlay |
| **Metallurgy** | **Copper and bronze** (copper + tin). Also **gold and silver**. ❌ **NO IRON** — iron had not yet been discovered |
| **Pottery** | Fine wheel-made **red pottery with black designs** — plants, animals, geometric patterns |
| **Textiles** | **Cotton and wool**; actual fragments of cotton cloth found; spindle whorls common |
| **Seal-making** | Steatite (soapstone), carved and then fired to harden |
| **Stone tools** | Chert blades, from the **Rohri hills** in Sindh |

> ⚠️ **NO IRON.** This is a Bronze/Chalcolithic civilisation. Any statement claiming Harappan iron tools is **false**. Iron enters India only around 1000 BCE (Chapter 4/5). Easy Prelims elimination.

### 🔷 Seals and the Script

The **seal** is the iconic Harappan artefact. ~**4,000 inscribed objects** have been found.

```
          ┌───────────────────────────────┐
          │  ⧉ ⌇ ⊕ ⋔ ⊞   ← script (top)   │   Typical seal:
          │                               │   • STEATITE (soapstone)
          │        ╭─────────╮            │   • Usually SQUARE, ~2–4 cm
          │       ╱  UNICORN  ╲           │   • Animal motif in the centre
          │      │  (one-horned│          │   • Script above (avg 5 signs)
          │       ╲   bull)   ╱           │   • Boss/knob on the back with
          │        ╰─────────╯            │     a hole → worn or tied on
          │      ╱▔▔▔▔▔╲                  │   • Pressed into wet clay to
          │  ═══════════════════          │     leave a SEALING
          └───────────────────────────────┘
```

**Animals shown:** the **one-horned "unicorn"** (most common), **humped bull**, elephant, rhinoceros, tiger, buffalo, goat, and composite/mythical creatures.
**Notably absent:** the **horse**, and the **lion**.

| Aspect of the script | What we know |
|---|---|
| **Number of signs** | Roughly **375–400** distinct signs |
| **Type** | Too many signs for an alphabet, too few for a purely logographic system → probably **logo-syllabic** |
| **Direction** | Generally **right to left**. Longer texts are sometimes **boustrophedon** — right-to-left on one line, left-to-right on the next (like an ox ploughing a field) |
| **Length** | Very short — **average ~5 signs**, longest ~26 |
| **Status** | ⚠️ **UNDECIPHERED** |

**Why can't we read it?**
1. **No bilingual text** — nothing like the Rosetta Stone, which cracked Egyptian hieroglyphs by pairing them with Greek
2. **Texts are far too short** — 5 signs gives cryptanalysis almost nothing to work with
3. **We don't know the underlying language** — was it Dravidian? Indo-Aryan? Something extinct? Each assumption yields a different (unverifiable) decipherment
4. Even *whether it encodes language at all* is disputed by a minority of scholars

> 💻 **Precisely a cryptanalysis problem with a fatal data shortage.** You have ~4,000 ciphertext samples averaging 5 tokens each, no plaintext, no crib, and an unknown plaintext language. Frequency analysis needs volume and a known language model — you have neither. **The bottleneck isn't cleverness; it's data.** That's why 100 years of very smart people have failed.

**What seals were probably FOR:** stamping bundles of trade goods. Press the seal into wet clay on a package or a rope-tie; the sealing hardens; if it arrives intact, the goods weren't tampered with, and the mark identifies the sender.

> 💻 **A seal is a tamper-evident signature.** It doesn't encrypt; it *authenticates* and proves integrity in transit. Harappan sealings found in **Mesopotamia** are therefore direct evidence of goods travelling that far.

### ⚖️ Weights and Measures

- Made of **chert** — a hard, uniform stone
- **Cubical**, mostly unmarked
- The sequence is **binary in the lower denominations**: **1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64...** then shifts to decimal multiples of 16 (160, 200, 320, 640)
- **16 was clearly a key unit** ⭐ (a fun continuity: India used 16 *annas* to the rupee until 1957)
- The same weights turn up **across the entire civilisation**, from Gujarat to Afghanistan

> 💻 **A binary weight sequence isn't an accident.** With weights 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 you can compose *any* integer from 1 to 31 — the minimum set of weights for maximum coverage. That is a genuinely optimal design. Someone thought hard about this.

**And the historical significance:** standardised weights across 1.5 million sq km means **regulated, trusted, long-distance trade** — and an authority able to define and enforce a standard.

|---|---|---|---|
| **Dancing Girl** ⭐⭐⭐ | Mohenjodaro | **Bronze** (lost-wax / *cire perdue* casting) | ~10.5 cm; a girl standing with hand on hip, bangles on one arm |
| **Priest-King / Bearded Man** ⭐⭐ | Mohenjodaro | **Steatite** | Trefoil-patterned shawl over the left shoulder; half-closed eyes |

## 7️⃣ Trade and Raw Materials — India's First Supply Chain

**The core insight:** the cities sat on flat alluvial plains that produce **grain but almost no stone, metal or gemstone.** Every hard material had to be imported. So the Harappans ran a continent-scale procurement network.

```
   ╔════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗
   ║        WHERE DID THE RAW MATERIALS COME FROM?                      ║

     LAPIS LAZULI ─────►│                  │◄───── TIN

     Karnataka          │  (alluvial plain │       Oman/Arabia

                        │   no metal ore)  │◄───── SILVER
```

### 🌍 International trade — the Mesopotamia connection

Mesopotamian texts (which we *can* read) mention trade with three lands. **This is where a readable civilisation tells us about an unreadable one.**

| Mesopotamian name | Almost certainly refers to |
|---|---|
| **MELUHHA** ⭐⭐⭐ | **The Harappan region** (the Indus) |
| **MAGAN** | **Oman** |
| **DILMUN** | **Bahrain** (the intermediary island entrepôt) |

**Goods Meluhha sent to Mesopotamia:** **carnelian beads**, ivory, **copper**, gold, shell, wood, and — the poetic one — **peacocks** and monkeys.

**The physical proof:**
- **Harappan seals and sealings found in Mesopotamia** (Ur, Kish, Susa)
- **Etched carnelian beads** — a distinctly Harappan technique — found in Mesopotamian royal graves
- **Harappan-style cubical weights** found abroad
- A **Persian Gulf seal** (round, Dilmun type) found at **Lothal** — trade flowing the other way

> 🎯 **This is exactly the Chapter 1 lesson paying off.** We can't read Harappan script — but Mesopotamians *could* write, and they wrote about trading with Meluhha. **A readable neighbour becomes a window into an unreadable civilisation.** Beautiful methodological point; use it in Mains.

**How did goods move?** Bullock carts (terracotta cart models are common finds), pack animals, riverboats, and **seagoing ships** — a Mohenjodaro seal actually depicts a boat with a mast.

## 8️⃣ Society and Religion — Tread Carefully Here

**This is where UPSC sets its traps, because the evidence is thin and the temptation to over-read is strong.**

### What the evidence reasonably supports

| Observation | Reasonable inference |
|---|---|
| Different house sizes, some with more rooms and wells | **Some social differentiation** existed |
| Citadel vs lower town | Some functional and possibly social separation |
| Burials with pottery, ornaments, occasionally copper mirrors | Belief in an **afterlife**; modest grave goods |
| Great Bath, and bathrooms in most houses | **Ritual purity / cleanliness** mattered greatly |
| Mother Goddess terracotta figurines | Possibly a **fertility cult** |
| Pashupati seal | Possibly a **proto-Shiva** / yogic tradition |
| Pipal tree motifs on seals; animal motifs | **Nature worship**, tree and animal veneration |
| Fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal | Some form of **fire ritual** |

### ❌ What we do NOT have — the elimination checklist

Memorise this list. **Prelims questions are frequently built by asserting one of these.**

| ❌ NOT found | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **No temples** | Unlike Mesopotamia's ziggurats and Egypt's temples. No structure is *conclusively* a temple |
| **No palaces** | No royal residence identified. **We do not know how they were governed** |
| **No royal names, no king lists** | Contrast with Egypt's pharaohs |
| **No monumental tombs** | Burials are relatively simple. No pyramids |
| **No large-scale weapons or fortification for war** | Walls exist, but few weapons of war (no swords). **Little evidence of organised warfare** |
| **No horses (reliably), no horse-drawn chariots** | Central to Rigvedic culture, absent here |
| **No iron** | Bronze Age civilisation |
| **No coins/money** | Trade was barter-based, mediated by standard weights |
| **No deciphered script** | So no direct knowledge of their beliefs, laws or politics |

> 🎯 **The "who ruled?" question.** NCERT frames it honestly — we genuinely don't know. Three hypotheses circulate:
> 1. **A single powerful state** (uniformity of bricks, weights, planning suggests strong central control)
> 2. **Several city-states** sharing a common culture and standards
> 3. **A collective/oligarchic or priestly authority** with no single monarch
>
> **The correct exam answer acknowledges the uncertainty.** Overconfidence here is a wrong answer.

> 💻 The uniformity is exactly what you'd get from a **shared standards specification** — which could come from a central authority *or* from a widely-adopted convention among peers. **You cannot distinguish the two from the artefacts alone.** That's the honest state of the field.

## 9️⃣ The End — Why Did It Collapse?

Around **1900 BCE (~3,900 years ago)** the mature Harappan civilisation unravelled. Cities were abandoned, the script vanished, weights and seals fell out of use, long-distance trade dried up, and people dispersed into smaller village settlements.

### What we observe archaeologically

- Drains and street cleaning stop being maintained; rubbish accumulates in streets
- New, flimsier structures built over older planned ones — often encroaching on streets
- Craft specialisation declines; the distinctive pottery, seals and weights disappear
- Writing disappears entirely
- **Population shifts eastward and southward** toward the Ganga-Yamuna region and Gujarat

### The competing theories

| Theory | The argument | The problem with it |
|---|---|---|
| **🌊 River change / drying** ⭐ **(strongest today)** | The **Ghaggar-Hakra** system dried up or shifted course. Over **half of all Harappan sites** lay along it. The Indus also changed course and flooded | Best supported by geology and site distribution; explains the *scale* of abandonment |
| **🌦️ Climate change** ⭐ | Weakening of the monsoon around 2200–1900 BCE (a well-documented global arid event) → agriculture fails → surplus collapses → cities can't be fed | Strong palaeoclimate support; probably works *together* with the river theory |
| **🌳 Deforestation & environmental degradation** | Baking millions of bricks and smelting copper consumed enormous fuel wood → deforestation → soil erosion, flooding, reduced rainfall | Plausible contributory factor |
| **🐄 Overgrazing / soil exhaustion** | Land degraded by intensive use | Contributory |
| **🌊 Floods & tectonic shifts** | Repeated devastating floods at Mohenjodaro; tectonic uplift damming rivers | Evidence of flooding is real but localised |
| **📉 Collapse of trade** | Mesopotamian demand fell; the trade economy that sustained the cities failed | Contributory, not sufficient alone |
| **⚔️ "Aryan invasion"** ❌ | Proposed by **Mortimer Wheeler** from some unburied skeletons at Mohenjodaro | **Largely REJECTED.** The skeletons are from different periods and show no battle wounds; there is no destruction layer, no burnt cities, no mass graves. **Do not present this as the accepted view** |

> ⭐ **The mainstream consensus today:** a **combination** — primarily **climatic drying and hydrological change** (the Ghaggar-Hakra failing), compounded by environmental degradation and collapsing trade. **Gradual decline and de-urbanisation, not a single catastrophic event or invasion.**

> 🎯 **The exam-safe answer:** "*A multi-causal, gradual de-urbanisation, in which climatic aridification and river-course changes were probably decisive; the invasion hypothesis is no longer accepted.*" That sentence is both true and well-calibrated.

### Late Harappan and continuity

The civilisation didn't vanish — it **transformed**. The **Late Harappan** phase (~1900–1300 BCE) shows smaller, rural settlements continuing, with cultural continuity in pottery, some crafts and agricultural practice. Many Harappan traits (weights, bead-making, cotton, urban water management, possibly some religious motifs) resurface in later Indian culture.

> 🌏 **A comparison worth holding:** Egypt and Mesopotamia recovered and continued as literate urban societies. **India's urbanism disappeared for over a thousand years** — until the "Second Urbanisation" of the Ganga valley around 600 BCE (Chapter 5). That millennium-long gap is a genuinely distinctive feature of Indian history.

## 🔁 90-Second Recap

1. **~2600–1900 BCE**, the largest Bronze Age civilisation by area (~1.5 million sq km), contemporary with Egypt and Mesopotamia.
2. **Discovered 1921–22:** Harappa by **Daya Ram Sahni**, Mohenjodaro by **R. D. Banerji**; announced by **John Marshall**.
3. **City plan:** citadel (west, higher, walled) + lower town (east, larger, grid streets).
4. **Drainage is the signature achievement** — covered, sloped, universal, with manholes and soak pits — and **drains were planned before houses**, implying a real civic authority.
5. **Star structures:** Great Bath (Mohenjodaro), dockyard (Lothal), three-part city + signboard + reservoirs (Dholavira), ploughed field + fire altars (Kalibangan), no citadel (Chanhudaro).
6. **Extremities:** Manda (N), Daimabad (S), Alamgirpur (E), Sutkagendor (W); Shortughai in Afghanistan.
7. **Crops:** wheat, barley, pulses, sesame, mustard, **cotton** (world's first); rice at Lothal/Rangpur.
8. **Technology:** bronze, gold, silver, **faience** (man-made), bead-making, shell work, cotton textiles. **NO IRON.**
9. **Seals** of steatite with animal motifs; script of ~375–400 signs, right-to-left, boustrophedon, **undeciphered**.
10. **Weights** of chert, cubical, in a **binary** sequence with 16 as a key unit — standardised across the whole civilisation.
11. **Trade** with **Meluhha/Magan/Dilmun** (Indus/Oman/Bahrain); raw materials sourced from Afghanistan (lapis, tin), Rajasthan (copper), Karnataka (gold), Gujarat (carnelian, shell).
12. **No temples, palaces, royal names, monumental tombs, or clear evidence of warfare** — governance remains unknown.
13. **Decline ~1900 BCE:** multi-causal — river drying (Ghaggar-Hakra), climate aridification, environmental degradation, trade collapse. **Invasion theory rejected.**
14. **Urbanism then disappeared from India for ~1,000 years** until the Ganga valley's Second Urbanisation.
