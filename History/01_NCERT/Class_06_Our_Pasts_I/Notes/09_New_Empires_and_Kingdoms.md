                                                                            │
                                                              ▼
   Shungas, Kanvas, Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas, Satavahanas — 500 years
   of REGIONAL powers, and a period of enormous trade and artistic wealth
                                                                            │
                                                              ▼
   ──► GUPTAS (319 CE) ──► "Golden Age" ──► decline after the HUNA invasions
                                                                            │
                                                              ▼
   Harsha's brief revival (606–647 CE), then permanent regionalisation:
   PALLAVAS · CHALUKYAS · RASHTRAKUTAS · PALAS · PRATIHARAS · CHOLAS

                                   ⭐ AND THE CENTRE OF GRAVITY MOVES SOUTH
```

> 🔑 **The insight worth carrying:** "fragmentation" is not the same as "decline." The **post-Mauryan** centuries of political fragmentation produced the **greatest trade boom** and the **first Buddha images** (Ch.8). The **post-Gupta** fragmentation produced the **greatest temples** (Ch.10). **Political unity and cultural achievement are not the same variable** — and saying so is a genuinely sophisticated point in a Mains answer.

  ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │                                                                      │

  │                                                                      │

  │  disburses salaries               permanently and irrevocably        │
  │                                                                      │
  │  Direct central control        →  SAMANTAS — subordinate rulers who  │
  │                                   keep their own territory, army     │
  │                                   and revenue, and owe the overlord  │
  │                                   tribute, troops and attendance     │
  │                                                                      │

  │  Cheap and scalable — you don't need a huge paid bureaucracy or a    │

The Mauryan model was **centralised and expensive**; the Gupta and post-Gupta model was **decentralised and cheap** — and the price was long-run fragmentation. **Framing it as a deliberate trade-off rather than mere "decline" produces a much better answer.**

## 1️⃣ 📜 PRASHASTIS — A New Kind of Source

> A ***PRASHASTI*** (Sanskrit: "in praise of") is an **inscription composed by a court poet in praise of a king.**

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Language** | ⭐ **SANSKRIT** — note the change. Ashoka used **Prakrit** (Ch.7); the Guptas use **Sanskrit**. Sanskrit is now the language of power and prestige |
| **Content** | The king's lineage, conquests, virtues, learning, generosity, physical beauty — often in elaborate poetry |
| **Value** | Gives us **names, campaigns, geography, dynastic sequence, and court culture** |
| **⚠️ Bias** ⭐⭐ | **They are eulogies.** They exaggerate, omit defeats, and describe an ideal rather than a record. **Never treat a prashasti as neutral reportage** |

> 💻 **A prashasti is a marketing page authored by the vendor.** The product names and version numbers are usually accurate — the performance claims are not. **Use it for facts; discount its evaluations.** Saying exactly this in a Mains answer is worth marks.

### ⭐⭐⭐ The Prayag Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar Inscription)

**The single most important inscription of the Gupta period.**

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **About** | **SAMUDRAGUPTA** |
| **Composed by** | **HARISHENA** — his court poet and minister ⭐ |
| **Language** | **Sanskrit**, in a mix of verse and prose (*champu* style) |
| **Where** | On a **pillar at Allahabad (Prayagraj)** |
| **⭐ The delicious oddity** | **It is inscribed on an ASHOKAN PILLAR.** Samudragupta's eulogy was carved onto a pillar already bearing **Ashoka's edicts**, ~600 years older. **The same stone carries the message of a king who renounced conquest and, beneath it, the boasts of a king who conquered relentlessly.** ⭐ *(The pillar was later moved to Allahabad Fort by Akbar, and Jahangir added an inscription too — three empires on one stone.)* |

**The four categories of rulers Samudragupta dealt with — learn this classification, it's asked:**

| Category | Who | How they were treated |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Aryavarta (north India) — 9 rulers** | Rulers of the Ganga valley | ⭐ **UPROOTED and their territory ANNEXED** — total conquest |
| **2. Dakshinapatha (south) — 12 rulers** | Rulers of the Deccan and further south | ⭐ **DEFEATED, then REINSTATED** as tributaries — the *dharmavijaya* model. He could not hold the south, so he extracted submission and went home |
| **3. Forest/*Atavika* rulers** | Central Indian forest chiefs | **Reduced to servitude** |
| **4. Frontier states and *ganas*** | Border kingdoms (Samatata, Davaka, Kamarupa, Nepala, Kartripura) and republics (Malavas, Yaudheyas, Arjunayanas, Madrakas, Abhiras) | **Submitted, paid tribute, obeyed orders** — not annexed. ⭐ Note that **ganas still existed** here (Ch.5 link) and were finally absorbed in this period |
| **5. Foreign rulers** | Kushanas, Shakas, the king of Sri Lanka (**Meghavarna**) | Sought his favour, offered daughters in marriage, sent gifts |

> 🎯 **The north/south distinction is the analytically interesting bit.** Samudragupta **annexed** the north and merely **subdued** the south. That tells you where the Gupta empire's real, administered territory ended — a distinction that a careless "he conquered all India" answer misses entirely.

**Harishena also praises Samudragupta as:** a **poet** (*kaviraja*), a **musician** (some of his gold coins show him playing the **VEENA** ⭐), a patron of learning, and one who performed the **Ashvamedha** (his *ashvamedha*-type coins survive).

### 🏛️ Gupta administration

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Nature** | ⚠️ **LESS centralised than the Mauryas.** The Guptas ruled the core directly and governed the periphery through **subordinate/tributary rulers** |
| **Titles** | *Maharajadhiraja*, *Parama-bhattaraka*, *Parameshvara* — grand titles reflecting a claim to paramountcy |
| **Divisions** | *Bhukti* (province, under an *uparika*) → *Vishaya* (district, under a *vishayapati*) → *Grama* (village) |
| **Key innovation: LAND GRANTS** ⭐⭐ | Land given to **brahmanas (*agrahara*/*brahmadeya*)** and to officials, recorded on **COPPER PLATES**, frequently **tax-free and hereditary**, and often carrying the **right to collect revenue** and even administer |
| **Officials often unpaid in cash** | Paid instead by assignment of land revenue → **hereditary offices** emerge |
| **Army** | Feudatory levies significant alongside the standing army |
| **Coins** ⭐ | Magnificent **GOLD coins (*dinaras*)** — the most beautiful in ancient India: king with bow, axe, lyre/veena, tiger-slaying, ashvamedha, king-and-queen types |

> ⭐ **Why land grants matter so much** (this is the analytical core of the chapter): giving land **permanently and tax-free** to a grantee means the state has **irrevocably surrendered that revenue** and, increasingly, the administrative and judicial authority over that area. Over generations the grantees become **local lords**, and the centre becomes a coordinator of semi-independent powers. **This is the process historians debate as "Indian feudalism."**
>
> 💻 It's delegating an entitlement permanently instead of leasing it — you get immediate loyalty and zero administrative overhead, but you have given away the capability with no revocation mechanism. **Repeat that a few hundred times over two centuries and the centre is hollow.**

### ⚖️ Was it a "Golden Age"? — Argue Both Sides

**This is a standard Mains question. A one-sided answer is a weak answer.**

| ✅ The case FOR | ❌ The case AGAINST |
|---|---|
| **Literature:** Kalidasa, the Puranas compiled, Sanskrit drama flourishes | **Rigid caste:** varna hierarchy hardened; **untouchability intensified** — Fa Xian records that *chandalas* had to strike a warning stick on entering a town ⭐ |
| **Science:** **Aryabhata**, Varahamihira, Brahmagupta; **decimal system and zero** | **Women's position declined** — earlier marriage, restricted property rights, and the **first epigraphic record of sati (Eran inscription, 510 CE)** ⭐ |
| **Art:** Ajanta paintings, exquisite sculpture, temple architecture begins | **Slavery and forced labour (*vishti*)** continued and arguably grew |
| **Gold coinage** of high purity and artistry | **Prosperity was uneven** — concentrated in the north and among elites; the "golden age" was golden for a narrow stratum |
| **Political stability** across much of north India | **Not pan-Indian** — the south was outside it; and the label was coined by **nationalist historiography** partly to counter colonial claims of Indian backwardness |
| **Trade and urban life** substantial | **Urban decline** may have begun late in the period; land grants signal ruralisation |

> 🎯 **The formulation that scores:** *"The Gupta period saw exceptional achievement in literature, science and art, but the 'Golden Age' label — a product of nationalist historiography — obscures intensifying caste rigidity, a decline in women's status, and the uneven distribution of prosperity. It was a golden age for a narrow elite, not for society as a whole."*

### The Hunas

**Huna (Hephthalite/White Hun) invasions** from Central Asia hit the Guptas from the mid-5th century. **Skandagupta repelled them**, but the wars **drained the treasury** (visible in the declining gold content of coins). Later **Toramana** and **Mihirakula** established Huna rule in north-west India before being defeated by a coalition including **Yashodharman** of Malwa. **The Hunas were a major contributory cause of Gupta decline.**

## 3️⃣ 👑 HARSHAVARDHANA (r. 606–647 CE)

**The last great ruler of northern India before the medieval period.**

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Dynasty** | **Pushyabhuti (Vardhana)** dynasty |
| **Original capital** | **Thanesar (Sthanvishvara)**, Haryana → shifted to ⭐ **KANAUJ (Kanyakubja)** |
| **How he came to power** ⭐ | His elder brother **Rajyavardhana** was killed by **Shashanka** of Gauda (Bengal). Harsha, only ~16, succeeded and also took over **Kanauj** on behalf of his widowed sister **RAJYASHRI** — whom he rescued from the Vindhya forest as she was about to immolate herself |
| **Extent** | Most of **north India** — Punjab, UP, Bihar, Bengal, Odisha; and Gujarat/Malwa influence |
| **⭐ THE GREAT CHECK** | **DEFEATED by PULAKESHIN II** of the **Chalukyas** on the banks of the **NARMADA** (c. 618–619 CE). **Harsha could never expand south of the Narmada** — the river became the boundary between them ⭐⭐ |
| **Religion** | Raised a Shaiva; became a patron of **Buddhism (Mahayana)**, but remained tolerant — patronised Shaivism and the Sun as well |
| **Assemblies** ⭐ | Held a grand assembly at **KANAUJ** in honour of Xuan Zang, and a quinquennial (every 5 years) distribution of charity at **PRAYAG (Allahabad)**, giving away his accumulated wealth |
| **Administration** | Similar to the Guptas but **more decentralised**; heavy reliance on **feudatories (*samantas*)**; **more land grants**, now also to officials |
| **Death** | Died **647 CE** without an heir → the empire dissolved immediately. His minister **Arunashva** usurped power and attacked a Chinese mission, prompting a Tibetan–Nepali force to intervene |

### The sources for Harsha ⭐⭐ — a great Chapter 1 case study

| Source | Author | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ***HARSHACHARITA*** ⭐⭐ | **BANABHATTA** — his court poet | A Sanskrit biography; **the first historical biography (*charita*) in Sanskrit literature** ⭐. Eulogistic and incomplete (it breaks off early), but rich on court life |
| ***SI-YU-KI*** ⭐⭐ | **XUAN ZANG** | The Chinese pilgrim's account — outsider's perspective; describes administration, Nalanda, cities, roads and society |
| **Harsha's own plays** ⭐ | **Harsha himself** | ***Nagananda***, ***Ratnavali***, ***Priyadarshika*** — he was a genuine dramatist |
| **Inscriptions** | — | Banskhera and Madhuban copper plates (which bear **Harsha's own signature** ⭐) |

> 🎯 **This is the ideal source situation and worth pointing out in an answer:** you have a **court biography** (insider, flattering), a **foreign traveller's account** (outsider, differently biased), the ruler's **own literary works**, and **inscriptions** (contemporary, official). **Triangulating across all four is exactly the method Chapter 1 taught.**

**Xuan Zang on Harsha's India:** roads reasonably safe but robbery not unknown (he was himself robbed); punishments harsher than under the Guptas; **taxation light — about 1/6**; a well-organised charity system; Nalanda thriving with thousands of students.

## 4️⃣ 🌴 THE SOUTH RISES

The most important structural development of this chapter: **after the Guptas, the political and cultural centre of gravity shifts south.**

| Dynasty | Capital | Period | Known for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PALLAVAS** ⭐⭐ | **KANCHIPURAM** | ~3rd–9th c. CE | ⭐ **Mahabalipuram** (rathas, Shore Temple), **Kailasanatha temple** at Kanchi; **rock-cut → structural temple transition**; **Mahendravarman I** (also a playwright — *Mattavilasa Prahasana*), **Narasimhavarman I "Mamalla"** (defeated and sacked Vatapi) |
| **CHALUKYAS of Badami** ⭐⭐ | **BADAMI (Vatapi)** | ~6th–8th c. CE | ⭐ **AIHOLE, BADAMI, PATTADAKAL** temples (**Pattadakal is a UNESCO World Heritage Site**); **PULAKESHIN II** — defeated **Harsha** |
| **PANDYAS** | Madurai | Revived ~6th c. | South Tamil Nadu; Meenakshi temple tradition |
| **CHOLAS** ⭐⭐⭐ | **Thanjavur** (later Gangaikondacholapuram) | Great from ~9th c. | ⭐ **Village self-government**; **Brihadeshwara temple**; **bronze Nataraja**; naval power |
| **RASHTRAKUTAS** ⭐ | Manyakheta | 8th–10th c. | ⭐ **KAILASHNATHA TEMPLE at ELLORA** (Krishna I) — the world's largest monolithic rock-cut structure |
| **PALAS** | Bengal/Bihar | 8th–12th c. | Buddhism; **Vikramshila** and **Odantapuri** universities; Dharmapala, Devapala |
| **PRATIHARAS** | Kanauj | 8th–11th c. | One of the three contenders in the **Tripartite Struggle** |

> ⭐ **The Tripartite Struggle** — **Palas (east), Pratiharas (west/north), Rashtrakutas (Deccan)** fought for control of **KANAUJ** for roughly two centuries (8th–10th c.). Kanauj had become the symbolic key to north Indian paramountcy after Harsha. **Know the three names and the prize.**

### ⚔️ Pallava vs Chalukya — and the Aihole Inscription ⭐⭐

The two great southern powers fought repeatedly across the Tungabhadra doab.

- **PULAKESHIN II** (Chalukya, r. 610–642) — his achievements are recorded in the ⭐⭐ **AIHOLE INSCRIPTION**, composed by his court poet **RAVIKIRTI** in **Sanskrit**. It records his **victory over Harshavardhana** and is one of the most important inscriptions of South India. *(Ravikirti immodestly compares himself to Kalidasa and Bharavi in the same inscription.)*
- Pulakeshin II also received an **embassy from the Persian king Khusrau II** — depicted, it is thought, in an **Ajanta painting** ⭐
- **NARASIMHAVARMAN I "MAMALLA"** (Pallava) avenged earlier defeats by **capturing and sacking the Chalukya capital VATAPI (Badami)** in c. 642, taking the title ***Vatapikonda*** ("conqueror of Vatapi"), and **Pulakeshin II died** in that conflict
- **Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram)** is named after **Mamalla** ⭐

> 🧠 **Compressed: "Pulakeshin II beat Harsha at the Narmada; Narasimhavarman I sacked Pulakeshin's capital Vatapi."** Both facts are asked.

### 🗳️ ⭐⭐⭐ SOUTH INDIAN LOCAL SELF-GOVERNMENT — The Crown Jewel

**This is the single most valuable thing in this chapter for cross-syllabus use.**

Village assemblies in Pallava and especially **Chola** territory:

| Assembly | Composition |
|---|---|
| ***UR*** ⭐ | Assembly of a **general/ordinary village** — of the landholding cultivators (non-brahmadeya villages) |
| ***SABHA*** ⭐⭐ | Assembly of a ***BRAHMADEYA*** village — one granted to **brahmanas**. **The most elaborately organised** |
| ***NAGARAM*** ⭐ | Assembly of **merchants** — governing a trading town |

**The *sabha* worked through committees (*variyams*)** — for irrigation (*eri-variyam*), gardens, justice, temples, gold, and annual affairs.

### 🏛️ The Uttaramerur Inscription ⭐⭐⭐

**Learn this. It is repeatedly asked and is superb Mains material.**

At **Uttaramerur (Kanchipuram district, Tamil Nadu)**, inscriptions of the Chola king **PARANTAKA I (c. 921 CE)** record, **in extraordinary procedural detail, how the village assembly's committee members were selected.**

```
   ╔═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗
   ║      THE UTTARAMERUR ELECTION PROCEDURE (KUDAVOLAI SYSTEM)            ║
   ╚═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝

   ✅ QUALIFICATIONS TO STAND:
      • Own tax-paying land of a prescribed minimum extent
      • Own a house on legally-owned land
      • Be between 35 and 70 years of age
      • Know the Vedas and administration (or, with less land, be
        well-versed in one Veda and its commentaries)
      • Be honest and have earned income by honest means

   ❌ DISQUALIFICATIONS — genuinely remarkable:
      • Anyone who has served on a committee in the last 3 years
        → ⭐ A COOLING-OFF / ROTATION RULE
      • Anyone who has NOT SUBMITTED ACCOUNTS of a previous term
        → ⭐ AUDIT AND ACCOUNTABILITY, enforced by disqualification
      • Anyone (or their relatives) guilty of specified offences,
        theft, or having taken others' property
      • Those who had committed sins, or associated with outcastes

   🗳️ THE METHOD — "KUDAVOLAI" ("pot-ticket"):
      1. Names of eligible candidates from each ward written on
         palm-leaf tickets
      2. Tickets placed in a POT
      3. A YOUNG BOY, too young to have any interest, draws the
         tickets out in a public assembly
      4. The drawn names are read out and appointed to committees
      → SELECTION BY LOT from a QUALIFIED, VETTED pool
```

> 🎯 **Why this earns marks in almost any governance answer:** here, in a 10th-century village inscription, are **eligibility criteria, explicit disqualifications, an anti-incumbency/rotation rule, a mandatory audit requirement enforced by debarment, and a transparent public selection procedure.** Those are the components of modern electoral law.
>
> ⚠️ **But be honest about the limits, exactly as with the gana-sanghas (Ch.5):** participation was restricted to **male, property-owning, Veda-learned brahmanas** in the *brahmadeya* villages. **It was sophisticated local self-government, not universal franchise.**
>
> 🔗 **The full lineage to deploy in GS2:** **Vajji gana-sangha (Ch.5) → Buddhist sangha's procedural rules (Ch.6) → Chola *sabha* and Uttaramerur (Ch.9) → 73rd/74th Amendments.** **That four-point arc is one of the strongest introductions you can write for a question on local self-government or the roots of Indian democracy.** Cross-reference your Polity notes.

## 5️⃣ ⚙️ How Kingdoms Were Changing: Samantas and Land Grants

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ***SAMANTA*** ⭐⭐ | A **subordinate ruler**. He kept his own territory, army and revenue; in return he brought **gifts, troops when summoned, and attended the overlord's court**. If the overlord weakened, the samanta declared independence |
| ***Brahmadeya* / *Agrahara*** ⭐ | Land granted to **brahmanas**, usually **tax-free and hereditary** |
| ***Devadana*** | Land granted to a **temple** |
| **Copper plates** ⭐ | The medium on which grants were recorded — the legal title deed |

> 🎯 **Why kings made these grants** — give more than one reason in an answer:
> 1. **Religious merit** and legitimacy
> 2. **To extend agriculture into forest and tribal areas** — brahmana settlers brought plough agriculture, new crops, and a new social order to frontier regions ⭐ (a genuinely important, often-missed function)
> 3. **To reward officials without cash** in an economy where coinage was contracting
> 4. **To spread Sanskritic culture and legitimising ideology** into newly-incorporated regions

> ⚠️ **The long-run cost:** each grant permanently alienated revenue and authority. **Kings were buying short-term loyalty with long-term sovereignty.**

## 6️⃣ 🗓️ 712 CE — A Date to Note

In **712 CE**, an Arab army under **MUHAMMAD BIN QASIM** conquered **SINDH**, defeating **Raja Dahir**.

- The first significant **Arab/Islamic political presence** in the subcontinent
- Its **direct political impact was limited and regional** — Sindh remained a frontier province and Arab power did not penetrate further
- But it opened sustained contact: ⭐ **Indian mathematics and astronomy (including the decimal numeral system and Brahmagupta's works) passed to the Arab world through this contact**, and thence to Europe as "Arabic numerals"

> 🎯 **The intellectually satisfying part:** the numeral system you use daily travelled **India → Arab world → Europe**, which is why Europeans call them "Arabic numerals" while Arabs called them ***al-arqam al-hindiyyah*** — "**Indian numerals**." **Ch.10 will give you Aryabhata and Brahmagupta, the source of that export.**

## 7️⃣ 🧑‍🌾 Ordinary People's Lives

NCERT deliberately closes with the non-royal.

**From the *Harshacharita*, Xuan Zang, and Sanskrit drama:**

- **Villages were the overwhelming reality** — most people were cultivators, herders, artisans and labourers
- ⭐ **A striking observation from Sanskrit plays:** the **kings and brahmanas speak SANSKRIT**, while **women and lower-status characters speak PRAKRIT**. **The language on stage encodes the social hierarchy** — a beautiful, quotable illustration of how deeply stratification ran
- **Xuan Zang** describes **untouchability** clearly — *chandalas* lived outside towns and had to announce their approach
- **Land grants brought new pressures** — villagers now owed dues to a grantee rather than the state, and their obligations were fixed in a copper plate they could not read
- **Slavery and *vishti* (forced labour)** continued

> 💻 **The Sanskrit/Prakrit split in drama is worth pausing on.** It's an access-control model expressed through language: the prestige register is available only to those with the social permissions to use it, and the play *encodes and reinforces* those permissions every time it's performed. **Chapter 6's insight — that the Buddha's choice of Prakrit was itself radical — lands with full force here.**

## 🔁 2-MINUTE RECAP

1. **Prashastis** are Sanskrit eulogies — factually useful, evaluatively biased. Note the language shift: **Ashoka = Prakrit; Guptas = Sanskrit**.
2. **Prayag Prashasti:** **Samudragupta**, composed by **Harishena**, carved **on an Ashokan pillar**. North rulers **uprooted**; southern rulers **defeated and reinstated**.
3. **Gupta rulers:** Chandragupta I (**Licchavi marriage**, Gupta era 319 CE) → **Samudragupta** (conqueror, veena) → **Chandragupta II** (**beat the Shakas**, **Fa Xian**, Vikramaditya) → Kumaragupta I (**Nalanda**) → **Skandagupta** (**repelled the Hunas**, **Sudarshana lake**).
4. **Gupta administration was LESS centralised than Mauryan**, relying on **land grants on copper plates** — often tax-free and hereditary → the **"Indian feudalism"** debate.
5. **"Golden Age" is contested:** brilliant literature, science and art, but hardening caste, declining women's status (**first sati record, Eran 510 CE**), and uneven prosperity.
6. **Harsha (606–647):** Thanesar → **Kanauj**; sister **Rajyashri**; **defeated by Pulakeshin II at the Narmada**; sources = ***Harshacharita*** (Banabhatta), **Xuan Zang**, his own **three plays**, and inscriptions.
7. **The south rises:** **Pallavas** (Kanchipuram, Mahabalipuram), **Chalukyas** (Badami; Aihole, Pattadakal), **Cholas** (Thanjavur), **Rashtrakutas** (Ellora Kailashnatha), **Palas**, **Pratiharas**.
8. **Pulakeshin II** (**Aihole inscription** by **Ravikirti**) beat Harsha; **Narasimhavarman I** later sacked **Vatapi**.
9. **Tripartite Struggle:** Palas, Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas fight over **Kanauj**.
10. **Local self-government:** ***ur*** (ordinary village), ***sabha*** (brahmadeya, with **variyams**), ***nagaram*** (merchants).
11. **Uttaramerur (Parantaka I, c. 921 CE):** the **kudavolai** pot-ticket system — age 35–70, property and learning qualifications, **3-year cooling-off**, **mandatory submission of accounts**. Sophisticated but **restricted** franchise.
12. **Samantas** kept their own armies and revenue — the political structure was devolving.
13. **712 CE:** **Muhammad bin Qasim** takes **Sindh**; the contact route by which **Indian numerals reached the Arab world and then Europe**.
14. **In Sanskrit drama, kings and brahmanas speak Sanskrit while women and lower-status characters speak Prakrit** — hierarchy encoded in language itself.
