## The rubric I mark against

Ten marks, five criteria. Deliberately the same five every week so the score is comparable over time and you can see the curve.

| # | Criterion | 2 marks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Directive obeyed** | Answered the verb that was asked — verdict given if "critically", extent stated if "to what extent" |
| 2 | **Both sides** | The strongest counter-argument appears, not a strawman |
| 3 | **Specificity** | Named article / committee / scheme / judgment — not "the government should" |
| 4 | **Evidence** | At least two numbers or concrete examples, correctly attributed |
| 5 | **Structure** | Anchored intro, headed body, forward-looking conclusion, within word limit |

**The score matters less than the pattern.** Four weeks in, one of these five will be visibly your weak column — and that becomes the thing to drill, instead of vaguely "getting better at Mains."

## Why one issue a week is enough

Forty issues, each argued from four lenses, gives ~160 reusable argument-blocks. GS questions recombine a much smaller set of tensions than the topic list suggests — an issue argued properly transfers to questions that look unrelated. The delimitation lenses (federal bargain, equity vs autonomy, comparative models) are the *same lenses* that answer questions on GST Council voting, All-India Services, and Governor's powers.

**Depth on few beats exposure to many.** That is the opposite of how Prelims works, and confusing the two is why most people's Mains preparation stalls.
