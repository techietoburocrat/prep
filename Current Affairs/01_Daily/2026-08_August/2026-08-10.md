## 1. 🗳️ Delimitation — the stakes, and the arithmetic

### Why in news

The delimitation debate returned to prominence as the **2027 Census** approaches. The **freeze on Lok Sabha seat allocation**, in place since **1976 and based on 1971 population**, was extended by the **84th Amendment (2001)** until **"the first census taken after 2026"** — which is the 2027 Census. That single clause is now due.

### The summary

India froze the link between population and parliamentary representation for **fifty-one years** in order to make family planning politically survivable. States that reduced fertility fastest would otherwise have lost seats to states that did not — a perverse incentive that would have destroyed population policy. That freeze now expires. **Unfreezing it transfers parliamentary power from South and West India to the Hindi-belt North**, which is why this is not a technical exercise but the most serious federal question India faces this decade.

### 📐 The constitutional machinery

| Provision | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Article 81** | Composition of the **Lok Sabha** — ceiling of **550** elected members (530 states + 20 UTs) |
| **Article 82** | **Readjustment after each census**, by an authority Parliament determines by law |
| **Article 170** | The same, for **State Legislative Assemblies** |
| **Article 330** | Reservation of seats for **SC/ST in the Lok Sabha** |
| **Article 332** | Reservation of seats for **SC/ST in State Assemblies** |
| **Article 243D** | Reservation in **Panchayats** |
| **Delimitation Act** | Parliament enacts one each cycle — **1952, 1962, 1972, 2002** |

**The freeze, in order:**

| Amendment | Year | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **42nd Amendment** | **1976** | Froze seat allocation at **1971** population levels, until the **2001 Census** |
| **84th Amendment** | **2001** | Extended the freeze to **"the first census taken after 2026"**; permitted redrawing of *boundaries within* states using 1991/2001 figures |
| **87th Amendment** | **2003** | Allowed the boundary redraw to use **2001** Census figures |

**So the 2002 Delimitation Commission redrew constituency boundaries but did not change any state's seat count.** That distinction — **boundaries yes, seat numbers no** — is the fact examiners test.

### 📊 The arithmetic that makes this explosive

| | |
|---|---|
| **Current Lok Sabha strength** | **543** elected (ceiling **550** under Art 81) |
| **Basis** | **1971 Census** population |
| **Projected strength if unfrozen** | ~**850** seats has been the working figure in the debate |
| **A Kerala MP represents** | ~**14 lakh** electors |
| **A UP / Bihar MP represents** | ~**19 lakh** electors |
| **The rejected bill** | **Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill, 2026** — sought to raise the ceiling from **550 to 850** and operationalise women's reservation through a fresh delimitation. **Introduced 16 April 2026, negatived 17 April 2026** ⚠️ *(see [Week 1 Review](_Week_01_Review.md))* |
| **Women's reservation** | The **106th Amendment (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam), 2023** makes one-third reservation **contingent on a census and a delimitation exercise** — so the two questions are now legally welded together |

**The structural unfairness runs both ways, and a good answer says so.** A Kerala voter currently has *more* representation per person than a Bihar voter — which offends the "one person, one vote, one value" principle the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed. **Equally, states that met national family-planning goals would be penalised for having done so.** Both statements are true. The exam rewards holding both.

### The core concerns

1. **Federal balance.** Southern and western states contribute a disproportionate share of GDP and tax revenue while their parliamentary weight would fall. Combined with **Finance Commission devolution** — where population is already a criterion — the perception is of a **double penalty**, and this is exactly where the delimitation debate merges with the fiscal-federalism debate.
2. **Gerrymandering risk.** Redrawing boundaries permits **"cracking"** (splitting a concentrated community across seats to dilute it) and **"packing"** (concentrating it in one seat to waste its votes). India's Delimitation Commission has been insulated from this historically; the guarantee is institutional, not statutory.
3. **The Rajya Sabha does not compensate.** In a classic federation the upper house equalises states — the US Senate gives Wyoming and California two seats each. **India's Rajya Sabha allocates seats by population too**, so it does *not* offset a population-based Lok Sabha. This is the single most useful comparative point available on this topic.
4. **Physical and procedural feasibility.** 850 members require a chamber, committee capacity and floor time. The **new Parliament building's Lok Sabha chamber was built with capacity for around 888 seats** — which tells you the expansion has been anticipated for years.
5. **Delimitation Commission orders are unchallengeable in court** (under the Delimitation Acts), which raises the stakes on getting the composition and criteria right the first time.

### The four proposals on the table

| Proposal | The idea | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Cap the loss** | Expand the House but **freeze each state's *share*** — nobody loses proportional weight | Breaks one-person-one-vote at the national level |
| **Strengthen the Rajya Sabha** | Give states equal or weighted representation in the upper house, US-style | Requires a fundamental amendment to the federal design |
| **GIS-based transparent redraw** | Publish the mapping algorithm and data so boundaries are auditable | Solves gerrymandering, not the federal split |
| **Phase it in** | Stagger implementation, and roll out women-reserved constituencies over cycles | Delays, does not resolve |

### What needs to happen

- **Decide the principle before the Census output arrives**, not after — once the numbers are public, every option becomes a zero-sum negotiation between states
- **Consider decoupling the women's reservation** from delimitation so one constitutional promise is not hostage to another
- **Publish delimitation criteria and data in advance**, with GIS mapping, so the process is auditable even though its orders are not justiciable
- **Address the fiscal-federal grievance in parallel** — through the Finance Commission's terms of reference — because delimitation cannot be settled while it is carrying the whole weight of North–South resentment

> 📖 **The story behind it.** The freeze was **Indira Gandhi's**, and it was enacted in **1976 — during the Emergency**, in the **42nd Amendment**, at exactly the moment the government's **coercive sterilisation programme** was at its most aggressive. The logic was blunt: **you cannot ask states to cut their populations while telling them that doing so will cost them seats in Parliament.** So representation was decoupled from population, and the incentive was neutralised.
>
> It worked. **Kerala and Tamil Nadu reached replacement-level fertility decades before Bihar and Uttar Pradesh** — Kerala's TFR is now well under 2, while Bihar's remained above 3 for a long period. The policy achieved precisely what it was designed to achieve.
>
> **And that success is the problem.** The freeze was a fifty-one-year loan against a demographic future that has now arrived. The states that complied are asking why compliance should cost them power; the states that did not are asking why their citizens should count for less. **There is no answer that is fair on both principles simultaneously** — which is the definition of a genuine constitutional dilemma rather than a policy question, and precisely why it is such good Mains material.
>
> Worth knowing as comparison: the **United States capped its House at 435 in 1929** and now redistributes those fixed seats among states after each census — meaning some states lose seats outright every decade. **India has never had to do that, because it has never unfrozen.**

> 🏛️ **Bodies involved**
>
> **Delimitation Commission** · **Type + instrument:** **Statutory** — constituted under a **Delimitation Act** passed by Parliament for each exercise (**1952, 1962, 1972, 2002**) · **Composition:** a **serving or retired Supreme Court judge as Chairperson**, the **Chief Election Commissioner or an Election Commissioner**, and the **State Election Commissioner** of the state concerned · **The distinguisher:** **its orders have the force of law and cannot be questioned in any court.** It is *not* a permanent body — it is constituted, does its work, and dissolves.
> · **Siblings:** **Election Commission of India** — **constitutional, Article 324**, permanent, and a **different body** from the Delimitation Commission though the CEC sits on it. **State Election Commissions** — constitutional under **Articles 243K and 243ZA**, and they handle **panchayat and municipal** elections, not state assembly elections. That last confusion is one of the most common errors in Polity.

> **Why it's asked.** The freeze chronology is the highest-value block in this item: **42nd Amendment 1976 froze it at 1971 levels · 84th Amendment 2001 extended it to "the first census after 2026" · 87th Amendment 2003 allowed the boundary redraw on 2001 figures · the 2002 Commission changed boundaries but not seat counts.** Then: **Art 81 (LS composition, ceiling 550) · Art 82 (readjustment) · Art 170 (assemblies) · Art 330/332 (SC/ST reservation)**. And the composition of the Delimitation Commission — **retired/serving SC judge + CEC or an EC + State Election Commissioner** — with the killer fact that **its orders cannot be challenged in court.** Finally, the **106th Amendment, 2023** ties women's reservation to census + delimitation.

## 2. ⚖️♀️ "Judgments and Gender" — the Supreme Court's report on how judges write

### Why in news

The **Supreme Court** released **"Judgments and Gender: Sensitivity and Compassion in Writing Judgments"**, replacing the **2023 Handbook on Combating Gender Stereotypes**. The work was led by former **Justice Aniruddha Bose**, and rests on an analysis of **125 trial court judgments** across the country.

### The summary

This is a report about **language as a legal instrument**. Its argument is that the words a judgment uses are not decoration on the reasoning — they **encode assumptions that determine outcomes**, and in sexual-offence cases those assumptions have historically run against the complainant. The remedy proposed is unusually concrete: a **glossary**, procedural rules for cross-examination, and training. It is a rare document that treats judicial *writing* as a reformable practice rather than a matter of individual style.

### What it actually recommends

**On language**

| Replace | With | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **"prosecutrix"** | **"victim", "survivor", or "complainant"** | "Prosecutrix" is a Latinate label that exists only for women in sexual offence cases — it marks her as a category, not a person |
| Chastity and morality framing | **"violation of bodily autonomy"** | Locates the wrong in **consent**, not in the woman's character or "honour" |
| Gendered assumptions about LGBTQIA+ persons | The **SOGIESC** framework — Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity, Gender Expression and Sex Characteristics | Gives courts vocabulary that does not presume the binary |

**On procedure**

- Witnesses to be treated as **"invited guests"** of the court, with dignity
- **Strict prohibition on irrelevant cross-examination**, enforced under the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023**
- **Trauma-informed judging** — recognise that **delayed reporting and minor inconsistencies in testimony are consistent with trauma**, not evidence of falsity
- **Mandatory in-camera trials and identity protection** under the **BNSS, 2023**
- **Pre-trial counselling** for survivors, and **active judicial intervention** during cross-examination
- **Mandatory soft-skills training** through **State Judicial Academies**, and adoption of a **standardised glossary** in the lower judiciary

### 📊 The evidence base

| | |
|---|---|
| **Judgments analysed** | **125** trial court judgments nationwide |
| **Finding** | Recurring **patriarchal language patterns** in judicial writing |
| **Witness Protection Scheme awareness** | **80.7%** of those surveyed were **unaware** of it ⚠️ |
| **Predecessor** | **Handbook on Combating Gender Stereotypes, 2023** — now replaced |

### The legal ground it stands on

| Law / case | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **BNS, BNSS, BSA — 2023** | The three new criminal laws, in force **1 July 2024**, replacing IPC, CrPC and the Evidence Act |
| **Section 53A / the erstwhile Section 155(4)** | The **"character evidence" bar** — a complainant's sexual history is **inadmissible** on consent. This was the reform the 2003 amendment made, and the report says trial courts still breach it in substance |
| ***Aparna Bhat v. State of MP*** **(2021)** | The Supreme Court **barred courts from imposing conditions like rakhi-tying on bail**, and laid down gender-sensitisation directions for judges — the direct ancestor of this report |
| **Witness Protection Scheme, 2018** | Approved by the Supreme Court in ***Mahender Chawla v. Union of India*** — three categories of threat perception |
| **Article 21** | Dignity as part of the right to life — the constitutional anchor for all of it |
| **Article 39A** | Equal justice and free legal aid |

### The core concerns

1. **A report is not a rule.** The document is persuasive, not binding. Its adoption depends on **State Judicial Academies** and, ultimately, individual judges.
2. **The trial court is where it matters and where reform is slowest.** Appellate language has improved considerably; the **125 trial judgments** are the point — that is where survivors actually appear.
3. **"Trauma-informed judging" cuts against a genuine principle.** Criminal law places the burden on the prosecution and gives the accused a right to test evidence. Instructing courts that inconsistency may indicate trauma rather than falsehood is defensible, but it **must not become a presumption of guilt**. The honest Mains answer names this tension rather than pretending it away.
4. **Language reform can become performative.** Changing "prosecutrix" to "survivor" while keeping the same reasoning changes nothing. The report's own procedural recommendations are the substantive half.
5. **80.7% unaware of witness protection** is the most damning number in the document — a scheme nobody knows about protects nobody.

> 📖 **The story behind it.** The word ***prosecutrix*** is a genuine linguistic fossil. It is the **Latin feminine of *prosecutor***, and it entered Indian judgments through **19th-century English legal usage** — at a time when a woman could not herself prosecute, and the offence of rape was understood in law as a wrong against **her guardian's property interest in her chastity**, not against her. The word survived the law that produced it by about a century and a half.
>
> Indian courts had already been forced into this territory once. The **Mathura custodial rape case (*Tuka Ram v. State of Maharashtra*, 1979)** — where the Supreme Court acquitted policemen partly because the complainant bore no injuries and was held to be "habituated to sexual intercourse" — provoked **an open letter from four law professors (Upendra Baxi, Lotika Sarkar, Raghunath Kelkar, Vasudha Dhagamwar)** that became the founding document of India's modern women's movement. It produced the **Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983**, which created **Section 114A of the Evidence Act — a presumption of absence of consent in custodial rape** — and closed in-camera trial provisions.
>
> **So the sequence is: Mathura 1979 → the open letter → the 1983 amendment → the 2003 bar on character evidence → Nirbhaya and the Justice Verma Committee, 2013 → *Aparna Bhat*, 2021 → this report, 2026.** Every step was forced by a case. That is worth saying in an answer: **India's law on sexual violence has never been reformed in the abstract; it has been reformed by outrage, one judgment at a time.**

> 🏛️ **Bodies involved**
>
> **State Judicial Academies** · **Type:** state-level training institutions under the respective **High Courts** · **The distinguisher:** judicial training is controlled by the **judiciary**, not the executive — which is why a reform of judicial writing must go through the academies rather than through a government circular. Their national counterpart is the **National Judicial Academy, Bhopal** (a society, funded by the Centre, governed by the Supreme Court).
> · **Related:** **NALSA** — **statutory**, Legal Services Authorities Act **1987**, **patron-in-chief is the CJI**, constitutional anchor **Article 39A**. · **NCW** — **statutory**, National Commission for Women Act **1990**. Contrast with **NCW ≠ NCSC/NCST/NCBC**, which are constitutional. See [00_INSTITUTIONS](../../00_INSTITUTIONS.md) §2.

> **Why it's asked.** The examinable spine: the report **replaces the 2023 Handbook on Combating Gender Stereotypes**; it recommends dropping **"prosecutrix"**; it introduces the **SOGIESC** acronym; it relies on **BSA 2023** for cross-examination limits and **BNSS 2023** for in-camera trials and identity protection. The three new criminal laws — **BNS, BNSS, BSA, in force 1 July 2024** — and which replaced what (**IPC → BNS · CrPC → BNSS · Evidence Act → BSA**) is itself a high-frequency Prelims item. ***Aparna Bhat* (2021)** and the **Witness Protection Scheme (2018)** are the two case/scheme names to carry.

## 3. ⚡ India crosses 300 GW of non-fossil power capacity

### Why in news

India reached **300.50 GW of installed non-fossil-fuel electricity capacity** as of **31 July 2026** — **54% of total installed capacity**, crossing the halfway mark.

### The summary

India's **Paris NDC target was 40% non-fossil installed capacity by 2030**; that was met in **2021, nine years early**, and revised upward to **50% by 2030** under the **updated NDC (2022)**. That too has now been passed. The live target is **500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030**, announced at **COP26 Glasgow (2021)** as part of the **Panchamrit** commitments. **300 GW is 60% of the way there, with four and a half years left** — the remaining 200 GW is a steeper climb than the last 100, for reasons that are about the grid rather than about panels.

### 📊 The capacity picture

| Source | Installed (July 2026) |
|---|---|
| **Solar** | **164.59 GW** — the largest single source |
| **Wind** | **58.14 GW** |
| **Hydro** | **57.24 GW** |
| **Bio-power** | **11.75 GW** |
| **Nuclear** | **8.78 GW** |
| **Total non-fossil** | **300.50 GW — 54%** of installed capacity |

| | |
|---|---|
| **Added in 2025–26 alone** | **55.29 GW** — of which **44.6 GW solar** and **6 GW wind** |
| **Renewable generation** | **190.96 BU** (2014–15) → **477.79 BU** (2025–26) |
| **Domestic solar PV module capacity (ALMM)** | **> 200 GW** |
| **The 2030 target** | **500 GW non-fossil** |

### 💰 The policy scaffolding

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **PLI — National Programme on High Efficiency Solar PV Modules** | Builds domestic **ingot–wafer–cell–module** manufacturing, not just assembly |
| **ALMM** | Approved List of Models and Manufacturers — only listed modules qualify for government-supported projects. **The core import-substitution tool** |
| **PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana** | Rooftop solar for **1 crore households** — **₹75,021 crore** |
| **PM-KUSUM** | Solar pumps and decentralised solar for farmers |
| **Green Energy Corridors** | Transmission built specifically to evacuate renewable power |
| **Green Hydrogen Mission** | **₹19,744 crore**; target **5 MMT** annual production by 2030 |
| **Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO)** | Statutory obligation on discoms to buy a share of renewable power |
| **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, 2023** | Compliance carbon market under the **Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022** |

### The core concerns — where the argument actually is

1. **Installed capacity is not generation.** Solar has a **capacity utilisation factor of roughly 20%** and wind around **25%**, against **55–70%** for coal. **54% of capacity produces well under 54% of electricity** — coal still supplies the majority of units generated. Quoting the capacity figure as though it were a generation figure is the single most common error in answers on this topic, and examiners know it.
2. **Storage is the binding constraint.** Solar peaks at midday; demand peaks in the evening. Without **battery storage and pumped hydro**, additional solar capacity increasingly displaces nothing. This is why **Viability Gap Funding for Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)** and **pumped storage policy** now matter more than another gigawatt of panels.
3. **Discom finances.** The distribution companies that must buy this power are loss-making in most states. **RDSS** exists to fix that; until it does, renewable capacity faces a buyer with no money — and curtailment follows.
4. **Grid integration and land.** Variable generation needs transmission and balancing capacity in the right places; **Green Energy Corridors** are the answer, and they lag generation.
5. **Nuclear is the quiet gap.** At **8.78 GW**, nuclear remains a small slice despite being the only firm, dispatchable non-fossil source. **This is exactly what the SHANTI Bill 2025 — opening nuclear to private participation and making AERB statutory — is meant to change.** *(Do not confuse it with the SHANTI maritime framework — see the note in [00_START_HERE](../../00_START_HERE.md).)*

### What needs to happen

- **Storage-first procurement** — tender for firm and dispatchable renewable power, not raw capacity
- **Finish the Green Energy Corridors** and reform discom finances through RDSS, or the power has nowhere to go and nobody to pay for it
- **Deepen manufacturing upstream** to polysilicon and wafers — ALMM secures modules but the cell and wafer stages are still import-dependent
- **Treat nuclear as part of the non-fossil answer**, not a separate debate

> 📖 **The story behind it.** The word to know here is **Panchamrit** — "five nectars", the name given to the five climate commitments **Prime Minister Modi announced at COP26, Glasgow, in November 2021**: (1) **500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030**; (2) **50% of energy requirements from renewables by 2030**; (3) reduce projected carbon emissions by **1 billion tonnes** by 2030; (4) reduce the **emissions intensity of GDP by 45%** below 2005 levels by 2030; (5) **net zero by 2070**.
>
> The term is borrowed from ritual — the five-ingredient offering of milk, curd, ghee, honey and sugar used in Hindu worship. Using it for a climate pledge was a framing decision: it makes the commitments sound like an offering rather than a concession, which is precisely how India has always argued its climate position — as **voluntary action by a country with among the lowest per-capita emissions in the world**, not as compliance with pressure.
>
> And the underlying number that makes India's position defensible: **India's per-capita CO₂ emissions are roughly a third of the world average and around an eighth of the United States'**, while it is the **third-largest emitter in absolute terms**. Both facts are true, and which one you lead with is the entire climate negotiation. **Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC)**, from the 1992 UNFCCC, is the principle that holds them together — and it is the phrase to deploy.

> 🏛️ **Bodies involved**
>
> **MNRE — Ministry of New and Renewable Energy** · created **1992** as the Ministry of Non-Conventional Energy Sources · **The distinguisher:** **India is the only country in the world with a dedicated ministry for renewable energy.**
> · **Its family:** **SECI** — Solar Energy Corporation of India, a **CPSU** and the main renewable tendering agency · **IREDA** — an **NBFC** (listed, Navratna) that lends to renewable projects · **NISE** (solar institute) · **NIWE** (wind institute).
> · **The regulators, kept straight:** **CERC** and **SERCs** — **statutory**, under the **Electricity Act, 2003**, set tariffs · **CEA — Central Electricity Authority**, statutory, technical planning · **BEE — Bureau of Energy Efficiency**, statutory under the **Energy Conservation Act, 2001** (star labelling, PAT scheme, and now the carbon market) · **ISA — International Solar Alliance**, a **treaty-based international organisation** co-founded by India and France in **2015**, HQ at **Gurugram** — the **first international organisation headquartered in India**.

> **Why it's asked.** The number set is the question: **300.50 GW, 54% of installed capacity, solar 164.59 GW > wind 58.14 GW > hydro 57.24 GW > bio 11.75 GW > nuclear 8.78 GW**, target **500 GW by 2030**. Then the **Panchamrit five** in order, from **COP26 Glasgow 2021**, with **net zero by 2070**. Then the institutional facts that get swapped: **ISA is headquartered at Gurugram and was co-founded with France in 2015** — the first IO based in India; **BEE is under the Energy Conservation Act 2001**; **CERC under the Electricity Act 2003**. And the conceptual trap worth rehearsing until it is automatic: **installed capacity ≠ generation, because of capacity utilisation factors.**

## 4. 💸 Unconditional cash transfers to women — 18 states, and the fiscal wall

### Why in news

**Delhi launched the Lakshmi Yojana** in **August 2026** — **₹2,500 per month** to eligible women — bringing to **18** the number of **States and UTs** running women-targeted **Unconditional Cash Transfer (UCT)** schemes since 2016, with sharp acceleration from **2023** onward.

### The summary

In three years, direct cash to women has gone from an outlier policy to something close to a **national norm adopted state by state**, driven by electoral competition rather than by any central framework. The evidence that cash transfers work is genuinely strong. The evidence that **states can afford them at this scale is not**, and two states have already had to cut beneficiary numbers.

### 📊 The picture

| | |
|---|---|
| **States/UTs with women-focused UCTs** | **18**, since 2016 — most launched **2023 onwards** |
| **Delhi's Lakshmi Yojana** | **₹2,500/month**, launched **August 2026** |
| **Pre-committed share of state expenditure** | ~**44%** already locked into **salaries, pensions and interest payments** |
| **States that have cut beneficiary numbers** | **Maharashtra** and **Madhya Pradesh**, citing fiscal stress |
| **Evidence base cited** | **SEWA Madhya Pradesh unconditional basic income pilot, 2011–13** — improved nutrition, school attendance, and productive asset-buying |
| **Constitutional basis** | **Article 282** (discretionary grants for any public purpose) and **Article 275** (statutory grants-in-aid) |

**The named schemes worth being able to list** ⚠️: Madhya Pradesh's **Ladli Behna**, Maharashtra's **Ladki Bahin**, West Bengal's **Lakshmir Bhandar**, Karnataka's **Gruha Lakshmi**, Tamil Nadu's **Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thogai**, Telangana's and Jharkhand's variants, and now Delhi's **Lakshmi Yojana**.

### The case in favour — and it is a real case

1. **Cash to women is spent differently.** The consistent finding across the development literature is that **income controlled by women goes disproportionately to food, health and children's schooling**. The SEWA MP pilot found exactly this.
2. **It is unconditional, so it has almost no leakage into administration.** No inspectors, no eligibility conditions to monitor, no supply chain — **DBT into a bank account**. Compare with the administrative cost of in-kind delivery.
3. **It partially compensates unpaid care work.** Indian women do roughly **three times the unpaid care work men do — 299 minutes a day against 97** *(NITI Aayog's "Reimagining Care", in the [7 August brief](2026-08-07.md))*. A transfer is a crude but real recognition of labour the statistical system does not count.
4. **It reaches women outside the workforce.** India's **female labour force participation** has risen but remains low; employment-linked benefits by definition miss most women.

### The core concerns

1. **The fiscal arithmetic is unforgiving.** With **~44% of state spending pre-committed** before a single new scheme, a large recurring transfer competes directly with **capital expenditure** — roads, schools, hospitals, irrigation. **A transfer is consumption; capex is future income.** States cutting capex to fund transfers are trading tomorrow's growth for today's, and this is the sharpest version of the argument.
2. **It is politically irreversible.** No government can withdraw a monthly payment to a large female electorate. That makes it a **permanent liability created by a temporary majority** — the same critique made of farm loan waivers and free power.
3. **Cash cannot buy what does not exist.** Money is only useful where there is a **functioning school, a staffed PHC, a market**. In the districts with the worst outcomes, the supply side is missing, and cash without supply is inflationary rather than developmental.
4. **The competitive dynamic drives escalation.** Once one state offers ₹1,500, the next must offer ₹2,000. The amounts have risen far faster than state revenues.
5. **Targeting is weak.** "Women above 18 below an income threshold" is close to universal in practice, so a large share flows to households that do not need it — which is defensible as a design choice for administrative simplicity, but must be defended, not assumed.

### What needs to happen

- **Fiscal rules that protect capital expenditure** — a floor on capex, not just a ceiling on deficit, under the **FRBM** framework
- **Sunset and review clauses** built into scheme design at launch, when they are still politically possible
- **Pair transfers with supply-side investment** in the districts where services are missing — cash and clinics, not cash instead of clinics
- **A Finance Commission conversation about it**, since these are state schemes with national fiscal consequences

> 📖 **The story behind it.** The intellectual ancestor is the **basic income** argument, and India's most important contribution to it happened in **Madhya Pradesh between 2011 and 2013**. **SEWA** (the Self-Employed Women's Association, founded by **Ela Bhatt in 1972**) ran, with UNICEF, an **unconditional basic income pilot in eight villages**: every man, woman and child received a small monthly cash payment, with no conditions and no targeting.
>
> The results are what everyone cites, because they contradicted the standard objection. Recipients did **not** spend it on alcohol. Nutrition improved, school attendance and performance rose, and — the finding that surprised economists most — **households bought productive assets and increased work**, rather than reducing it. Cash acted as **capital**, not as consumption.
>
> The **Economic Survey 2016-17** then devoted a full chapter to **Universal Basic Income**, arguing it was "conceptually appealing" and possibly fiscally feasible if it replaced existing subsidies. **That last clause is the one everybody forgot.** The Survey's UBI was a *substitute* for the existing subsidy architecture. What has actually been built since 2023 is a set of transfers layered **on top of** every existing subsidy, funded by state governments with far less fiscal room than the Centre. **The idea was tested as a replacement and adopted as an addition** — and that, in one sentence, is the whole critique.
>
> Note also the older ancestor with the same defect: **MGNREGA (2005) is a work guarantee, not a cash transfer** — the labour requirement is the self-targeting mechanism that keeps out those who do not need it. UCTs deliberately drop it, which buys simplicity and loses targeting.

> **Why it's asked.** For Mains this is an **argument**, not a fact set — but keep the anchors: **Article 282 is the constitutional basis for discretionary grants**, the **SEWA MP pilot (2011–13)** is the evidence base, the **Economic Survey 2016-17 UBI chapter** is the policy origin, and **~44% pre-committed state expenditure** is the fiscal constraint to quote. For Prelims, the reliable item is the **scheme-to-state mapping**: **Ladli Behna → MP · Ladki Bahin → Maharashtra · Lakshmir Bhandar → West Bengal · Gruha Lakshmi → Karnataka**. Also know **DBT** rests on the **JAM trinity** — Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile — and that **Aadhaar-based transfers were upheld in *Puttaswamy/Aadhaar* (2018) precisely for subsidy delivery under Article 110-linked reasoning.**

## 5. 📲 MDR and the Payment and Settlement Systems Act — who pays for "free" UPI

### Why in news

The **Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** proposes to modify **Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems (PSS) Act, 2007**, creating a statutory framework under which a **Merchant Discount Rate (MDR)** could be levied. The government simultaneously clarified that **UPI will remain free for consumers and small merchants**.

### The summary

**MDR is the fee a merchant pays to accept a digital payment**, shared among the acquiring bank, the issuing bank and the network. In **2020, India set MDR to zero for UPI and RuPay debit cards** through the **Finance Act 2019's insertion of Section 10A** into the PSS Act — making digital acceptance free for merchants, which is a large part of why UPI volumes exploded. The unresolved consequence is that **somebody still has to pay for the infrastructure**, and for six years that somebody has been the banks, absorbing the cost, partly offset by government incentive payments. This amendment is the first move toward putting the question on a statutory footing.

### What the amendment does

| | |
|---|---|
| **Vehicle** | **Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026** |
| **Provision touched** | **Section 10A**, PSS Act 2007 — the zero-MDR mandate |
| **What it enables** | A statutory framework allowing a **nominal, threshold-based MDR** to be determined **if necessary** |
| **Who determines it** | The **UPI and Services Steering Committee**, headed by **NPCI** ⚠️ |
| **P2P transactions** | **100% free** — unchanged |
| **Consumer-to-merchant payments** | **Free, zero charges to the consumer** |
| **Where MDR could apply** | **Only specified large-merchant transactions above high thresholds** |

### 🏛️ The PSS Act, 2007 — the statute itself

| | |
|---|---|
| **Enacted** | **December 2007**; in force **12 August 2008** |
| **Regulator** | **RBI**, now working with the **Payments Regulatory Board (PRB)** |
| **Core rule** | **No person may operate a payment system without RBI authorisation** |
| **RBI's powers** | Set technical standards, audit, inspect, issue directions, resolve disputes |
| **Section 25** | **Dishonour of an electronic funds transfer is a criminal offence** — deliberate parity with cheque bouncing under **Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881** |
| **Settlement finality** | Once determined, a settlement is **irrevocable** — the legal basis on which the whole system rests |
| **Netting** | Given **legal recognition**, which it did not have before 2007 |

**The PRB point is worth carrying:** the **Payments Regulatory Board** replaced the earlier Board for Regulation and Supervision of Payment and Settlement Systems, and it is **chaired by the RBI Governor**. The long-running policy argument was whether payments should be regulated by an **independent** board outside the RBI — the government proposed this and the RBI resisted. **The RBI won; the PRB sits inside the RBI.** That is a live "regulatory turf" example for a GS-II answer.

### 📊 Why the money question is not trivial

| | |
|---|---|
| **UPI operator** | **NPCI** — a **Section 8 (not-for-profit) company** under the Companies Act, promoted by the RBI and IBA in **2008** |
| **Zero-MDR since** | **1 January 2020**, via **Section 10A** inserted by the **Finance Act 2019** |
| **Applies to** | **UPI and RuPay debit cards** — **not** to credit cards or Visa/Mastercard debit, which still carry MDR |
| **Government response** | Annual **incentive schemes** compensating banks for low-value UPI transactions |
| **International arm** | **NIPL** — NPCI International Payments Ltd, which takes UPI abroad (UAE, Singapore, Nepal, France, Sri Lanka, Mauritius) |

### The core concerns

1. **Zero MDR removed the business model from the plumbing.** Banks and payment service providers bear real costs — fraud management, servers, dispute resolution — with no revenue from the largest payment rail in the country. That suppresses investment in exactly the resilience the system needs.
2. **But MDR on small merchants would be regressive and would kill acceptance.** The kirana store accepting a ₹40 payment cannot pay a percentage fee. The **threshold-based design** — MDR only on large merchants above high thresholds — is the attempt to square this, and it is a genuinely sensible piece of policy design.
3. **A subsidy by another name.** Government incentive payments to banks are a fiscal cost for a service the public experiences as free. **Making the cost visible is the honest thing to do; it is also politically difficult, which is why the amendment is framed as enabling rather than mandating.**
4. **Concentration risk.** UPI volume is overwhelmingly concentrated in **two private apps**; NPCI's **30% market-share cap has been repeatedly deferred**. A payments system this systemically important with this much concentration is a financial stability question.
5. **NPCI's status is anomalous.** A **not-for-profit company** operates critical national financial infrastructure. It is regulated by the RBI but is not a public authority in the ordinary sense — a genuine governance question about who owns a public utility built as a private entity.

> 📖 **The story behind it.** **"Merchant Discount Rate" is a misleading name that survives from the 1950s.** When **Diners Club** and then **BankAmericard** began, the bank did not charge the merchant a fee — it **bought the merchant's receivable at a discount**, paying, say, ₹97 for a ₹100 slip. The merchant "discounted" the sale to get paid immediately and to avoid the credit risk. The mechanism became a percentage fee, but the name stuck: **it is a "discount rate", not a "fee", because it began life as an actual discount.**
>
> India's decision to set it to zero in 2020 was **unusual by world standards, and deliberate.** In most countries, interchange fees are *capped* by the regulator — the **EU capped them at 0.2–0.3% in 2015** — but not eliminated. India eliminated them for UPI and RuPay, effectively treating retail digital payments as **public infrastructure rather than a commercial service** — the same category as roads. The result is the reason UPI processes more transactions than any comparable system on earth: **acceptance had no cost, so acceptance became universal.**
>
> **The unresolved question is what happens when public infrastructure is operated by entities that must fund it commercially.** Roads are paid for by taxes. UPI's rails are paid for by banks, partly reimbursed by the Budget, on an annual decision. **This amendment is the first attempt in six years to put that arrangement on a permanent statutory footing** — which is why a small clause in a taxation bill is a bigger story than it looks.

> 🏛️ **Bodies involved**
>
> **NPCI — National Payments Corporation of India** · **Type + instrument:** **Section 8 (not-for-profit) company**, Companies Act; established **2008** under the **PSS Act 2007** framework, promoted by the **RBI and the Indian Banks' Association** · **The distinguisher:** it is an **umbrella operator, not a regulator** — the RBI regulates it. It runs **UPI, RuPay, IMPS, NACH, AePS, BBPS, FASTag (NETC), NFS (ATMs)**. · **Siblings:** **NIPL** (international arm) · **NPCI Bharat BillPay Ltd** · **RBI** (regulator) · **IFSCA** (regulator inside GIFT City).
>
> **Payments Regulatory Board (PRB)** · **Type:** **Statutory** — under the PSS Act as amended · **Chaired by:** the **RBI Governor** · **The distinguisher:** the outcome of a real turf fight — payments regulation stayed **inside** the RBI rather than moving to an independent board.

> **Why it's asked.** The high-value facts: **PSS Act 2007, in force 12 August 2008, RBI is the regulator, Section 25 makes electronic funds transfer dishonour criminal on a par with cheque bouncing under NI Act Section 138**. Then: **Section 10A, inserted by the Finance Act 2019, made MDR zero for UPI and RuPay debit from 1 January 2020 — and note that credit cards were never covered.** And the institutional trap: **NPCI is a Section 8 not-for-profit company, not a statutory body and not a regulator** — it is arguably the most-tested classification error in the whole digital-payments block. See [00_INSTITUTIONS](../../00_INSTITUTIONS.md) §6.

### 9. 🧬 **Paramutation — non-Mendelian inheritance in mammals**

New work identified **paramutation** in mammals — an **epigenetic** phenomenon in which one allele **heritably alters the expression of its partner allele**, and the change is passed to subsequent generations **without any change in the DNA sequence**.

**Why it matters conceptually:** classical **Mendelian inheritance** says alleles segregate unchanged. Paramutation breaks that — an allele can be "silenced" by its partner and stay silenced in the offspring. It was first described in **maize**; finding it in mammals extends **epigenetic inheritance** (the same family as **DNA methylation and histone modification**) into animals.

### 10. 🦠 **AI-designed bacteriophages against resistant bacteria**

Researchers used **AI to design bacteriophages** — viruses that infect and kill bacteria — as a route around **antimicrobial resistance (AMR)**.

**Phage therapy** predates antibiotics (**Félix d'Hérelle, 1917**) and was largely abandoned in the West when penicillin arrived, but was sustained in **Georgia (the Eliava Institute, Tbilisi)**. Its advantage is **specificity** — a phage kills one bacterial strain and leaves the microbiome intact; its problem is that specificity makes it hard to industrialise. **AI design attacks exactly that bottleneck.**
**The AMR frame:** AMR is projected to be among the leading causes of death globally by 2050; India has a **National Action Plan on AMR** and the **Red Line campaign** on antibiotic packaging.

### 11. 🌏 **Taiwan Strait — China's traffic-control claims**

China asserted expanded traffic-control claims over the **Taiwan Strait**, revisiting the **freedom of navigation** question.

**The legal core, which is what gets asked:** the Strait is about **130 km at its narrowest**, so **a corridor of high seas / EEZ lies between the two 12-nautical-mile territorial seas**. Under **UNCLOS (1982)**, **freedom of navigation applies in the EEZ** — China's position that the Strait is not "international waters" is not accepted by most states. **India's stated position supports freedom of navigation and overflight and unimpeded lawful commerce** under UNCLOS. Note **China is a party to UNCLOS; the United States is not** — a persistent irony worth knowing. **Roughly half of the world's container fleet transits the Strait annually** ⚠️.

## 📝 Issue of the Week — *Week 2 writing drill*

> ⏱️ **12 minutes, by hand, 150 words. Timer first, model answer after — not the other way round.**

**Question:** *"India froze the link between population and parliamentary representation in 1976 to make population control politically possible. Fifty years on, unfreezing it is politically impossible. Examine the dilemma and suggest a way forward."* **(150 words, 10 marks)**

**Facts you may use:** LS strength **543**, ceiling **550** (Art 81); freeze via **42nd Amendment 1976** at 1971 levels, extended by the **84th (2001)** to "the first census after 2026"; **87th (2003)** allowed boundary redraw on 2001 data; **Kerala MP ~14 lakh electors vs UP/Bihar ~19 lakh**; projected strength ~**850**; **131st Amendment Bill negatived April 2026**; **106th Amendment (2023)** ties women's reservation to census + delimitation; **India's Rajya Sabha allocates seats by population, unlike the US Senate**.

<details>
<summary><b>Model answer — open only after your 12 minutes are up</b></summary>

**The dilemma.** Delimitation forces a choice between two principles both of which the Constitution honours. **"One person, one vote, one value"** demands that a Bihar voter count as much as a Kerala voter — currently a UP or Bihar MP represents ~19 lakh electors against Kerala's ~14 lakh. **Federal fairness** demands that states which achieved replacement-level fertility, in line with national policy, not lose parliamentary weight for having complied. The 1976 freeze (42nd Amendment), extended by the 84th (2001) to "the first census after 2026", bought fifty years without resolving the conflict.

**Why unfreezing is hard.** Southern and western states contribute disproportionately to GDP and tax revenue; combined with population-weighted Finance Commission devolution, they perceive a double penalty. The Rajya Sabha offers no relief, since India — unlike the US Senate — allocates upper-house seats by population too. The **131st Amendment Bill was negatived in April 2026**, and the **106th Amendment (2023)** has now welded women's reservation to the same exercise.

**The way forward.** Expand the House while **capping any state's proportional loss**, so no state's relative weight falls even as absolute numbers rise. **Decouple women's reservation** so one constitutional promise is not hostage to another. Make the redraw auditable through **published GIS criteria**, given that Delimitation Commission orders are non-justiciable. And address the fiscal grievance **through the Finance Commission's terms of reference**, so delimitation is not made to carry the entire weight of federal resentment.

**Conclusion.** The freeze was a loan against the future; the future has arrived. The settlement must be negotiated **before** the Census output makes every option zero-sum.

</details>
