# 🌍 GEOGRAPHY & ENVIRONMENT — START HERE

**Two subjects on paper. One subject in reality. We study them merged.**

> 🔑 **The one-line summary:** together these are **~25–35 Prelims questions — a quarter of the paper**, the single biggest cluster in Prelims, bigger than Polity or History. Plus **~90 of GS-I's 250** and a solid block of GS-III. They are won on **physical geography + the atlas**, not on the number of books finished.

Created 8 August 2026. Merged into one subject **9 August 2026** at your call — *"we are preparing Geography and Environment together, I do not classify things."*

> ### 🔗 Why the merge is correct, not just convenient
>
> The syllabus divides them; nature doesn't. A **monsoon** is geography when you ask what drives it and environment when you ask what's disrupting it. **Soil** is geography as a landform product and environment as a degrading resource. **Coral reefs, mangroves, glaciers, cyclones, biomes, the water cycle** — every one sits on the line.
>
> The evidence that the line is fake is already in this document: two professional analysts, both reputable, [disagreed by a factor of two](#prelims-geography-questions-per-year) on how many 2022 Prelims questions were "Geography" — because there is no official tagging and the boundary cases are arbitrary. **If the experts can't split them, splitting them would only mean studying monsoons twice and mangroves nowhere.**
>
> The old plan had Environment as separate subject #5, months later. That would have meant re-opening every climate, soil and biome topic from cold. Merged, each topic is opened **once, completely** — the physical process and its degradation learned in the same sitting, because that is how they'll be asked.

⚠️ **Do not treat any single one of these tables as fact — including mine.** The 2022 row disagrees by a factor of two. That is not sloppiness on anyone's part; it's because **there is no official subject tagging.** UPSC publishes a question paper, not a syllabus label per question. Every one of these tables is a coaching institute's private judgement call about which bucket a question falls in.

The disagreement clusters exactly where you'd predict:

- A question on **mangrove degradation** — Geography or Environment?
- A question on **cropping patterns in the Deccan** — Geography or Economy?
- A question on **cyclone naming conventions** — Geography or Current Affairs?

**The defensible conclusion, which is all you need:**

> **Geography is ~10–20 questions a year, ~15 on average, and it has never been fewer than 8.** Combined with Environment — which you cannot cleanly separate from it — the pair is reliably **25–35 questions, i.e. a quarter of the paper.**

Plan to that. Don't plan to a spuriously precise number.

### Mains: the PYQ pool is the honest measure

Vajiram's Geography PYQ compilation for GS-I lists **96 questions across 2013–2025**. That's **13 papers → ~7.4 Geography questions per paper.**

GS-I is 20 questions for 250 marks (10 × 10-markers + 10 × 15-markers). So ~7.4 questions lands at roughly **85–100 marks**, which is exactly what the paper-analysis sources independently claim. Two different methods agreeing is a good sign.

👉 **Working number: Geography is ~90/250 of GS-I. Call it 36%.**

## 📖 The syllabus — all of it, verbatim

### Prelims (GS Paper I)

> *"**Indian and World Geography** — Physical, Social, Economic Geography of India and the World."*

Fourteen words. That's the entire official Prelims geography syllabus, and it is why PYQs matter more here than in any other subject — the syllabus tells you nothing, so **the question papers are the syllabus.**

### Mains GS Paper I — the three geography lines

> 1. *"Salient features of **world's physical geography**."*
> 2. *"**Distribution of key natural resources** across the world (including South Asia and the Indian sub-continent); factors responsible for the **location of primary, secondary, and tertiary sector industries** in various parts of the world (including India)."*
> 3. *"Important **Geophysical phenomena** such as earthquakes, Tsunami, Volcanic activity, cyclone etc., geographical features and their location — **changes in critical geographical features** (including water-bodies and ice-caps) and in flora and fauna and the effects of such changes."*

Read line 3 again. **"Changes in… and the effects of such changes"** is UPSC's licence to ask a current-affairs question wearing a geography costume. Glacier retreat, shifting monsoons, Sundarbans salinity, a new volcanic eruption — all of it enters through that clause. This is the line that makes Mains geography *analytical* rather than factual.

### Mains GS Paper III — the geography-adjacent lines

> - *"**Disaster and disaster management**."* ← entirely geography's job
> - *"Major crops, cropping patterns, irrigation systems, storage, transport and marketing of agricultural produce."*
> - *"Conservation, environmental pollution and degradation, environmental impact assessment."*
> - *"Infrastructure: energy, ports, roads, airports, railways."*

**Why E1 sits in the middle rather than at the end:** ecosystems, biomes and food chains are *downstream of climate*. Doing the ecology core straight after Stages 2–3 means you meet "tropical rainforest biome" while the climate logic that produces it is still fresh — one continuous explanation instead of two disconnected ones months apart. E2 comes later because Shankar IAS is largely factual (Acts, conventions, protected areas) and factual material should sit closer to the exam.

### Why Stage 2 and 3 are interleaved, not sequential

Class 11 *Fundamentals* and Leong cover **the same syllabus at two different depths**. Reading one cover-to-cover then the other means relearning the same chain twice with a month in between. Instead, go topic by topic:

> Read *Fundamentals* Ch. 9 (Atmospheric Circulation) → immediately read Leong's chapters on pressure belts and winds → make one merged note → move on.

One pass, two sources, one note. This alone saves ~10 hours.

## 💡 My advice — the seven things that actually decide this subject

You asked for this part specifically, so I'm going to be direct rather than diplomatic.

### 1. Physical geography is a dependency graph, not a syllabus. Build it as one.

This is the single most important thing in this document, and it is the reason **Geography should be easier for you than for most candidates.**

Most people memorise these as six separate chapters. They are one causal chain:

```
Earth's rotation
      ↓
Coriolis effect
      ↓
Pressure belts (equatorial low, subtropical high, polar high)
      ↓
Planetary winds (trades, westerlies, polars)
      ↓
Ocean currents ────────┐
      ↓                │
Climate types          │
(Köppen: Af, BWh, Cs…) │
      ↓                │
Natural vegetation ←───┘
      ↓
Soil types
      ↓
Agriculture & population distribution
```

Every node is *derivable* from its parent. Once the graph is in your head, you can **reconstruct** why the Atacama is a desert, why Western Europe is mild at 55°N, why the Sahel is where it is — instead of remembering three hundred disconnected facts.

You already do this for a living. A system where downstream state is derived from upstream state, and where you debug by walking back up the chain, is a **call graph**. Treat it like one. Your notes for Stages 2–3 should be this diagram, expanded — not a chapter summary.

Rote learners hate physical geography. People who think in systems find it the easiest 30 marks in Prelims. **You're in the second group. Lean into it.**

### 2. The atlas is a habit, not a book. 15 minutes, every single day.

**This is the highest return-per-minute activity in the entire subject**, and it's the one everybody skips because it doesn't feel like studying.

You cannot cram maps. Nobody has ever successfully sat down for a six-hour map session in April and retained it. But 15 minutes a day for 40 weeks is **70 hours of spaced repetition** on exactly the content UPSC loves asking about — and it costs you nothing, because it fits in the gaps.

Concretely, each day pick one and trace it:
- One river system, source → mouth, with its tributaries and the states it crosses
- One mountain range, with its passes
- One country's neighbours (and know every neighbour of India cold, including maritime)
- Whatever place was in this week's [current affairs](../Current%20Affairs/00_START_HERE.md) — a strait, a conflict zone, a summit venue, a newly-declared biosphere reserve

That last one is the trick: it **welds your current affairs block to your geography block** and makes both stick. You're already writing CA daily. Add the map lookup to that same 45-minute slot and Stage 7 costs you nothing extra.

### 3. ✅ The merge is done — now actually study them merged.

This was originally advice. You've made it the plan, so here's how to execute it rather than just declare it.

**One topic, one sitting, both halves.** When you open a topic, close it from both sides before moving on:

| Topic | The geography half | The environment half |
|---|---|---|
| Monsoon | ITCZ shift, jet streams, why it breaks | Erratic rainfall, crop failure, climate-change signal |
| Soil | How each type forms, where it's found | Erosion, salinity, desertification, soil health cards |
| Rivers | Course, tributaries, basin | Pollution, Namami Gange, interlinking debate |
| Coasts | Landforms, deltas, tides | Mangroves, coral bleaching, sea-level rise, CRZ |
| Forests/vegetation | Types, distribution, why there | Deforestation, ISFR data, biodiversity hotspots, FRA |
| Atmosphere | Layers, circulation, pressure | Ozone hole, greenhouse gases, air-quality index |

**Never write two notes for one phenomenon.** One note, both halves, tagged `[P]`/`[M]` (see #4). If you catch yourself writing "mangroves" in two files, you've re-created the split you just abolished.

**Where the merge pays off hardest:** the ~15 Environment Prelims questions are mostly *factual* — protected-area locations, convention names, species status, Act provisions. They're cheap marks, and they attach naturally to geography topics you're covering anyway. Learning "Sundarbans" once — as a delta *and* a mangrove biosphere reserve *and* a tiger habitat *and* a Ramsar site — costs barely more than learning it as a delta alone.

### 4. Write every note twice-tagged: `[P]` and `[M]`.

Geography has the widest Prelims/Mains divergence of any subject, and this is where most people's notes fail — they make Prelims notes, then rediscover in January that GS-I needs something completely different.

| | Prelims wants | Mains wants |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclones** | Naming conventions, Coriolis threshold, which basin, Saffir–Simpson categories | Why the east coast is hit harder than the west; what NDMA's preparedness gaps are; how Odisha's response model worked |
| **Soils** | Which soil type, which state, which crop | Why alluvial soil productivity is falling; the fertiliser-subsidy link |
| **Industry location** | Where the iron & steel plants are | *Why there* — Weber's least-cost logic, and why it's shifted since 1991 |

Tag as you write. One line at the top of each note:
```
[P] facts, map pins, one-liners
[M] causes, consequences, current linkage, a usable example
```
It costs ten seconds per note and saves rewriting everything in month six.

### 5. Do the PYQs *early* — after Stage 3, not at the end.

The Prelims syllabus is **fourteen words.** It tells you nothing. The question papers are the only real syllabus, so reading them late means you studied blind for two months.

Do a **PYQ skim in Stage 0** (2 hrs, just read questions, don't solve — you're calibrating depth), then solve properly after Stage 3. You will discover things like: UPSC asks far more about **ocean currents and atmospheric circulation** than about the state-wise mineral tables everyone memorises. That changes where your remaining hours go.

Your Polity folder already has 147 Prelims + 299 Mains PYQs organised. Same treatment here — target **~150 Prelims (2011→2026)** and the **96 Mains (2013→2025)**.

### 6. Don't buy Majid Husain. Seriously.

Repeating the Tier-2 warning because it is the most expensive mistake available in this subject. Those books are calibrated for a 500-mark optional. You're studying for a ~90-mark GS block. **Leong + NCERT is complete for GS.** The 100 hours you'd burn there is Economy's entire NCERT foundation.

The only thing that changes this: deciding to take Geography as your optional. Which brings up —

### 7. Geography will never be your optional — so cap it hard at GS depth. And go build the Pub Ad folder.

**Your optional is already decided: Public Administration** (Paper I — Administrative Theory, Paper II — Indian Administration). That settles two things.

**First, it makes the Tier-2 warning absolute rather than conditional.** Majid Husain, Savindra Singh and Khullar are now *permanently* off your list. There is no future scenario where you need them. Geography stops at ~90 GS marks for you, forever — so stop reading the moment NCERT + Leong is done, and don't let a good chapter tempt you deeper. **Depth beyond GS level in Geography is now pure waste.**

**Second — and this is the bigger point — Public Administration has no folder in `Mission/` at all.**

§4 of [00_TARGET_Topper_Marks.md](../00_TARGET_Topper_Marks.md) is blunt about why that matters: the two optional papers give an AIR-1 **311 of their 902 written marks**, at **61–64% efficiency** — higher than *any* GS paper. It's 500 marks, it's the single biggest lever in the exam, and right now it has less structure than Ethics does.

The good news is that Pub Ad is unusually well-placed in your setup:

| Overlaps with | How much |
|---|---|
| **GS-II** (Polity, Governance) | 🟢 Heavy — and Polity is your most-developed folder already |
| **GS-IV** (Ethics) | 🟢 Heavy — your [Ethics doc](../Ethics/00_START_HERE.md) notes syllabus topics 6 & 7 *are* public administration: RTI, Lokpal, Citizen's Charter, 2nd ARC |
| **Essay** | 🟡 Governance/reform essays draw straight from it |
| **Interview** | 🟢 It's literally the job |
| **Geography** | 🔴 **Almost none.** No synergy to exploit here |

That last row is why this note belongs in the Geography doc: **there's no cross-subject shortcut between these two.** Geography earns its ~150 compulsory marks on its own and then stops.

👉 **Recommendation: after this Geography block, scaffold `Public Administration/` before starting Economy.** It's 500 marks with zero infrastructure, and its two strongest overlaps (Polity, Ethics) are subjects you've *already* built — so the marginal cost of starting it now is lower than it will ever be again.
