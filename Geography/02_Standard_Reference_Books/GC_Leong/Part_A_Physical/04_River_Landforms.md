## 1️⃣ The three stages — the framework NCERT assumes

**A river's work changes down its course. Leong organises everything around this.**

| Stage | Dominant process | Landforms |
|---|---|---|
| **YOUTH (upper course)** | **VERTICAL erosion — cutting DOWN** | **V-shaped valley, gorge, canyon, waterfall, rapids, pothole, interlocking spurs** |
| **MATURITY (middle)** | **LATERAL erosion + transport — cutting SIDEWAYS** | **Wider valley, meanders, river cliffs & slip-off slopes, small floodplain** |
| **OLD AGE (lower)** | **DEPOSITION** | **Wide floodplain, large meanders, ox-bow lakes, levees, braiding, delta, marshes** |

**W.M. Davis's "CYCLE OF EROSION"** — **youth → maturity → old age → PENEPLAIN** (an almost featureless low plain), with **MONADNOCKS** (isolated resistant residual hills) standing above it. ⚠️ *Davis's cycle is now regarded as over-simplified by geomorphologists — real landscapes rarely complete it undisturbed — but UPSC still uses its vocabulary, so know the terms and don't over-commit to the theory.*

## 2️⃣ ⭐ RIVER CAPTURE (stream piracy) — Leong's signature topic, absent from NCERT

**A river with a steeper gradient erodes headward faster than its neighbour, eventually breaks through the divide between them, and DIVERTS the neighbour's headwaters into its own channel.**

```
        BEFORE                          AFTER CAPTURE

    A ──────────→ sea            A ────╮
                                       │ ELBOW OF CAPTURE
    B ──────────→ sea            B ←───╯ (sharp right-angle bend)
    (B erodes headward,                 ↓
     steeper gradient)           B carries A's water to the sea
                                        ↓
                                 A's old lower course is now a
                                 MISFIT / BEHEADED STREAM
                                 in an oversized WIND GAP
```

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Elbow of capture** | **The sharp, near-right-angle bend where the captured river turns into its new course** — *the diagnostic field evidence* |
| **Misfit / beheaded stream** | **The now-underfed river below the capture point — a small stream in a valley far too large for it** |
| **Wind gap** | **The dry, abandoned valley the water used to flow through** |
| **Captor stream** | The one that did the capturing |

> **The Indian examples, and they are asked:**
> - **The Tista is believed to have captured parts of neighbouring Himalayan drainage.**
> - **The Chambal–Banas system in Rajasthan shows capture features.**
> - **The classic global example is the Rhine capturing the upper Moselle/Meuse headwaters.**
>
> 🎯 **Why it matters conceptually: river capture proves drainage patterns are DYNAMIC, not fixed.** **Divides migrate, and a "river system" is a snapshot of an ongoing competition between basins.** *That framing is worth more than the vocabulary.*

## 3️⃣ DRAINAGE PATTERNS — asked in Prelims, thin in NCERT

| Pattern | Shape | Controlled by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DENDRITIC** | **Tree-like, branching** | **Uniform rock, no structural control** | **The Indo-Gangetic plain; the commonest pattern on Earth** |
| **TRELLIS** | **Rectangular, tributaries joining at right angles** | **Alternating hard & soft rock bands** (folded terrain) | **The Singrauli basin; Appalachian rivers** |
| **RADIAL** | **Outward from a central high point, like spokes** | **A dome or a volcanic cone** | **Rivers off the AMARKANTAK plateau — Narmada, Son, Mahanadi all rise there and flow outward** |
| **CENTRIPETAL** | **INWARD to a central basin or lake** | A structural basin | **Loktak Lake, Manipur; the Ladakh plateau** |
| **RECTANGULAR** | Right-angled bends | **Joint and fault patterns in the bedrock** | Vindhya region |
| **BARBED** | **Tributaries join the main stream pointing UPSTREAM** — the "wrong" way | **Evidence of RIVER CAPTURE reversing the main stream's flow** | **The Arun, Nepal** |
| **ANNULAR** | Ring-shaped | An eroded dome with concentric hard/soft rings | — |
| **DERANGED** | Chaotic, with many lakes and marshes | **Recently glaciated ground** — no time to organise | **The Canadian Shield** |

> **Barbed drainage is the one to remember, because it's the only pattern that is itself EVIDENCE of a process.** **Tributaries pointing upstream mean the main river once flowed the other way — so a capture reversed it.** *Deploy it alongside §2.*

## 4️⃣ DELTA TYPES — the classification NCERT skips

**A DELTA forms where a river's sediment load is deposited faster than the sea can remove it.**

**Conditions required:** **large sediment load · a gentle gradient at the mouth · a shallow, sheltered sea · weak tides and currents · no strong longshore drift.**

| Delta type | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ARCUATE (fan-shaped)** | **Triangular, convex seaward, many distributaries** | **The NILE** *(the original — the Greek letter Δ gave the landform its name)*, **the GANGA–BRAHMAPUTRA (the world's largest), the Godavari, the Krishna, the Cauvery, the Indus, the Niger** |
| **BIRD'S FOOT** | **Long narrow finger-like distributaries projecting into the sea** | **The MISSISSIPPI** — *fine sediment, weak waves, so the levees build far out to sea* |
| **ESTUARINE** | **No delta at all — a funnel-shaped drowned mouth** | **The NARMADA and TAPI** *(rift valleys — see [A2](02_Earth_Movements_and_Vulcanicity.md))*, **the Thames, the Ob, the Amazon** |
| **CUSPATE** | **Tooth/pointed, where waves spread the sediment evenly both ways** | **The Tiber, Italy** |
| **Inland / lacustrine** | Into a lake or inland basin | **The Volga into the Caspian; the Okavango inland delta, Botswana** |

> ⭐ **The question that gets asked: "Why do the east-coast rivers of India form deltas while the west-coast rivers form estuaries?"**
>
> **Give all four reasons:**
> 1. **The east-flowing rivers (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery) have LONG, gentle courses across the Deccan** — time and gradient to gather and drop a large load.
> 2. **The west-flowing Narmada and Tapi flow through RIFT VALLEYS** — steep, narrow, fast troughs that flush sediment straight out.
> 3. **The west coast is a comparatively steep, [submergent coast](08_Coastal_Landforms.md)** with deeper water offshore; the east coast is a gentle [emergent coast](08_Coastal_Landforms.md) with a broad shallow shelf.
> 4. **The Bay of Bengal is shallower and more sheltered than the Arabian Sea.**
>
> 🎯 **This single question ties together [A2 rift valleys](02_Earth_Movements_and_Vulcanicity.md), [A8 coastal emergence](08_Coastal_Landforms.md) and Indian drainage. It is exactly the kind of integrated answer that scores.**

## 5️⃣ ANTECEDENT vs SUPERIMPOSED DRAINAGE — a favourite Prelims pair

| | **ANTECEDENT (precedent)** | **SUPERIMPOSED (epigenetic)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | **The river EXISTED BEFORE the uplift, and cut down as fast as the land rose — so it kept its course** | **The river developed on a younger cover of rock, then cut DOWN INTO a buried older structure beneath, inheriting a course unrelated to that structure** |
| **Evidence** | **A river cutting a deep GORGE straight THROUGH a mountain range instead of going around it** | **A river course that ignores the geology it now flows over** |
| **Indian examples** | **The INDUS, SUTLEJ and BRAHMAPUTRA — all rise NORTH of the Himalaya and cut through it in enormous gorges.** *They are older than the mountains they cross* | **The Damodar, Subarnarekha and Chambal, cutting into the Chota Nagpur/peninsular structure** |

> ⭐ **The antecedent rivers of the Himalaya are one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the mountains' recent, ongoing uplift.**
>
> **Think about what it means: the Brahmaputra rises in Tibet, on the FAR side of the Himalaya, and reaches the Bay of Bengal by cutting a gorge through the range.** **No river could carve a path through a mountain range that already existed. So the river must be OLDER than the mountain — it was there first, and it sawed downward at roughly the rate the land rose beneath it.**
>
> 🎯 **State it that way and you've demonstrated reasoning rather than recall.**

## 6️⃣ REJUVENATION — when a river gets a second youth

**If the land is uplifted (or sea level falls), a river's gradient steepens and it starts cutting DOWN again in a course that had reached maturity or old age.**

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Incised / entrenched meanders** | **Meanders cut deep into rock — proof that a floodplain meander was later forced to erode vertically.** **The Great Bend of the Colorado; the Grand Canyon's meanders** |
| **River terraces** | **Old floodplain remnants left as steps ("paired terraces") above the new, lower channel.** **Common along the Ganga and its Himalayan tributaries** |
| **Knick point / rejuvenation head** | **A break of slope in the long profile, often marked by rapids or a waterfall, migrating upstream** |
| **Raised beaches / wave-cut platforms above sea level** | The coastal equivalent — see [A8](08_Coastal_Landforms.md) |

## 8️⃣ 🌱 ENVIRONMENT OVERLAY

| 🌱 Issue | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sediment starvation below dams** | **Dams trap sediment, so deltas downstream STOP GROWING and begin to SINK and erode.** **The Nile delta since Aswan; the Mississippi delta losing land; the Sundarbans and the Godavari–Krishna deltas facing the same.** *Combine with sea-level rise and you have a delta drowning from two directions at once — a first-rate Mains point* |
| **River interlinking** | **The National River Linking Project — Ken-Betwa as the first.** *Arguments both ways: drought relief and irrigation vs ecological disruption, displacement, cost, and the question of whether "surplus" basins really are* |
| **River pollution** | **Namami Gange; the Yamuna; industrial effluent; sewage as the dominant load** |
| **Floodplain encroachment** | **Building on floodplains converts a natural flood into a disaster.** Chennai, Mumbai, Delhi's Yamuna floodplain |
| **Sand mining** | **Riverbed sand mining lowers channels, undercuts bridges, drops water tables and destroys habitat.** A large, poorly-regulated Indian problem |
| **Ravines / badlands** | **The Chambal ravines — gully erosion on a massive scale; reclamation programmes** |
| **Environmental flows** | The concept that a river needs a minimum flow to stay alive — central to Ganga policy |
