## 1️⃣ India's Biodiversity

**India has ~8% of the world's total recorded species (~1.6 million species) on 2.4% of its land.**

⚠️ **A striking figure:** an estimated **10% of India's wild flora and fauna are on the threatened list**, and many are on the verge of extinction. **Cheetah, pink-headed duck, mountain quail and forest spotted owlet** are among species already lost or presumed lost.

*(Note: cheetahs were declared extinct in India in 1952; African cheetahs were reintroduced in Kuno in 2022 — worth knowing as current affairs, though it does not change the historical extinction fact.)*

### Classification of species — with examples

| Category | Meaning | Indian examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Population levels normal for survival | Cattle, sal, pine, rodents |
| **Endangered** | In danger of extinction; survival unlikely if causal factors continue | **Black buck, crocodile, Indian wild ass, Indian rhino, lion-tailed macaque, sangai (Manipur brow-antlered deer)** |
| **Vulnerable** | Population declined; likely to move to endangered if causes continue | **Blue sheep, Asiatic elephant, Gangetic dolphin** |
| **Rare** | Small populations; may become endangered/vulnerable | **Himalayan brown bear, wild Asiatic buffalo, desert fox, hornbill** |
| **Endemic** | Found only in particular areas, isolated by natural or geographic barriers | **Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, Arunachal mithun** |
| **Extinct** | No longer found after repeated searching of known habitats | **Asiatic cheetah, pink-headed duck** |

> ⚠️ **These are NCERT's categories and they differ slightly from the formal IUCN Red List** (Extinct → Extinct in the Wild → Critically Endangered → Endangered → Vulnerable → Near Threatened → Least Concern). **Know both** — NCERT's version for these examples, the IUCN version for current-affairs questions about a species' status.

## 2️⃣ Causes of Depletion — The Real Argument

### The colonial and developmental causes

| Cause | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Colonial forestry** | Large-scale forest clearance for **railway sleepers, shipbuilding and plantations** (tea, coffee, rubber). Commercial forestry replaced diverse forest with **monoculture** |
| **Agricultural expansion** | **Between 1951 and 1980, over 26,200 sq km of forest was converted to agricultural land** — a standing Prelims figure |
| **Shifting cultivation** | Tribal belts of NE and central India — sustainable historically, damaging when the cycle shortens |
| **Large development projects** | **Since 1951, over 5,000 sq km of forest cleared for river valley projects.** The **Narmada Sagar Project alone submerged 40,000 hectares of forest** |
| **Mining** | **Dolomite mining in the Buxa Tiger Reserve (West Bengal)** severely threatened the habitat and blocked elephant migration |
| **Grazing and fuelwood** | Often blamed, but **NCERT explicitly notes this is not the main cause** |
| **Unequal resource access** | Over-consumption by the affluent causes disproportionate damage |

> **The argument NCERT is making, which is what a Mains answer should carry:** deforestation is frequently blamed on **poor communities, grazing and fuelwood collection.** NCERT states directly that this is **not the primary cause** — the primary causes are **commercial forestry, agricultural expansion, large infrastructure projects and mining.** **The people who live in and depend on forests are not the main agents of their destruction.**
>
> **This reframing matters** because it underpins the Forest Rights Act 2006 and the case for community conservation. **It is also a genuinely contrarian point that few candidates make.**

> 🌱 **A second point worth carrying:** biodiversity loss is **socially skewed.** Deforestation costs poor women most directly — they walk further for fuel and fodder, which lengthens working hours, damages health, and pulls girls out of school to help. **Environmental degradation is a gender and poverty issue, not only an ecological one.** Strong for GS-I (society), GS-II and Essay.

## 3️⃣ Conservation in India

### Why conserve

Preserves ecological diversity and life-support systems — **water, air and soil.** Preserves the **genetic diversity** of plants and animals, essential for breeding, agriculture and future adaptation.

### Legal and programme framework

| Instrument | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972** | The core statute — protected species lists, protected areas, banned hunting and trade |
| **Project Tiger, 1973** | Launched with **9 tiger reserves**; among the world's best-known conservation programmes |
| **Other projects** | **Project Rhino, Project Great Indian Bustard**, projects for the Indian elephant, three-horned crocodile, Asiatic lion, black buck, snow leopard, Kashmir stag (hangul), Nilgiri tahr |
| **Insects added** | **In 1991, plants and insects were also added** for the first time to the protected list |

**Key tiger reserves to know:** **Corbett** (Uttarakhand), **Sunderbans** (WB), **Bandhavgarh** (MP), **Sariska** (Rajasthan), **Manas** (Assam), **Periyar** (Kerala).

### Types and distribution of forests

**A very frequently asked classification:**

| Type | Meaning | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **RESERVED FORESTS** | **More than half of total forest land.** Regarded as **most valuable** for conserving forest and wildlife resources | ~**54%** |
| **PROTECTED FORESTS** | **Almost one-third of total forest area.** Protected from **further depletion** | ~**29%** |
| **UNCLASSED FORESTS** | Other forests and wastelands belonging to **both government and private individuals and communities** | ~**17%** |

**State patterns worth knowing:**
- **Reserved forests are more than 75%** of forest area in **Jammu & Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra.**
- **Protected forests dominate** in **Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan.**
- **All of North-eastern states and parts of Gujarat have a very high percentage of unclassed forests, managed by local communities.**

## 4️⃣ Community and Conservation — The Chapter's Best Material

**This is where Class 10 gives you material most candidates don't have.**

| Movement / practice | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sariska Tiger Reserve** | **Villagers fought against mining** by citing the Wildlife Protection Act |
| **Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri', Alwar (Rajasthan)** | **Five villages declared 1,200 hectares of forest their own protected area**, with their own rules banning hunting and outside encroachment |
| **CHIPKO MOVEMENT** | **Himalayas.** Villagers (notably women) **hugged trees** to prevent felling. Successfully resisted deforestation **and** showed that **community afforestation with indigenous species can succeed** |
| **BEEJ BACHAO ANDOLAN** (Tehri) and **NAVDANYA** | Showed that **diversified crop production without chemical fertilisers is economically viable** — seed conservation movements |
| **JOINT FOREST MANAGEMENT (JFM)** | **Began in Odisha, 1988.** Village communities form institutions to **protect degraded forest** managed by the forest department, in return for **intermediary benefits like non-timber forest produce and a share in timber harvest** |
| **Sacred groves** | Forests protected by religious custom — **Mundas and Santhals of Chhota Nagpur worship mahua and kadamba; tribals of Odisha and Bihar worship tamarind and mango; peepal and banyan are considered sacred**. **Macaques and langurs around temples; blackbuck, chinkara, nilgai and peacocks protected by the Bishnois of Rajasthan** |

> **Chipko is worth knowing properly, not just by name.** It began in the early 1970s in the Garhwal Himalayas (Reni village, 1974 is the celebrated episode), led substantially by **women**, who bore the direct cost of deforestation through longer walks for fuel and fodder. **Its success led to a 1980 ban on green felling above 1,000 m in the Himalayas.** It is simultaneously an environmental movement, a women's movement and a livelihood movement — **which is why it works in GS-I (society), GS-III (environment) and Essay alike.**

> 🎯 **The core argument to extract from this section:**
> **Conservation that excludes local communities tends to fail; conservation that gives them ownership and a stake tends to succeed.** The reason is straightforward — people who depend on a forest have both the strongest incentive to preserve it and the best knowledge of it. **Enforcement from outside is expensive and easily evaded; local ownership is self-enforcing.**
>
> **This is the intellectual basis of the biosphere reserve model, JFM, PESA 1996 and the Forest Rights Act 2006** — and it connects to Elinor Ostrom's Nobel-winning work on managing commons.

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **India has ~8% of world species on 2.4% of land; ~10% of its wild flora and fauna are threatened.**
2. **Categories: normal, endangered, vulnerable, rare, endemic, extinct** — know NCERT's examples **and** the formal IUCN list.
3. **The main causes of depletion are commercial forestry, agricultural expansion, large projects and mining — NOT grazing and fuelwood.** NCERT says this explicitly.
4. **26,200 sq km forest → agriculture (1951–80); 5,000+ sq km cleared for river valley projects; Narmada Sagar alone submerged 40,000 ha.**
5. **Buxa Tiger Reserve — dolomite mining blocked elephant migration.**
6. 🌱 **Deforestation costs poor women most** — longer walks for fuel and fodder, health damage, girls pulled from school.
7. **WPA 1972; Project Tiger 1973 with 9 reserves; plants and insects added 1991.**
8. **Forests: Reserved ~54% (most valuable) · Protected ~29% · Unclassed ~17%.** NE states are largely **unclassed**, community-managed.
9. **Chipko** — Himalayas, women, tree-hugging; led to a ban on green felling above 1,000 m.
10. **JFM began in Odisha in 1988** — communities protect degraded forest for NTFP and a timber share.
11. **Sacred groves and the Bishnois** show community conservation predates state conservation.
12. 🎯 **The chapter's argument: conservation succeeds when local communities own it.** That underpins PESA, FRA 2006 and the biosphere reserve model.
