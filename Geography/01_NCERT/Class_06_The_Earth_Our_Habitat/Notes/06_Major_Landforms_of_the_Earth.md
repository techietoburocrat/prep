# Chapter 6 — Major Landforms of the Earth

> **The one-line point:** The Earth's surface is being built up from **inside** (forces that raise land) and worn down from **outside** (forces that flatten it). The three great landforms — **mountains, plateaus, plains** — are snapshots of that permanent contest, and each one dictates how people live on it.

## 🎬 Start Here: A Permanent Tug-of-War

Every landform on Earth is the current score in a contest between two opposing sets of forces.

```
   ┌────────────────────────────┐      ┌────────────────────────────┐
   │   INTERNAL (ENDOGENIC)     │      │   EXTERNAL (EXOGENIC)      │
   │   forces from INSIDE       │  vs  │   forces from OUTSIDE      │
   ├────────────────────────────┤      ├────────────────────────────┤
   │ • Plate movement           │      │ • Running water (rivers)   │
   │ • Folding, faulting        │      │ • Wind                     │
   │ • Volcanic eruption        │      │ • Glaciers                 │
   │ • Earthquakes              │      │ • Waves                    │
   │                            │      │ • Rain, frost, temperature │
   │  → BUILD UP. Raise land.   │      │  → WEAR DOWN. Level land.  │
   │     Create relief.         │      │     Erode and deposit.     │
   └────────────────────────────┘      └────────────────────────────┘
```

> ⛰️ **The comparison that fits: a sandcastle at the edge of the tide.** You keep piling sand up; the waves keep flattening it. What you see at any moment is not a finished object — it's the **current balance** between building and wearing away.
>
> The Himalayas are still rising by ~5 mm a year (India is still pushing into Asia), and simultaneously being cut down by rivers, ice and landslides. **The Aravallis lost that contest long ago** — once mighty, now worn to stubs. Same forces, different stage.

**This is the key idea of the chapter: landforms are processes, not objects.**

## 1️⃣ ⛰️ Mountains

> **Mountain** = a natural elevation of the Earth's surface, higher than the surrounding land, usually with a **narrow summit** and **steep slopes**.

### Basic facts

- Some are **higher than the clouds**.
- Temperature **falls with altitude** — high peaks are permanently snow-covered.
- Steep slopes → **little land for farming**, thin soil.
- **Sparsely populated** — the harshest of the three landforms to live on.

### The three types of mountain — the most testable content here

| Type | How it forms | Diagram | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FOLD** | Two plates collide; layers of rock **buckle and fold upward** | `∧∧∧` | **Himalayas**, **Alps**, **Andes**, **Rockies** — the world's great young ranges |
| **BLOCK** | The crust **cracks (faults)**; some blocks are pushed up (**horst**), others drop down (**graben / rift valley**) | `▔▔│▁▁│▔▔` | **Vosges** (France), **Black Forest** (Germany), **Satpura** (India), East African Rift |
| **VOLCANIC** | Built up by **lava and ash** accumulating around a vent | `◬` | **Mt. Kilimanjaro** (Africa), **Mt. Fuji** (Japan), **Mt. Etna** |

```
   FOLD MOUNTAIN                 BLOCK MOUNTAIN            VOLCANIC MOUNTAIN
   Plates push together          Crust cracks & shifts     Lava piles up

        ╱▔╲  ╱▔╲                 ▔▔▔│▔▔▔│▔▔▔                    ◬
      ╱     ╲    ╲                  │HORST│                    ╱ ┆ ╲
    →═════════════←              ▔▔▔│     │▔▔▔               ╱  ┆  ╲
     rock layers                    │▁▁▁▁▁│                ╱ lava ┆ ╲
     buckle upward               GRABEN (dropped)         ════vent════
```

> 🍞 **Fold mountains — the analogy that fits:** push two ends of a **tablecloth** on a table towards each other and it rises into ridges and troughs. That is exactly what happens to rock layers when plates collide, just far slower and far harder. **The Himalayas are the tablecloth ridge created when India collided with Asia** — a collision still underway.

> 🧱 **Block mountains — think of a cracked pavement slab** where one section has been pushed up and the neighbouring one has sunk. The raised block is a **horst**, the sunken one a **graben** (rift valley). No folding at all — just vertical movement along fractures.

### Young vs old mountains — a distinction that explains a lot

| | **Young (fold)** | **Old (worn)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Height** | High | Low, rounded |
| **Peaks** | Sharp, jagged | Blunt, eroded |
| **Slopes** | Steep | Gentle |
| **Seismic** | **Active** — earthquakes | Stable |
| **Example** | **Himalayas** (world's **youngest** major range) | **Aravalli** (among the **world's oldest** ranges) |

> 🎯 **The Aravalli–Himalaya contrast is worth carrying.** The Aravallis were once comparable in scale to today's Himalayas. Hundreds of millions of years of erosion have reduced them to low hills. **Every mountain is on that trajectory.**

### Range, system and chain

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Range** | Mountains arranged in a **line** | Himadri, Aravalli |
| **System** | A **series of ranges** of similar age and origin | The Himalayan system (Himadri + Himachal + Shiwalik) |
| **Chain / Cordillera** | A **long belt** of ranges of different types and ages | The Andes–Rockies belt |

### 🌍 World's major mountains

| Range | Where | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Himalayas** | Asia | Highest range. **Mt. Everest, 8,849 m** — highest point on Earth |
| **Andes** | South America | **World's longest mountain range** (~7,000 km) |
| **Alps** | Europe | Classic fold range |
| **Rockies** | North America | |
| **Urals** | Between Europe and Asia | Old, worn; the continental boundary |
| **Atlas** | NW Africa | |
| **Aravalli** | India | Among the **world's oldest** ranges |

### 🌱 Why mountains matter — beyond scenery

| Use | Detail |
|---|---|
| 💧 **Water towers** | **The single most important function.** Snow and glaciers store water in winter and release it in summer, feeding perennial rivers |
| 🌲 **Forests & biodiversity** | Timber, medicinal plants, and habitat across many climate zones stacked vertically |
| ⚡ **Hydroelectric power** | Steep gradients + reliable flow |
| 🏔️ **Tourism** | Hill stations, trekking, pilgrimage |
| 🛡️ **Natural barriers** | Defence, and climatic barriers — the Himalayas block cold Central Asian winds and trap the monsoon |
| 🐄 **Pastures** | Summer grazing on alpine meadows |

> 🌱💧 **"Water towers" is the concept to hold on to.**
>
> The Himalayas feed the **Ganga, Indus and Brahmaputra** — rivers supporting well over a billion people. These rivers flow **all year**, unlike peninsular rivers that shrink in summer, precisely because Himalayan snow and ice release meltwater through the dry months. **The mountains are a natural reservoir with a built-in seasonal release schedule.**
>
> ⚠️ **Which is why glacier retreat is not a distant problem.** As glaciers shrink, flows increase briefly, then fall permanently. That is a water-security question for northern India, Pakistan and Bangladesh simultaneously. **Tag it for GS-III.**

> 🌱 **Why mountain ecosystems are fragile — three specific reasons:**
> 1. **Thin soil on steep slopes** — remove the vegetation and it washes away in one monsoon, and takes centuries to rebuild.
> 2. **Slow growth** — cold, short growing seasons mean recovery from damage takes far longer than in the plains.
> 3. **Slope instability** — deforestation, road-cutting and unplanned construction trigger **landslides**. The Himalayas are young, seismically active and already unstable; human activity compounds it.
>
> This is the background to Joshimath-type subsidence, Uttarakhand's flash floods, and the debate over Himalayan road and dam projects. **Also GS-III disaster management.**

> 🌈 **A striking ecological fact:** climbing a tropical mountain takes you through the same sequence of vegetation zones as travelling from the equator to the poles — tropical forest, then temperate forest, then coniferous, then alpine meadow, then snow. **A single mountainside compresses the planet's climatic zones into a few kilometres of altitude**, which is why mountains hold such concentrated biodiversity. The Western Ghats and Eastern Himalaya are both global **biodiversity hotspots** for exactly this reason.

## 2️⃣ 🏜️ Plateaus

> **Plateau** = an **elevated flat land** — a raised area that is more or less flat on top, standing **above the surrounding land**, usually with at least one **steep side (escarpment)**.

Also called **tablelands** — the name is the definition.

### Key facts

- Vary hugely in height — from a few hundred to several thousand metres.
- May be **young or very old**.
- The **Deccan Plateau** in India is **one of the oldest landforms on Earth**.
- The **Tibetan Plateau** is the **highest in the world** (~4,000–6,000 m) — the *"Roof of the World"*.
- The **East African Plateau** (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda) is rich in **gold and diamonds**.
- The **Western Plateau of Australia** and the **Guiana Highlands** (South America) are other examples.

```
   A PLATEAU — flat on top, steep at the edge

         ▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
        ╱ flat tableland     ╲
       ╱                      ╲  ← escarpment
      │                        │    (steep side)
   ▁▁▁┘                        └▁▁▁
      surrounding lowland
```

### 🌱 Why plateaus matter

| Use | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⛏️ **Minerals** | **The most important point.** Plateaus are **the world's storehouse of minerals** — old, stable, deeply eroded rock exposes ore bodies |
| 🏭 **Industry** | Mineral wealth → mining → heavy industry |
| ⚡ **Waterfalls** | Rivers dropping over escarpments create falls — **Jog Falls** (India), **Victoria Falls** (Africa) |
| 🌾 **Lava soils** | Weathered volcanic rock gives **black soil**, ideal for **cotton** |

> ⛏️ **Why plateaus hold minerals — the reasoning, not just the fact.** Plateaus are typically ancient blocks of hard rock that have been eroded for hundreds of millions of years. That prolonged erosion strips away the softer overburden and **exposes mineral-bearing rock near the surface**, where it can be mined. Young mountains bury their minerals; old plateaus expose them.
>
> India's mineral belt — **Chhotanagpur Plateau** (Jharkhand/Odisha/Chhattisgarh) — is precisely this: coal, iron ore, mica, bauxite. It is why India's steel industry sits there. **A geography fact that explains an economic geography fact.**

## 3️⃣ 🌾 Plains

> **Plain** = a **large stretch of flat or gently rolling land**, generally not more than ~200 m above sea level.

### How they form

Most great plains are built by **rivers and their tributaries**. A river flowing down from mountains carries **sediment** — stones, sand, silt — and **deposits it** as the land flattens and the current slows. Over millennia this builds a deep layer of **alluvium**.

```
   HOW A RIVER BUILDS A PLAIN

   MOUNTAINS          FOOTHILLS              PLAIN
   fast current       slowing                slow current
   ↓ ERODES           ↓ drops boulders       ↓ DEPOSITS fine silt
     ╱╲                                       ▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
   ╱    ╲___                              ▁▁▁▁ deep ALLUVIUM ▁▁▁▁
                                          → the most fertile soil
                                            on Earth
```

### 🌍 Great plains of the world

| Plain | River system |
|---|---|
| **Indo-Gangetic Plain** | Ganga, Indus, Brahmaputra — **among the most densely populated regions on Earth** |
| **Yangtze Plain** | Yangtze (China) |
| **Mississippi Plain** | Mississippi (USA) |
| **Amazon Plain** | Amazon (South America) |

### Why plains support the most people

This causal chain is worth learning as a chain, because it recurs throughout geography:

```
   FLAT LAND
        ↓  easy to plough, easy to irrigate
   DEEP FERTILE ALLUVIAL SOIL
        ↓
   HIGH AGRICULTURAL PRODUCTIVITY → FOOD SURPLUS
        ↓
   ROADS, RAILWAYS AND CANALS ARE CHEAP TO BUILD
        ↓
   TRADE, TOWNS, DENSE SETTLEMENT
        ↓
   THE MOST DENSELY POPULATED LANDFORM ON EARTH
```

> 🏛️ **This is why the great early civilisations were river-plain civilisations** — the Indus, the Nile, Mesopotamia, the Yellow River. It is also why **Uttar Pradesh and Bihar are India's most populous states**, and why the Northern Plains have been the political heartland of the subcontinent for three thousand years. **Physical geography wrote the demographic and political map.** You'll meet this again in Ch.7, and it will echo through your History notes.

### 🌱 The environmental cost of plains

Plains are the most productive and the most **modified** landform:

- **Almost entirely converted** to farmland and cities; original vegetation largely gone.
- **Flooding** — flat land drains slowly; the Ganga and Brahmaputra plains flood annually.
- **Groundwater depletion** — intensive irrigation in Punjab, Haryana and western UP is pumping aquifers faster than they recharge.
- **Soil degradation** — over-fertilisation, salinisation from canal irrigation, loss of organic matter.
- **Air pollution** — the Indo-Gangetic Plain traps pollutants against the Himalayas, producing some of the world's worst winter air quality. **The mountains that bring the rivers also block the escape of smoke.**

> 🌱 That last point is a good one to hold: **Delhi's winter smog is partly a landform problem.** Cold, still air sits in the plain, hemmed in by the Himalayas to the north, with no wind to disperse it. Geography does not cause the pollution, but it decides where it accumulates.

## 4️⃣ Comparing the Three

| | ⛰️ **MOUNTAINS** | 🏜️ **PLATEAUS** | 🌾 **PLAINS** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Height** | High, steep | Elevated, flat-topped | Low (<200 m), flat |
| **Formed by** | Folding, faulting, volcanism | Uplift + long erosion | **River deposition** |
| **Soil** | Thin, poor | Variable; lava soils fertile | **Deep, fertile alluvium** |
| **Population** | **Sparse** | Moderate | **Very dense** |
| **Farming** | Difficult; terraced | Moderate | **Excellent** |
| **Chief value** | 💧 **Water, forests, hydro-power, tourism** | ⛏️ **Minerals** | 🌾 **Agriculture, transport, settlement** |
| **Transport** | Very difficult | Moderate | Easy and cheap |
| **India example** | Himalayas, Western Ghats | Deccan, Chhotanagpur | Indo-Gangetic Plain |
| 🌱 **Chief vulnerability** | Landslides, glacier loss, fragile soils | Mining damage, deforestation | Floods, groundwater loss, pollution |

> 🔑 **The one-line summary of the whole chapter:** **mountains give water, plateaus give minerals, plains give food.** An economy needs all three, which is a large part of why India — possessing all three in abundance — has the resource base it does.

**Trap 1:** *"The Himalayas are the world's longest mountain range."* **No — the Andes.** The Himalayas are the **highest**, not the longest.
**Trap 2:** *"Satpura is a fold mountain."* **No — block mountain.** So are the Vosges and the Black Forest.
**Trap 3:** *"Plateaus are always high."* No — they vary widely. The defining feature is **flat top + steep side**, not absolute height.
**Trap 4:** *"The Aravallis are young."* **Among the oldest ranges in the world.** The Himalayas are the young ones.
**Trap 5:** Confusing **horst** (raised) with **graben** (sunken).

## 🔑 Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Internal / endogenic forces** | Forces from within the Earth that **build up** relief |
| **External / exogenic forces** | Forces from outside (water, wind, ice) that **wear down** relief |
| **Mountain** | Natural elevation with a narrow summit and steep slopes |
| **Fold mountain** | Formed by rock layers buckling when plates collide |
| **Block mountain** | Formed by faulting; blocks raised (**horst**) or dropped (**graben**) |
| **Volcanic mountain** | Built by accumulated lava and ash |
| **Horst** | The **raised** block between two faults |
| **Graben / rift valley** | The **sunken** block between two faults |
| **Range** | Mountains arranged in a line |
| **Mountain system** | A series of ranges of similar age and origin |
| **Chain / cordillera** | A long belt of ranges of mixed types and ages |
| **Plateau / tableland** | Elevated flat land with at least one steep side |
| **Escarpment** | The steep edge of a plateau |
| **Plain** | Large flat or gently rolling land, generally under 200 m |
| **Alluvium** | Fine fertile sediment deposited by rivers |
| **Erosion** | The wearing away and removal of material |
| **Deposition** | The laying down of transported material |
| 🌱 **Water tower** | A mountain range storing water as snow/ice and releasing it seasonally |
| 🌱 **Biodiversity hotspot** | A region of exceptionally high, threatened biodiversity |
| 🌱 **GLOF** | Glacial Lake Outburst Flood |

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Landforms are the running score between internal forces (building up) and external forces (wearing down).**
2. **Three great landforms: mountains, plateaus, plains.**
3. **Three mountain types: fold** (Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies), **block** (Vosges, Black Forest, Satpura — horst and graben), **volcanic** (Kilimanjaro, Fuji).
4. **Andes = longest range. Himalayas = highest. Aravalli = among the oldest.**
5. **Range** (a line) → **system** (series of ranges) → **chain** (long mixed belt).
6. 🌱 **Mountains are water towers** — snow and glaciers give India its perennial rivers. Glacier retreat is therefore a water-security threat.
7. 🌱 **Mountain ecosystems are fragile** — thin soil, slow growth, unstable slopes. Hence landslides.
8. **Plateaus are flat-topped with steep sides.** **Tibetan = highest** ("Roof of the World"); **Deccan = one of the oldest**.
9. ⛏️ **Plateaus are the world's storehouse of minerals**, because long erosion exposes ore-bearing rock. Chhotanagpur is India's example.
10. 🌾 **Plains are built by river deposition of alluvium** — the most fertile soil there is.
11. **Flat land → fertile soil → surplus food → cheap transport → dense settlement.** This chain explains the Indo-Gangetic Plain and every river civilisation in history.
12. 🌱 **Plains are the most modified landform** — floods, groundwater depletion, soil degradation, and the Indo-Gangetic air-quality trap.
13. 🔑 **Mountains give water. Plateaus give minerals. Plains give food.**
