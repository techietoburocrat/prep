# Chapter 4 — Climate 🌱

> **The one-line point:** India's climate is a **monsoon climate** — and the monsoon is far more than a sea breeze. It is driven by **differential heating, the shifting ITCZ, jet streams in the upper atmosphere, and the Tibetan Plateau acting as a giant heat source.** This chapter is where the monsoon stops being a simple story and becomes the real mechanism.

## 1️⃣ The Six Controls of Climate

| Control | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Latitude** | Determines sun angle → temperature. The Tropic of Cancer halves India |
| **Altitude** | Temperature falls ~6.5°C/km. Hills are cooler than plains at the same latitude |
| **Pressure and wind system** | Drives the monsoon itself |
| **Distance from the sea** | **Continentality** — coastal areas have moderate climate; interiors have extremes |
| **Ocean currents** | Affect coastal temperature |
| **Relief features** | Mountains block or force up winds — the **rain shadow** |

> 🌡️ **Continentality is worth a specific example.** **Mumbai and Nagpur are at similar latitudes.** Mumbai's annual temperature range is small (the sea moderates it); **Nagpur swings far more** because it is deep inland. **Water heats and cools slowly; land does both fast** — the same fact that drives the monsoon (Cl.7 Ch.4).

## 2️⃣ The Monsoon Mechanism — The Full Version

Class 6 gave you the basic idea; Class 7 gave the pressure chain. **Class 9 adds four more mechanisms**, and these are what Prelims actually asks about.

### (a) The basic pressure engine

```
   SUMMER                                WINTER
   ──────                                ──────
   Intense heating over NW India         Land cools rapidly
        ↓                                     ↓
   LOW PRESSURE over the               HIGH PRESSURE over
      subcontinent                          Central Asia / N India
        ↓                                     ↓
   High pressure over the                 Low pressure over the ocean
   Indian Ocean                                ↓
        ↓                                 Wind blows LAND → SEA
   Wind blows SEA → LAND                  (dry)
   (moisture-laden)                            ↓
        ↓                                 🍂 NORTH-EAST MONSOON
   ☔ SOUTH-WEST MONSOON
```

### (b) The ITCZ — Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone

> **ITCZ** = a broad **low-pressure trough near the equator** where the north-east and south-east trade winds converge.

**In summer, the ITCZ shifts north** over the Gangetic plain (to about 20–25°N). This northward shift pulls the **south-east trade winds** across the equator. **On crossing the equator they are deflected right by the Coriolis force and become the south-west monsoon.**

> **This is the single most important addition Class 9 makes.** The south-west monsoon is **not a locally generated wind** — it is the **southern hemisphere's south-east trades, crossing the equator and turning right.** That's why it arrives loaded with moisture: it has travelled thousands of kilometres over warm ocean.

### (c) Jet streams — the upper-air control

| Jet stream | When | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Subtropical Westerly Jet (STWJ)** | **Winter** | Blows south of the Himalayas, ~25°N. Brings **western disturbances** |
| **Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ)** | **Summer** | Blows over peninsular India ~14°N. **Helps intensify the monsoon** |

**The withdrawal of the STWJ from the Indian plains is the trigger for monsoon onset.** As long as the westerly jet sits over northern India, it suppresses the rising air the monsoon needs. **When it retreats north of the Himalayas in early June, the monsoon can burst.**

> 🎯 **"The monsoon bursts when the subtropical westerly jet withdraws north of the Himalayas and the easterly jet establishes itself" is a sentence that will lift a GS-I monsoon answer immediately.** Most candidates only describe land–sea heating.

### (d) The Tibetan Plateau

The vast, high Tibetan Plateau heats intensely in summer, acting as a **massive elevated heat source** — creating strong rising air and reinforcing the low pressure that draws the monsoon in. **It is effectively a heater suspended 4–5 km up in the atmosphere**, and its role is a large part of why the Indian monsoon is the strongest in the world.

### (e) Western disturbances

**Low-pressure systems originating over the Mediterranean**, carried east by the subtropical westerly jet. They bring **winter rainfall to north-west India** — vital for the **rabi crop, especially wheat** — and snowfall to the western Himalayas.

### (f) El Niño and the Southern Oscillation (ENSO)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Southern Oscillation (SO)** | A see-saw of atmospheric pressure between the eastern and western Pacific |
| **El Niño** | **Warming of the surface waters of the eastern Pacific**, off the Peruvian coast |
| **Effect on India** | **Associated with WEAK monsoons and drought in India** |
| **La Niña** | The opposite phase — cooling of the eastern Pacific; **often associated with a good monsoon** |

> **How to hold El Niño straight — it's the most-confused item in this chapter.**
> **El Niño = warm eastern Pacific = weak Indian monsoon.**
> The mechanism, briefly: normally the western Pacific (near Indonesia) is warmer, and rising air there feeds a circulation that supports the Indian monsoon. When the eastern Pacific warms, that convection **shifts eastward, away from Asia** — weakening the pull that drives monsoon rainfall over India.
>
> ⚠️ **The nuance:** El Niño **increases the probability** of a weak monsoon; it does not guarantee one. Some El Niño years have had normal rainfall. Saying "strongly associated with, but not deterministic of" is more accurate and reads better than an absolute claim.

## 3️⃣ Onset and Withdrawal

| | |
|---|---|
| **Onset** | Around **1 June** on the **Kerala coast** |
| **Split** | The monsoon splits into two branches at the peninsula's tip: the **Arabian Sea branch** and the **Bay of Bengal branch** |
| **Covers India by** | Mid-July |
| **"Burst"** | The sudden, violent onset of rainfall — not a gradual increase |
| **Withdrawal** | Begins in NW India by **early September**; complete by early December in the south |
| **Duration** | ~100–120 days |

**The Bay of Bengal branch is deflected west by the Himalayas** and travels **up the Ganga plain**, which is why rainfall in the northern plains **decreases from east to west** — Kolkata gets far more than Delhi.

**Why Mawsynram/Cherrapunji are the wettest:** the Bay branch strikes the **funnel-shaped Khasi Hills of Meghalaya**, which channel and force the moist air sharply upward. **The shape of the hills concentrates the rainfall.**

**Why Rajasthan is dry despite the Arabian Sea branch passing over it:** the **Aravallis run parallel to that branch** rather than across it, so the air is never forced to rise. **No forced ascent, no orographic rainfall.**

## 4️⃣ The Four Seasons

| Season | Months | Characteristics | Local phenomena |
|---|---|---|---|
| ❄️ **COLD WEATHER (Winter)** | **Mid-Nov – Feb** | Clear skies, low humidity, cool. Temperature decreases south → north | **Western disturbances** bring rain to NW India (vital for wheat). **NE monsoon** brings rain to the Coromandel coast |
| 🔥 **HOT WEATHER (Summer)** | **March – May** | Rising temperature, falling pressure. Low-pressure trough forms | **LOO** — hot dry wind, N India · **KAAL BAISAKHI** — violent thunderstorms in WB/Assam · **MANGO SHOWERS** — pre-monsoon showers in Kerala & Karnataka that help ripen mangoes |
| ☔ **ADVANCING MONSOON (Rainy)** | **June – September** | **~75% of annual rainfall.** Monsoon "burst"; **breaks** in the monsoon | Rainfall arrives in wet and dry spells, not continuously |
| 🍂 **RETREATING MONSOON (Autumn)** | **October – November** | Monsoon withdraws; clear skies, high humidity. **"October heat"** — oppressive warmth | **Cyclones** in the Bay of Bengal hit the east coast |

> 🥭 **The three named local winds are almost free Prelims marks:**
> - **Loo** — hot, dry, dusty wind, north India, May–June. Can be lethal.
> - **Kaal Baisakhi** — "the calamity of Baisakh"; violent evening thunderstorms in West Bengal and Assam.
> - **Mango showers** — pre-monsoon showers in Kerala and coastal Karnataka; help mangoes ripen. Also called **"cherry blossom showers"** in Karnataka's coffee regions, where they aid coffee flowering.

> ⚠️ **"Breaks" in the monsoon** are dry spells *within* the rainy season. **The monsoon does not rain continuously for four months** — it comes in wet and dry phases, and a long break at the wrong stage of crop growth can be as damaging as a weak monsoon overall.

## 5️⃣ Distribution of Rainfall

| Level | Where |
|---|---|
| **Very high (>400 cm)** | Western coast, Western Ghats windward, NE India (Meghalaya) |
| **Moderate (100–200 cm)** | Southern parts of Gujarat, eastern Tamil Nadu, NE Peninsula, eastern MP |
| **Low (50–100 cm)** | Western UP, Punjab, Haryana, parts of Karnataka and Deccan |
| **Very low (<50 cm)** | **Western Rajasthan, Ladakh, parts of Punjab and Gujarat**, the Deccan interior |

**Wettest:** **Mawsynram/Cherrapunji** (Meghalaya). **Driest:** western Rajasthan and **Ladakh** (a cold desert in the Himalayan rain shadow).

**The general pattern:** rainfall decreases **east → west** across the northern plains, and **windward → leeward** across the Western Ghats.

> 🌱 **Monsoon variability is the real problem, not the average.** India's *average* rainfall is adequate; what causes distress is that it is **concentrated in four months, unevenly distributed in space, and highly variable year to year.** Climate change is intensifying this — **fewer rainy days but more intense downpours**, meaning more floods *and* more droughts in the same country in the same year. **That specific formulation is strong GS-III material.**

## 6️⃣ The Monsoon as a Unifying Bond

NCERT makes an argument worth being able to reproduce:

- The Himalayas protect the whole subcontinent from cold Central Asian winds, giving it a **shared climatic regime**.
- The monsoon **arrives across the whole country**, and its rhythm sets the **agricultural calendar everywhere**.
- **Festivals, cropping seasons and cultural life** are organised around it across regions and religions.
- The river valleys that carry the monsoon's water sustain a **single, interconnected hydrological system**.

> 🎯 **"The monsoon is a unifying bond" is a quotable line for both Geography and Indian Society answers** — one physical phenomenon giving a hugely diverse subcontinent a shared rhythm.

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. **Six controls: latitude, altitude, pressure/winds, distance from sea, ocean currents, relief.**
2. **The SW monsoon is the southern hemisphere's SE trades, crossing the equator and deflecting right** as the ITCZ shifts north.
3. **STWJ (winter) brings western disturbances; TEJ (summer) intensifies the monsoon.** **The monsoon bursts when the STWJ withdraws north of the Himalayas.**
4. **The Tibetan Plateau is an elevated heat source** reinforcing the monsoon low.
5. **Western disturbances give NW India its winter rain — critical for wheat.**
6. **El Niño = warm eastern Pacific = weak Indian monsoon** (probabilistic, not certain). **La Niña = opposite.**
7. **Onset ~1 June on the Kerala coast; two branches (Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal); covers India by mid-July.**
8. **Rainfall falls east → west across the northern plains** because the Bay branch is deflected up the Ganga plain.
9. **Mawsynram is wettest because the funnel-shaped Khasi Hills force air sharply up. Rajasthan is dry because the Aravallis run parallel to the wind.**
10. **Four seasons + three local winds: Loo, Kaal Baisakhi, Mango showers.**
11. **Tamil Nadu's rain comes from the retreating/NE monsoon (Oct–Dec).**
12. 🌱 **Variability, not the average, is the problem** — and climate change means fewer rainy days with more intense downpours: floods and droughts together.
