# Chapter 6 — Population

> **The one-line point:** Population is **the pivotal element** in social studies — it is both the producer and the consumer of every other resource. India's story is not really about numbers any more; it is about **composition** — how old people are, how educated, how healthy, and how unevenly all of that is distributed.

## 1️⃣ Size and Distribution

| | 2011 Census |
|---|---|
| **Population** | **1.21 billion** — **17.5% of world population on 2.4% of land area** |
| **Density** | **382 persons/sq km** |
| **Most populous state** | **Uttar Pradesh** (~199 million) |
| **Least populous state** | **Sikkim** (~0.6 million) |
| **Highest density** | **Bihar** (1,106) — then West Bengal, Kerala, UP |
| **Lowest density** | **Arunachal Pradesh** (17) |
| **UT with highest density** | **Delhi** |

> ⚠️ **Two traps here.** **Uttar Pradesh is the most populous state; Bihar has the highest density.** They are different questions. And **Arunachal Pradesh has the lowest density, but Sikkim has the smallest population** — again different.

### Why distribution is uneven

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Relief** | **Plains are densely populated**; mountains and deserts are not |
| **Climate** | Moderate climates attract; extremes repel |
| **Soil** | Fertile alluvium supports dense populations |
| **Water** | The first requirement — river valleys are the densest regions |
| **Minerals** | Attract mining settlement — Jharkhand, Odisha |
| **Urbanisation & industry** | Jobs pull people — Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru |
| **Social/political** | Services, stability, government policy |

**Half of India's population lives in just five states: Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, West Bengal and Madhya Pradesh.** Rajasthan is the largest state by area but holds only ~5.5% of the population — **because most of it is the Thar Desert.** **Area and population are not correlated; habitability is what matters.**

## 2️⃣ Population Growth and Change

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Population growth** | The change in the number of people over time |
| **Growth rate** | Change expressed as a percentage |
| **Natural increase** | **Birth rate − death rate** |
| **Migration** | Movement in or out (does not change *national* total, but redistributes it) |

### India's demographic transition

```
   INDIA'S POPULATION STORY IN FOUR PHASES

   Pre-1921   High birth rate, HIGH death rate  → population nearly static
              1921 = THE "DEMOGRAPHIC DIVIDE" — the last year
                 India's population DECLINED

   1921-51    Death rate starts falling; birth rate still high
              → steady growth begins

   1951-81    Death rate falls FAST (medicine, food security, sanitation)
                 while birth rate stays high
              → POPULATION EXPLOSION. Growth peaked at ~2.2%/yr

   1981-now   Birth rate finally begins to fall
              → growth rate DECLINING, though absolute numbers still rise
```

> **1921 is called the "Demographic Divide" or "Year of the Great Divide"** — the last year in which India's population actually fell (influenza pandemic, famine). **Every year since has seen growth.** This is a favourite one-liner.

> **The key insight, restated because it is so often got wrong:** India's population explosion was caused by **death rates falling**, not by birth rates rising. Better medicine, food security and sanitation meant people stopped dying young. **"Indians started having too many children" is simply false as an explanation.**

> 📉 **The current position matters for answers.** India's **Total Fertility Rate (TFR) is now ~2.0 (NFHS-5)** — **below the replacement level of 2.1.** India's population is still growing only because of **population momentum**: a large cohort already of childbearing age. **India has, in effect, already completed its fertility transition** — a point most candidates don't know, and one that changes the whole framing of a population answer away from "control" and towards "ageing and skills."

## 3️⃣ Population Composition

### Age structure

| Group | Share (2011) | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Children (0–14)** | ~29% | Economically dependent; need education and nutrition |
| **Working age (15–59)** | **~63%** | Economically productive — **the demographic dividend** |
| **Aged (60+)** | ~8% | Dependent; need pensions and healthcare |

**India's median age is around 28** — one of the youngest large populations in the world.

> **The dividend is conditional and time-bound** (see [Cl.8 Ch.6](../../Class_08_Resources_and_Development/Notes/06_Human_Resources.md)). It requires **jobs, education, health and female workforce participation.** **A young population without employment is unrest, not growth.** India's window runs to roughly the 2040s.
>
> **And the internal variation is sharp:** Kerala and Tamil Nadu are already ageing, while Bihar and UP remain very young. **India will experience a demographic dividend and a demographic burden simultaneously, in different states** — which connects directly to delimitation, fiscal devolution and internal migration debates in GS-II.

### Sex ratio

> **Sex ratio** = number of **females per 1,000 males**.

| | |
|---|---|
| **India, 2011** | **943** |
| **Highest** | **Kerala (1,084)** — the only state above 1,000 |
| **Lowest** | **Haryana (879)** |
| **Child sex ratio (0–6), 2011** | **919 — and it had been falling**, which is the more alarming figure |

> ⚠️ **The child sex ratio is the number to watch, not the overall sex ratio.** The overall ratio is influenced by women's longer life expectancy. **The 0–6 ratio reflects what is happening now** — and its decline points to sex-selective practices. **This is the statistical basis for the PCPNDT Act (1994) and Beti Bachao Beti Padhao.** **A precise, high-value distinction for GS-I/GS-II.**

### Literacy

| | 2011 |
|---|---|
| **Overall** | **73%** |
| **Male** | ~80.9% |
| **Female** | **~64.6%** — the gap is the point |
| **Highest** | **Kerala (~94%)** |
| **Lowest** | **Bihar (~62%)** |

**Definition:** a literate person is aged **7 and above** who can **read and write with understanding** in any language.

### Occupational structure

| Sector | Share of workforce | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **~53%** (declining) | Agriculture, still the largest employer |
| **Secondary** | ~13% | Manufacturing |
| **Tertiary** | ~34% (rising) | Services |

> **The structural problem in one line:** agriculture employs roughly **half the workforce** but contributes only around **a sixth of GDP.** **That gap is the definition of disguised unemployment** — too many people on too little land, producing too little value each. **Moving people out of agriculture into productive non-farm work is the central challenge of Indian development**, and this single comparison states it better than a paragraph.

### Health

| Indicator | Progress |
|---|---|
| **Life expectancy** | Rose from ~37 years (1951) to ~70 |
| **Infant mortality rate** | Fallen sharply, but still high compared to peers |
| **Malnutrition** | **Persistent** — stunting and anaemia remain widespread despite growth |

## 4️⃣ Adolescents and the National Population Policy

**Adolescents (10–19) are about one-fifth of India's population** — a very large cohort. Their nutrition, education and health determine the country's next thirty years.

**Concerns:** low nutritional intake (especially girls), anaemia, early marriage, lack of health awareness, and mental health.

### National Population Policy (NPP) 2000

| Goal | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Immediate** | Meet contraceptive needs, health infrastructure, health personnel |
| **Medium-term** | Bring **TFR to replacement level (2.1)** |
| **Long-term** | Stabilise population by **2045** |

**Specific NPP 2000 provisions worth knowing:**
- **Free and compulsory school education up to age 14**
- **Reduce infant mortality below 30 per 1,000 live births**
- **Universal immunisation**
- **Delay the age of marriage for girls**
- Promote small families through voluntary means

> **The argument the policy embodies, which is the point of it:** population is stabilised through **education, healthcare and delayed marriage — not through coercion.** **The strongest single predictor of falling fertility anywhere in the world is female education.** Kerala is the domestic proof: high female literacy, low fertility, high sex ratio, high life expectancy — achieved without coercive measures.
>
> 🎯 **This makes the "development is the best contraceptive" argument, and it is the correct answer to any question about population control.** Coercive approaches — including India's own 1975–77 experience — are both ethically indefensible and empirically less effective.
