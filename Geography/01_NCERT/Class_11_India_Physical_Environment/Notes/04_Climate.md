## 1️⃣ ⭐ Unity and Diversity in the Monsoon Climate

⭐ **The Indian subcontinent experiences a variety of climatic conditions.** ⭐ **Yet, the climate of India is described as the "MONSOON" type.**

⭐ **The word MONSOON is derived from the Arabic word "MAUSIM" which literally means SEASON.** ⭐ **"Monsoon" refers to the seasonal reversal in the wind direction during a year.**

### ⭐ Regional variations — the extremes worth quoting

| Contrast | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Temperature extremes** | ⭐ **In summer, the mercury occasionally touches 55°C in the western Rajasthan; it drops down to as low as minus 45°C in winter around Leh** |
| ⭐ **Diurnal range** | ⭐ **Churu in Rajasthan may record a temperature of 50°C or more on a June day while the mercury hardly touches 19°C on the same day in Tawang (Arunachal Pradesh)** |
| ⭐ **Coastal moderation** | ⭐ **On a December night, temperature in Drass (Ladakh) may drop down to minus 45°C while Thiruvananthapuram on the same night records 20°C or 22°C** |
| ⭐ **Rainfall extremes** | ⭐ **MAWSYNRAM in the southern ranges of the Khasi Hills receives the highest average rainfall in the world — while Jaisalmer rarely gets more than 12 cm** |
| ⭐ **Snow vs monsoon** | ⭐ **While snowfall occurs in the Himalayas, it only rains over the rest of the country** |
| ⭐ **Timing** | ⭐ **Tura, situated in the Garo Hills of Meghalaya, may receive an amount of rainfall in a single day which is equal to 10 years of rainfall at Jaisalmer** |

> ⭐ **These contrasts make the point NCERT wants: India has almost every climatic regime on Earth within one country** — and yet the **monsoon rhythm unites all of them.** ⭐ **The whole country waits for the same wind.**

## 2️⃣ ⭐ Factors Determining India's Climate

⭐ **India's climate is controlled by a number of factors which can be broadly divided into two groups: (i) factors related to LOCATION AND RELIEF; (ii) factors related to AIR PRESSURE AND WINDS.**

### ⭐ Factors related to location and relief

| Factor | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Latitude** | ⭐ **The Tropic of Cancer passes through the central part of India in east-west direction.** ⭐ **Thus, northern part of the India lies in sub-tropical and temperate zone and the part lying south of the Tropic of Cancer falls in the tropical zone** |
| ⭐ **The Himalayan mountains** | ⭐ **The lofty Himalayas in the north along with its extensions act as an EFFECTIVE CLIMATIC DIVIDE.** ⭐ **The towering mountain chain provides an invincible shield to protect the subcontinent from the cold northern winds.** ⭐ **These cold and chilly winds originate near the Arctic circle and blow across central and eastern Asia.** ⭐ **The Himalayas also trap the monsoon winds, forcing them to shed their moisture within the subcontinent** |
| ⭐ **Distribution of land and water** | ⭐ **India is flanked by the Indian Ocean on three sides in the south and girdled by a high and continuous mountain-wall in the north.** ⭐ **As compared to the landmass, water heats up or cools down slowly.** ⭐ **This differential heating of land and sea creates different air pressure zones in different seasons in and around the Indian subcontinent.** ⭐ **Difference in air pressure causes reversal in the direction of monsoon winds** |
| ⭐ **Distance from the sea** | ⭐ **With a long coastline, large coastal areas have an equable climate.** ⭐ **Areas in the interior of India are far away from the moderating influence of the sea.** ⭐ **Such areas have extremes of climate** |
| ⭐ **Altitude** | ⭐ **Temperature decreases with height.** ⭐ **Due to thin air, places in the mountains are cooler than places on the plains** |
| ⭐ **Relief** | ⭐ **The physiography or relief of India also affects the temperature, air pressure, direction and speed of wind and the amount and distribution of rainfall.** ⭐ **The windward sides of Western Ghats and Assam receive high rainfall during June–September whereas the southern plateau remains dry due to its leeward situation along the Western Ghats** |

> ⭐⭐ **The Himalayas do TWO enormous jobs, and both must be stated in a Mains answer:**
> ⭐ **1. They BLOCK the freezing Central Asian winter winds** — which is why north India is far warmer in winter than other places at the same latitude.
> ⭐ **2. They TRAP the monsoon** — forcing moisture-laden winds to rise and shed their rain inside the subcontinent rather than passing over it.
>
> ⭐ **Without the Himalayas, India would be a cold desert.** Compare the same latitudes westward — the Sahara and the Arabian Desert. **India's fertility is a gift of that wall.**

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐⭐ The Monsoon Mechanism — In Full

### ⭐ (a) Differential heating and pressure

⭐ **The differential heating and cooling of land and water creates low pressure on the landmass of India while the seas around experience comparatively high pressure.**

⭐ **In winter, the pattern reverses — a high pressure develops over the landmass and low pressure over the ocean.**

### ⭐⭐ (b) The shift of the ITCZ

⭐ **The shift of the position of the INTER TROPICAL CONVERGENCE ZONE (ITCZ) in summer over the Ganga plain** — this is the equatorial trough normally positioned about 5°N of the equator. ⭐ **It is also known as the MONSOON TROUGH during the monsoon season.**

⭐ **The ITCZ shifts NORTHWARD in summer, drawing the south-east trade winds of the southern hemisphere across the equator.** ⭐ **On crossing the equator these winds are deflected to the right by the Coriolis force and become the SOUTH-WEST MONSOON.**

> ⭐⭐ **This is the single most important mechanism in the chapter:** ⭐ **the south-west monsoon is NOT a locally generated wind. It is the SOUTHERN HEMISPHERE'S SOUTH-EAST TRADE WINDS, crossing the equator and turning right.** ⭐ **That is why it arrives loaded with moisture — it has travelled thousands of kilometres over warm ocean.**

### ⭐⭐ (c) Jet streams — the upper-air control

| Jet stream | ⭐ Season | ⭐ Role |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **SUBTROPICAL WESTERLY JET (STWJ)** | ⭐ **Winter** | ⭐ **Blows SOUTH of the Himalayas, around 25°N. Brings WESTERN DISTURBANCES** |
| ⭐ **TROPICAL EASTERLY JET (TEJ) / Somali Jet** | ⭐ **Summer** | ⭐ **Blows over peninsular India around 14°N. INTENSIFIES the monsoon** |

⭐⭐ **The withdrawal of the STWJ from the Indian plains is the TRIGGER for monsoon onset.** ⭐ **As long as the westerly jet sits over northern India, it suppresses the rising air the monsoon requires.** ⭐ **When it retreats north of the Himalayas in early June, the monsoon can BURST.**

> 🎯 ⭐⭐ **"The monsoon bursts when the subtropical westerly jet withdraws north of the Himalayas and the tropical easterly jet establishes itself" is the sentence that will lift a GS-I monsoon answer immediately.** ⭐ **Most candidates describe only land–sea heating. Adding the upper-air control is what distinguishes a good answer.**

### ⭐ (d) The Tibetan Plateau — an elevated heat source

⭐ **The presence of the high-pressure area, east of Madagascar, approximately at 20°S over the Indian Ocean.** ⭐ **The intensity and position of this high-pressure area affects the Indian Monsoon.**

⭐ **The TIBETAN PLATEAU gets intensely heated during summer, which results in strong vertical air currents and the formation of low pressure over the plateau at about 9 km above sea level.**

> ⭐ **The Tibetan Plateau is effectively a HEATER SUSPENDED 4–5 KM UP IN THE ATMOSPHERE.** ⭐ **It creates a high-level low-pressure region that reinforces the pull drawing the monsoon in — and it is a large part of why the Indian monsoon is the strongest in the world.**

### ⭐ (e) Southern Oscillation, El Niño and the Indian Ocean Dipole

⭐ **SOUTHERN OSCILLATION (SO):** ⭐ **When the tropical eastern south Pacific Ocean experiences high pressure, the tropical eastern Indian Ocean experiences low pressure.** ⭐ **But in certain years, there is a reversal in the pressure conditions and the eastern Pacific has lower pressure in comparison to the eastern Indian Ocean.** ⭐ **This periodic change in pressure conditions is known as the Southern Oscillation.**

⭐ **The difference in pressure over Tahiti (Pacific, 18°S/149°W) and Darwin in northern Australia (Indian Ocean, 12°30′S/131°E) is computed to predict the intensity of the monsoons.** ⭐ **If the pressure differences were NEGATIVE, it would mean below average and late monsoons.**

⭐ **EL NINO:** ⭐ **A feature connected with the SO.** ⭐ **It is a warm ocean current that flows past the Peruvian Coast, in place of the cold Peruvian current, every 2 to 5 years.** ⭐ **The changes in pressure conditions are connected to the El Nino. Hence, the phenomenon is referred to as ENSO (El Nino Southern Oscillations).**

> ⭐⭐ **Hold El Niño straight: ⭐ EL NIÑO = WARM eastern Pacific = WEAK Indian monsoon.**
> ⭐ **Mechanism:** normally the western Pacific (Indonesia) is warmer, and rising air there feeds a circulation supporting the Indian monsoon. When the eastern Pacific warms, that convection **shifts eastward, away from Asia** — weakening the pull driving monsoon rainfall over India.
> ⚠️ ⭐ **El Niño INCREASES THE PROBABILITY of a weak monsoon; it does not guarantee one.** **"Strongly associated with, but not deterministic of" is the accurate and better-reading formulation.**

> ⭐ **The INDIAN OCEAN DIPOLE (IOD) is worth adding for current affairs**, though NCERT does not develop it: ⭐ **a positive IOD (warmer western Indian Ocean near Africa, cooler eastern near Indonesia) is associated with a GOOD Indian monsoon and can OFFSET an El Niño.** **Mentioning that El Niño's effect can be moderated by a positive IOD shows genuinely current reading.**

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐ Onset, Advance, Break and Withdrawal

| Stage | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **ONSET** | ⭐ **Around the 1st week of JUNE on the KERALA coast** |
| ⭐ **Two branches** | ⭐ **The monsoon splits at the peninsula's tip into the ARABIAN SEA branch and the BAY OF BENGAL branch** |
| ⭐ **"BURST"** | ⭐ **The sudden, violent onset of rainfall — a sudden increase in rainfall, not a gradual build-up** |
| ⭐ **Covers India by** | ⭐ **Mid-July** |
| ⭐ **BREAK** | ⭐ **Dry spells WITHIN the rainy season.** ⭐ **The monsoon is not continuous — it comes in wet and dry phases** |
| ⭐ **WITHDRAWAL / RETREAT** | ⭐ **Begins in NW India by EARLY SEPTEMBER; complete from the southern half of the peninsula by early December** |
| ⭐ **Duration** | ⭐ **~100–120 days, from early June to mid-September** |

⭐ **The BAY OF BENGAL BRANCH is deflected WEST by the Himalayas and travels UP THE GANGA PLAIN** — which is why rainfall in the northern plains **decreases from east to west.** ⭐ **Kolkata gets far more than Delhi.**

### ⭐ Why the extremes are where they are

| Place | ⭐ Explanation |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **MAWSYNRAM / CHERRAPUNJI (wettest)** | ⭐ **The Bay branch strikes the FUNNEL-SHAPED Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, which channel and force the moist air sharply upward.** ⭐ **The SHAPE of the hills concentrates the rainfall** |
| ⭐ **RAJASTHAN (dry)** | ⭐ **The ARAVALLIS run PARALLEL to the Arabian Sea branch rather than across it, so the air is never forced to rise.** ⭐ **No forced ascent, no orographic rainfall** |
| ⭐ **LADAKH (cold desert)** | ⭐ **Lies in the RAIN SHADOW of the Great Himalaya — the moisture is spent before the air arrives** |
| ⭐ **Deccan interior (dry)** | ⭐ **Leeward of the Western Ghats — the classic RAIN SHADOW** |

> ⭐ **The Rajasthan explanation is the elegant one and it gets asked.** ⭐ **The Arabian Sea branch DOES pass over Rajasthan.** ⭐ **But because the Aravallis lie PARALLEL to the wind direction rather than perpendicular to it, the air is never lifted, never cooled, and never precipitates.** **A mountain range only causes rain if it stands ACROSS the wind.**

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐ The Four Seasons

⭐ **The Meteorological Department of India recognises FOUR seasons:**

| Season | ⭐ Months | ⭐ Characteristics | ⭐ Local phenomena |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **COLD WEATHER (Winter)** | ⭐ **Mid-Nov to Feb** | ⭐ **Clear sky, low temperature, low humidity, feeble variable winds.** ⭐ **Temperature decreases from south to north** | ⭐ **WESTERN DISTURBANCES** bring rain to NW India — vital for the RABI/WHEAT crop. ⭐ **NE monsoon brings rain to the Coromandel coast** |
| ⭐ **HOT WEATHER (Summer)** | ⭐ **March to May** | ⭐ **Rising temperature, falling pressure.** ⭐ **A low-pressure trough forms** | ⭐ **LOO** (hot dry wind, N. India) · ⭐ **KAAL BAISAKHI / NOR'WESTERS** (violent thunderstorms in WB and Assam) · ⭐ **MANGO SHOWERS** (pre-monsoon showers in Kerala and coastal Karnataka; also **"CHERRY BLOSSOM SHOWERS"** in Karnataka's coffee regions) |
| ⭐ **SOUTH-WEST MONSOON (Rainy)** | ⭐ **June to September** | ⭐ **~75% of annual rainfall.** ⭐ **Monsoon "burst"; BREAKS in the monsoon** | Rainfall in wet and dry spells |
| ⭐ **RETREATING MONSOON (Autumn)** | ⭐ **October to November** | ⭐ **Monsoon withdraws; clear skies, high humidity.** ⭐ **"OCTOBER HEAT" — oppressive warmth** | ⭐ **CYCLONES in the Bay of Bengal strike the east coast.** ⭐ **Tamil Nadu's main rainy season** |

> ⭐⭐ **Tamil Nadu is the exception that gets asked constantly.** ⭐ **Most of India gets its rain from the SOUTH-WEST monsoon (June–Sept).** ⭐ **TAMIL NADU gets most of its rain from the RETREATING / NORTH-EAST monsoon (Oct–Dec)** — because by then the winds blow from land to sea **across the Bay of Bengal**, picking up moisture before striking the Coromandel coast.

> ⭐ **The three named local winds are near-free Prelims marks:** ⭐ **LOO** (hot, dry, dusty, north India, May–June; can be lethal) · ⭐ **KAAL BAISAKHI / NOR'WESTERS** ("the calamity of Baisakh"; violent evening thunderstorms in WB and Assam) · ⭐ **MANGO SHOWERS** (pre-monsoon showers helping mangoes ripen; **"cherry blossom showers"** in coffee regions).

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐ Distribution and Variability of Rainfall

### ⭐ The rainfall zones

| Zone | ⭐ Amount | ⭐ Where |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Areas of HIGH rainfall** | ⭐ **Over 200 cm** | ⭐ **Western coast, Western Ghats, sub-Himalayan areas in the north east and the hills of Meghalaya.** ⭐ **Here, rainfall exceeds 1,000 cm in parts of the Khasi and Jaintia hills** |
| ⭐ **Areas of MEDIUM rainfall** | ⭐ **100–200 cm** | ⭐ **Southern parts of Gujarat, east Tamil Nadu, north-eastern Peninsula covering Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, eastern Madhya Pradesh, northern Ganga plain along the sub-Himalayas and the Cachar Valley and Manipur** |
| ⭐ **Areas of LOW rainfall** | ⭐ **50–100 cm** | ⭐ **Western Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, eastern Rajasthan, Gujarat, Deccan Plateau** |
| ⭐ **Areas of INADEQUATE rainfall** | ⭐ **Less than 50 cm** | ⭐ **Parts of the Peninsula, especially in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Maharashtra, Ladakh and most of western Rajasthan** |

⭐ **Snowfall is restricted to the Himalayan region.**

### ⭐⭐ Variability of rainfall

⭐ **The variability of rainfall is computed with the help of the following formula:**

⭐ **C.V. = (Standard Deviation ÷ Mean) × 100** ⭐ **where C.V. is the coefficient of variation.**

| Variability | ⭐ Where |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Less than 25%** | ⭐ **Western coasts, Western Ghats, north-eastern Peninsula, eastern plain of the Ganga, north-eastern India, Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh and south-western part of Jammu and Kashmir.** ⭐ **These areas have an annual rainfall of over 100 cm** |
| ⭐ **More than 50%** | ⭐ **Western part of Rajasthan, northern part of Jammu and Kashmir and interior parts of the Deccan plateau.** ⭐ **These areas have an annual rainfall of less than 50 cm** |
| ⭐ **25–50%** | ⭐ **The rest of India** |

> ⭐⭐ **The relationship in that table is the important insight, and it's cruel: ⭐ THE AREAS WITH THE LEAST RAINFALL ALSO HAVE THE MOST VARIABLE RAINFALL.**
>
> ⭐ **Western Rajasthan gets under 50 cm AND has over 50% variability.** ⭐ **The Western Ghats get over 100 cm AND have under 25% variability.**
>
> ⭐ **So the driest regions cannot even rely on the little they get** — while the wettest regions can plan around a dependable supply. **This is why drought is concentrated where it is, and why irrigation and water harvesting matter disproportionately in exactly those regions.** **A precise, high-value point for GS-III.**

## 7️⃣ ⭐ Köppen's Classification Applied to India

⭐ **Koeppen's scheme is based on the monthly values of temperature and precipitation.** ⭐ **He identified five major climatic types.**

| Type | ⭐ Code | ⭐ Where in India |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Tropical Savannah** | ⭐ **Aw** | ⭐ **Most of the Peninsular plateau, south of the Tropic of Cancer** |
| ⭐ **Tropical monsoon** | ⭐ **Amw** | ⭐ **West coast of India, south of Goa** |
| ⭐ **Tropical wet & dry** | ⭐ **As** | ⭐ **Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu** |
| ⭐ **Semi-arid steppe** | ⭐ **BShw** | ⭐ **North-western Gujarat, some parts of western Rajasthan and Punjab** |
| ⭐ **Hot desert** | ⭐ **BWhw** | ⭐ **Extreme western Rajasthan** |
| ⭐ **Monsoon with dry winter** | ⭐ **Cwg** | ⭐ **Ganga plain, eastern Rajasthan, northern Madhya Pradesh, most of North-east India** |
| ⭐ **Cold humid winter with short summer** | ⭐ **Dfc** | ⭐ **Arunachal Pradesh** |
| ⭐ **Polar type** | ⭐ **E** | ⭐ **Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand** |

⚠️ ⭐ **Note the odd one: the Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu is classified "As" — tropical wet and dry with a DRY SUMMER** — because its rain comes in the retreating monsoon, not the south-west monsoon.

## 8️⃣ 🌱 Monsoon and Climate Change

> 🌱 ⭐ **The key argument for GS-III: VARIABILITY, not the average, is the problem.**
>
> ⭐ **India's average rainfall is adequate.** What causes distress is that it is **concentrated in four months, unevenly distributed in space, and highly variable year to year** (the C.V. table above).
>
> 🌱 ⭐ **Climate change is intensifying exactly this: FEWER RAINY DAYS but MORE INTENSE DOWNPOURS.** ⭐ **The result is MORE FLOODS AND MORE DROUGHTS, in the same country, sometimes in the same season.**
>
> 🎯 ⭐ **That specific formulation — "the total may not change much; the distribution is changing catastrophically" — is far stronger than a generic statement about warming.**

🌱 ⭐ **Also worth carrying:** ⭐ **phenological mismatch** (Cl.6 Ch.3) — a delayed or erratic monsoon decouples sowing calendars, flowering, pollinator emergence and harvest timings that were synchronised over millennia. **The mismatch is the damage, not the temperature number.**

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. ⭐ **"Mausim" = season. Monsoon = seasonal REVERSAL of wind direction.**
2. ⭐ **India's extremes: 55°C (W. Rajasthan) to −45°C (Drass); Mawsynram wettest on Earth vs Jaisalmer under 12 cm.** **One country, nearly every climate.**
3. ⭐⭐ **The Himalayas do two jobs: BLOCK cold Central Asian winds AND TRAP the monsoon.** **Without them, India would be a cold desert.**
4. ⭐⭐ **The SW monsoon is the southern hemisphere's SE trades, crossing the equator and deflecting right** as the ITCZ (monsoon trough) shifts north.
5. ⭐⭐ **STWJ (winter, ~25°N) brings western disturbances; TEJ (summer, ~14°N) intensifies the monsoon.** ⭐ **The monsoon BURSTS when the STWJ withdraws north of the Himalayas.**
6. ⭐ **The Tibetan Plateau is an elevated heat source creating low pressure at ~9 km.**
7. ⭐ **Southern Oscillation measured as TAHITI minus DARWIN pressure; NEGATIVE = weak, late monsoon.** ⭐ **El Niño = warm Peruvian current every 2–5 years = weak monsoon (probabilistic).** ⭐ **A positive IOD can offset it.**
8. ⭐ **Onset ~1 June, Kerala; two branches; covers India by mid-July; BREAKS within; withdrawal from early September.**
9. ⭐ **Rainfall falls east→west across the northern plains** (Bay branch deflected up the Ganga plain). ⭐ **Mawsynram is wet because funnel-shaped Khasi Hills force air sharply up; Rajasthan is dry because the Aravallis run PARALLEL to the wind.**
10. ⭐ **Four seasons + three local winds: Loo, Kaal Baisakhi, Mango showers.** ⭐ **Tamil Nadu's rain comes from the retreating/NE monsoon.**
11. ⭐⭐ **Variability C.V. = (SD ÷ Mean) × 100.** ⭐ **The driest regions (<50 cm) also have the HIGHEST variability (>50%)** — they cannot rely even on the little they get.
12. ⭐ **Köppen in India: Aw · Amw · As (Coromandel) · BShw · BWhw · Cwg · Dfc (Arunachal) · E (Himalaya).**
13. 🌱 ⭐ **Climate change means fewer rainy days with more intense downpours — floods AND droughts together.** **The distribution is changing, not merely the total.**
