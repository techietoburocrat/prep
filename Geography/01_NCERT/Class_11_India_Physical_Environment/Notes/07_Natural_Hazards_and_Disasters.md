## 1️⃣ ⭐⭐ Hazard vs Disaster — The Core Distinction

| Term | ⭐ Definition |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **NATURAL HAZARDS** | ⭐ **Elements of circumstances in the Natural environment that have the potential to cause harm to people or property or both.** ⭐ **These are inevitable events which happen in the natural environment** |
| ⭐ **NATURAL DISASTERS** | ⭐ **Natural hazards which cause UNACCEPTABLE loss of life or property.** ⭐ **A disaster is an event that occurs suddenly/unexpectedly in most cases and disrupts the normal course of life in the affected area** |

⭐ **Natural disasters are events that occur in nature and cause loss of life and property.**

> ⭐⭐ **The distinction, stated as an equation you can deploy in any GS-III answer:**
>
> ⭐ **HAZARD × VULNERABILITY = DISASTER**
>
> ⭐ **The hazard is beyond human control.** An earthquake will occur where plates meet, whether or not people live there. ⭐ **But the DISASTER — the death toll and destruction — depends almost entirely on where people live, how they build, what warning they receive and how quickly help arrives.**
>
> ⭐ **A magnitude-7 earthquake in an empty desert is a hazard. The same earthquake under a city of unreinforced buildings is a catastrophe.**
>
> 🎯 ⭐ **This is why disaster mortality is a POLICY VARIABLE, not a natural constant** — and it is the single most important framing sentence for this entire topic.

### ⭐ Classification of natural disasters

⭐ **Natural disasters are classified into:**

| Type | ⭐ Examples |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **ATMOSPHERIC** | ⭐ **Blizzards, thunderstorms, lightning, tornadoes, tropical cyclones, drought, hailstorm, frost, heat wave, cold wave** |
| ⭐ **TERRESTRIAL** | ⭐ **Earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, avalanches, subsidence, soil erosion** |
| ⭐ **AQUATIC** | ⭐ **Floods, tidal waves, storm surge, tsunami** |
| ⭐ **BIOLOGICAL** | ⭐ **Plants and animals as epidemics and pests** |

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ Earthquakes

⭐ **An earthquake is a sudden shaking or trembling of the earth's crust.** ⭐ **It is a natural event caused by release of energy from within the earth's crust.**

*(Mechanism, focus/epicentre, P/S/L waves and the shadow zones: [Cl.11 Fundamentals Ch.3](../../Class_11_Fundamentals_of_Physical_Geography/Notes/03_Interior_of_the_Earth.md).)*

### ⭐⭐ India's seismic zones

⭐ **On the basis of the seismicity and other geological and geophysical characteristics, India has been divided into FIVE SEISMIC ZONES** *(now generally cited as four active zones, II–V)*:

| Zone | ⭐ Risk | ⭐ Where |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **ZONE V** | ⭐ **VERY HIGH — highest risk** | ⭐ **Entire north-eastern India, parts of Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, the Rann of Kachchh in Gujarat, part of north Bihar and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands** |
| ⭐ **ZONE IV** | ⭐ **HIGH** | ⭐ **Remaining parts of J&K and Himachal, Union Territory of Delhi, Sikkim, northern parts of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and West Bengal, parts of Gujarat and small portions of Maharashtra and Rajasthan** |
| ⭐ **ZONE III** | ⭐ **MODERATE** | ⭐ **Kerala, Goa, Lakshadweep islands, remaining parts of UP, Gujarat and West Bengal, parts of Punjab, Rajasthan, MP, Bihar, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka** |
| ⭐ **ZONE II** | ⭐ **LOW** | ⭐ **Remaining parts of the country** |

⚠️ ⭐ **DELHI IS IN ZONE IV** — a frequently asked and frequently missed fact.

### ⭐ Why India is seismically active

⭐ **The Indian plate is continuing its northward movement, colliding with the Eurasian plate.** ⭐ **This makes the entire Himalayan belt seismically active** (Ch.2). ⭐ **The Shiwaliks' unconsolidated sediment compounds the damage.**

### 🌱 ⭐ Consequences and management

⭐ **Consequences:** ⭐ **ground shaking, differential ground settlement, land slides, soil liquefaction, ground lurching, avalanches, ground displacement, floods from dam and levee failures, structural collapse, fires, tsunami.**

| Measure | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Seismic zoning and building codes** | ⭐ **Establishing earthquake monitoring centres; using SEISMOGRAPH** |
| ⭐ **Vulnerability maps** | ⭐ **Preparing a vulnerability map of the country and dissemination of information among the people** |
| ⭐ **Construction** | ⭐ **Modifying the house types and building designs in the vulnerable areas and discouraging construction of high-rise buildings, large industrial establishments and big urban centres in such areas** |
| ⭐ **Community preparedness** | ⭐ **Educating people, mock drills, first-response training** |

> 🌱 ⭐⭐ **The framing that scores:** ⭐ **EARTHQUAKES DO NOT KILL PEOPLE — BUILDINGS DO.** ⭐ **Prediction remains scientifically unreliable, so the policy answer is ENFORCEMENT OF SEISMIC BUILDING CODES, not forecasting.**
>
> ⭐ **The evidence: comparable-magnitude events in Japan and Haiti have produced casualty figures differing by orders of magnitude, and the difference is construction quality.**
>
> ⭐ **SOIL LIQUEFACTION is worth naming** — in water-saturated loose soil, shaking makes the ground temporarily behave like a liquid, and buildings sink or tilt without structural failure. **Which is why codes must address soil type, not just structure.**

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐ Tsunami

⭐ **Tsunamis are waves generated by SEISMIC ACTIVITY on the ocean floor** — not by tides.

⭐ **The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 DECEMBER 2004, triggered by a magnitude ~9.1 earthquake off Sumatra, killed ~230,000 people across 14 countries, including in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands.**

⭐ **India's response: the INDIAN TSUNAMI EARLY WARNING CENTRE (ITEWC) at INCOIS, Hyderabad, operational since 2007.**

> 🌱 ⭐ **The conservation lesson, worth repeating because it is the strongest available argument for mangrove protection:** ⭐ **Coasts with intact MANGROVES and CORAL REEFS suffered measurably LESS damage in 2004 than cleared stretches nearby.** ⭐ **Ecological infrastructure outperformed engineered defences.**

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐ Tropical Cyclones

*(Mechanism, formation conditions, eye and eye wall: [Cl.11 Fundamentals Ch.10](../../Class_11_Fundamentals_of_Physical_Geography/Notes/10_Atmospheric_Circulation_and_Weather_Systems.md).)*

⭐ **Tropical cyclones are intense low pressure areas confined to the area lying between 30°N and 30°S latitudes.**

⭐ **In India, tropical cyclones originate in the BAY OF BENGAL and the ARABIAN SEA.**

### ⭐ The Indian pattern

| Fact | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Frequency** | ⭐ **The EAST COAST (Bay of Bengal) receives far more cyclones than the west coast (Arabian Sea)** — roughly four times as many |
| ⭐ **Most affected** | ⭐ **Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal** |
| ⭐ **Season** | ⭐ **Two peaks: pre-monsoon (April–May) and post-monsoon (October–December), the latter more severe** |
| ⭐ **Landfall** | ⭐ **The place where a tropical cyclone crosses the coast** |
| ⭐ **Storm surge** | ⭐ **The rise of sea level associated with a cyclone; the LARGEST killer** |

⭐ **Cyclone warning is one of the OLDEST activities of the INDIA METEOROLOGICAL DEPARTMENT (IMD).**

> 🌱 ⭐⭐ **The Odisha success story — the single best Indian example that disaster mortality is a policy variable:**
>
> ⭐ **The 1999 Odisha Super Cyclone killed approximately 10,000 people.**
> ⭐ **Cyclone Phailin in 2013, of comparable intensity, killed FEWER THAN 50** — because of early warning, cyclone shelters, and the evacuation of nearly a million people.
>
> ⭐ **Same hazard. Radically different outcome. The variable was preparedness.**
>
> 🎯 ⭐ **This pairs directly with the earthquake argument (buildings kill, not quakes) and makes a compelling two-example structure for any GS-III disaster answer.**

## 5️⃣ ⭐⭐ Floods

⭐ **Floods are a recurrent phenomenon in India.** ⭐ **Unlike other natural disasters like earthquakes and volcanoes, causes of floods are well established.**

⭐ **Floods are caused by INADEQUATE CAPACITY within the banks of the rivers to contain the high flow brought down from the upper catchment due to heavy rainfall.**

### ⭐ Causes

| Cause | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Heavy rainfall** | ⭐ **Especially during the monsoon; concentrated in a short period** |
| ⭐ **Silting of river beds** | ⭐ **Reduces the water-carrying capacity of the channel** |
| ⭐ **Deforestation in catchments** | ⭐ **Increases run-off and reduces infiltration** |
| ⭐ **Landslides blocking flow** | ⭐ **Temporary damming and sudden release** |
| ⭐ **Cyclones and storm surges** | ⭐ **Coastal flooding** |
| ⭐ **Encroachment on flood plains** | ⭐ **Construction on the river's natural overflow area** |
| ⭐ **Faulty drainage in urban areas** | ⭐ **Urban flooding — Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad** |

### ⭐ Flood-prone areas

⭐ **The flood-prone areas are: the plains of ⭐ BIHAR, UTTAR PRADESH, WEST BENGAL, ASSAM, PUNJAB, HARYANA, coastal ODISHA and ANDHRA PRADESH, and parts of GUJARAT.**

⭐ **About 40 million hectares of land in the country is liable to floods.**

⭐ **The Brahmaputra, Ganga and Indus systems account for most Indian flooding.**

> ⭐⭐ **The flood paradox worth stating in a Mains answer:** ⭐ **The Ganga–Brahmaputra floods kill people and destroy property every year — and they are ALSO the reason 400+ million people can be fed there.** ⭐ **The annual flood deposits the silt that makes the plain among the most fertile on Earth.**
>
> ⭐ **Flood control that eliminated flooding entirely would eliminate the silt deposition too.** ⭐ **This tension underlies embankment policy, the Farakka Barrage debate, and India–Bangladesh water sharing.**
>
> 🌱 ⭐ **And note the perverse effect of embankments:** they confine the river, so silt is deposited **within the channel** rather than spread over the plain, **raising the river bed year by year** until it flows above the surrounding land. **The embankment that prevents small floods makes the eventual breach catastrophic.** **The Kosi is the textbook case.**

### ⭐ Flood management

⭐ **Construction of flood protection embankments in the flood-prone areas, construction of dams, afforestation and discouraging major construction activities in the upper reaches of most of the flood-creating rivers, etc.**

⭐ **In the Indian context, the FLOOD FORECASTING AND WARNING is done by the CENTRAL WATER COMMISSION (CWC).**

## 6️⃣ ⭐⭐ Droughts

⭐ **Drought is a complex phenomenon as it involves elements of meteorology like precipitation, evaporation, evapotranspiration, ground water, soil moisture, storage and surface run-off, agricultural practices, particularly the types of crops grown, socio-economic practices and ecological conditions.**

### ⭐⭐ Types of drought — the classification that gets asked

| Type | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **METEOROLOGICAL DROUGHT** | ⭐ **A situation when there is prolonged period of inadequate rainfall marked with mal-distribution of the same over time and space** |
| ⭐ **AGRICULTURAL DROUGHT** | ⭐ **Also known as SOIL MOISTURE DROUGHT, characterised by low soil moisture that is necessary to support the crops, thereby resulting in crop failures.** ⭐ **Moreover, if an area has more than 30 per cent of its gross cropped area under irrigation, the area is EXCLUDED from the drought-prone category** |
| ⭐ **HYDROLOGICAL DROUGHT** | ⭐ **Results when the availability of water in different storages and reservoirs like aquifers, lakes, reservoirs, etc. falls below what the precipitation can replenish** |
| ⭐ **ECOLOGICAL DROUGHT** | ⭐ **When the productivity of a natural ecosystem fails due to shortage of water and as a consequence of ecological distress, damages are induced in the ecosystem** |

> ⭐ **The 30% irrigation exclusion is a precise, examinable detail** — ⭐ **an area with more than 30% of its gross cropped area under irrigation is EXCLUDED from the drought-prone category.**

> ⭐⭐ **The four-type progression is worth understanding as a SEQUENCE, not a list:**
> ⭐ **METEOROLOGICAL drought comes first (rain fails) → AGRICULTURAL drought follows (soil moisture runs out, crops fail) → HYDROLOGICAL drought follows that (reservoirs and aquifers fall) → ECOLOGICAL drought is the deepest stage (the ecosystem itself fails).**
> ⭐ **Each stage takes longer to arrive and longer to recover from.** ⭐ **A single failed monsoon causes meteorological and agricultural drought; repeated failures cause hydrological and ecological drought, which can take years to reverse.**

### ⭐ Drought-prone areas of India

| Category | ⭐ Where |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **EXTREME DROUGHT AFFECTED AREAS** | ⭐ **Most parts of RAJASTHAN, particularly areas to the west of the Aravali hills, i.e. Marusthali and Kachchh regions of Gujarat.** ⭐ **Included here are also the districts like Jaisalmer and Barmer from the Indian desert that receive less than 90 mm average annual rainfall** |
| ⭐ **SEVERE DROUGHT PRONE AREA** | ⭐ **Parts of eastern Rajasthan, most parts of Madhya Pradesh, eastern parts of Maharashtra, interior parts of Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka Plateau, northern parts of interior Tamil Nadu and southern parts of Jharkhand and interior Odisha** |
| ⭐ **MODERATE DROUGHT AFFECTED AREA** | ⭐ **Northern parts of Rajasthan, Haryana, southern districts of Uttar Pradesh, the remaining parts of Gujarat, Maharashtra except Konkan, Jharkhand and Coimbatore plateau of Tamil Nadu and interior Karnataka** |

⚠️ ⭐ **The rest of India can be considered either free or less prone to drought.**

> ⭐ **Recall the variability paradox from Ch.4:** ⭐ **the areas with the LEAST rainfall also have the HIGHEST rainfall variability (>50% C.V.).** ⭐ **So the drought-prone regions cannot rely even on the little they normally receive.** **This is why drought geography and rainfall variability geography coincide almost exactly.**

### 🌱 Consequences and management

⭐ **Consequences: crop failure, food scarcity, loss of livestock, unemployment, distress migration, health impacts, and long-term land degradation and desertification.**

⭐ **Management:** ⭐ **rainwater harvesting, watershed development, drought-resistant crops (millets), micro-irrigation, crop insurance, employment guarantee (MGNREGA), and interlinking/water transfer proposals.**

> 🌾 ⭐ **Millets are the crop-level answer to drought** (Cl.10 Ch.4): drought-hardy, tolerant of poor soil, nutritious, and far less water-demanding than paddy. ⭐ **Promoting them in drought-prone regions is simultaneously a climate-adaptation, nutrition and groundwater strategy.**

## 7️⃣ ⭐⭐ Landslides

⭐ **Landslides are relatively small and localised events compared to the other natural hazards.**

⭐ **The landslides occur mainly in the HIMALAYAS and the WESTERN GHATS.**

### ⭐ India's landslide zonation

| Zone | ⭐ Where |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **VERY HIGH VULNERABILITY ZONE** | ⭐ **Highly unstable, relatively young mountainous areas in the Himalayas and Andaman and Nicobar, high rainfall regions with steep slopes in the Western Ghats and Nilgiris, the north-eastern regions, along with areas that experience frequent ground-shaking due to earthquakes** |
| ⭐ **HIGH VULNERABILITY ZONE** | ⭐ **Areas that have almost similar conditions to those included in the very high vulnerability zone are also included in this category.** ⭐ **The only difference between these two is the combination, intensity and frequency of the controlling factors.** ⭐ **All the Himalayan states and the states from the north-eastern regions except the plains of Assam are included in the high vulnerability zones** |
| ⭐ **MODERATE TO LOW VULNERABILITY ZONE** | ⭐ **Areas that receive less precipitation such as Trans-Himalayan areas of Ladakh and Spiti (Himachal Pradesh), undulated yet stable relief and low precipitation areas in the Aravali, rain shadow areas in the Western and Eastern Ghats and Deccan plateau also experience occasional landslides.** ⭐ **Landslides due to mining and subsidence are most common in states like Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Goa** |
| ⭐ **OTHER AREAS** | ⭐ **The remaining parts of India, particularly states like Rajasthan, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal (except district Darjeeling), Assam (except district Karbi Anglong) and Coastal regions of the southern States are safe as far as landslides are concerned** |

### 🌱 ⭐ Why Himalayan landslides are worsening

| Cause | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Geology** | ⭐ **The Shiwaliks are made of UNCONSOLIDATED SEDIMENTS** (Ch.2) on steep slopes |
| ⭐ **Seismicity** | ⭐ **A young, actively rising, seismically violent range** |
| ⭐ **Heavy rainfall** | ⭐ **Monsoon downpours saturate and lubricate slope material** |
| 🌱 ⭐ **HUMAN ACTIVITY** | ⭐ **Road-cutting removing support from below, unplanned construction, hydroelectric projects, deforestation, mining, machinery vibration, reservoir drawdown** |

> 🌱 ⭐⭐ **The point that lifts a landslide answer above description** — and it comes straight from NCERT's own list of activating causes (Cl.11 Fundamentals Ch.6):
>
> ⭐ **Most of the "activating causes" of mass movement are HUMAN: removal of support from below, overloading by artificial filling, machinery vibration, heavy drawdown of reservoirs, and indiscriminate removal of natural vegetation.**
>
> ⭐ **So Himalayan landslide risk is being MANUFACTURED by road-cutting, hydro projects and unregulated construction — not merely inherited from geology.**
>
> ⭐ **Joshimath's subsidence, Uttarakhand's flash floods and the Char Dham road controversy are the contemporary cases.**

### ⭐ Mitigation

⭐ **Restriction on construction and other developmental activities such as roads and dams, limiting agriculture to valleys and areas with moderate slopes, and control on development of large settlements in the high vulnerability zones.**

⭐ **Promoting large-scale afforestation programmes and construction of bunds to reduce the flow of water.**

⭐ **Terrace farming should be encouraged in the north-eastern hill states where JHUMMING (Slash and Burn/Shifting Cultivation) is still prevalent.**

## 8️⃣ 🌱 India's Disaster Management Framework

| Institution / instrument | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Disaster Management Act, 2005** | ⭐ **The legal foundation** |
| ⭐ **NDMA** | ⭐ **National Disaster Management Authority — chaired by the PRIME MINISTER** |
| ⭐ **SDMA / DDMA** | ⭐ **State and District Disaster Management Authorities** |
| ⭐ **NDRF** | ⭐ **National Disaster Response Force — the specialised response force** |
| ⭐ **NIDM** | ⭐ **National Institute of Disaster Management — training and research** |
| ⭐ **IMD** | ⭐ **Cyclone and weather warning** |
| ⭐ **CWC** | ⭐ **Flood forecasting** |
| ⭐ **INCOIS / ITEWC** | ⭐ **Tsunami early warning, Hyderabad** |
| ⭐ **Sendai Framework 2015–2030** | ⭐ **The international framework for disaster risk reduction; India is a signatory** |

> 🌱 ⭐ **The doctrinal shift worth naming in a Mains answer:** ⭐ **Indian disaster policy has moved from a RELIEF-CENTRIC approach (respond after the event) to a PREVENTION, MITIGATION AND PREPAREDNESS approach (reduce risk before it).** ⭐ **The Disaster Management Act 2005 embodies that shift, and the Sendai Framework reinforces it.**
>
> 🎯 ⭐ **The Sendai Framework's four priorities are worth knowing: (1) understanding disaster risk; (2) strengthening disaster risk governance; (3) investing in disaster risk reduction for resilience; (4) enhancing disaster preparedness for effective response and "Build Back Better."**

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. ⭐⭐ **HAZARD × VULNERABILITY = DISASTER.** The hazard is natural; **the disaster is a policy outcome.**
2. ⭐ **Four classes: atmospheric, terrestrial, aquatic, biological.**
3. ⭐ **Seismic Zone V: entire NE, parts of J&K/HP/Uttarakhand, Rann of Kachchh, north Bihar, Andaman & Nicobar.** ⚠️ **DELHI is in Zone IV.**
4. ⭐ **Earthquakes don't kill people, buildings do** — so the answer is **code enforcement, not prediction.** **Soil liquefaction** means codes must address soil type too.
5. ⭐ **2004 tsunami (26 Dec) → ITEWC at INCOIS Hyderabad, 2007.** 🌱 **Intact mangroves measurably reduced damage.**
6. ⭐ **East coast gets ~4× the cyclones of the west. Storm surge is the biggest killer. IMD issues warnings.**
7. ⭐⭐ **Odisha 1999: ~10,000 deaths. Phailin 2013: fewer than 50.** **Same hazard, different preparedness.**
8. ⭐ **~40 million hectares are flood-prone.** ⭐ **CWC does flood forecasting.** ⭐ **The flood paradox: the flood that destroys is also the flood that fertilises.** **Embankments raise river beds and make breaches catastrophic (Kosi).**
9. ⭐⭐ **Four drought types as a SEQUENCE: meteorological → agricultural (soil moisture) → hydrological → ecological.** ⭐ **>30% irrigated gross cropped area = excluded from drought-prone.**
10. ⭐ **Extreme drought: western Rajasthan (Jaisalmer, Barmer, <90 mm) and Kachchh.** **Drought geography matches rainfall-variability geography exactly.**
11. ⭐ **Landslides: Himalayas, Western Ghats, Nilgiris, NE, A&N.** 🌱 ⭐ **Most activating causes are HUMAN — Himalayan landslide risk is being manufactured, not merely inherited.**
12. ⭐ **Disaster Management Act 2005; NDMA chaired by the PM; NDRF; NIDM; Sendai Framework 2015–2030.** ⭐ **The doctrinal shift: from RELIEF-CENTRIC to PREVENTION AND PREPAREDNESS.**
