## 1️⃣ ⭐ The Hydrological Cycle

⭐ **Water is a cyclic resource.** ⭐ **It can be used and re-used.** ⭐ **Water also undergoes a cycle from the ocean to land and land to ocean.**

⭐ **The HYDROLOGICAL CYCLE describes the movement of water on, in, and above the earth.** ⭐ **The water cycle has been working for billions of years and all life on earth depends on it continuing to work.**

⭐ **About 71% of the planetary water is found in the oceans.** ⭐ **The remaining is held as freshwater in glaciers and icecaps, groundwater sources, lakes, soil moisture, atmosphere, streams and within life.**

⭐ **Nearly 59% of the water that falls on land returns to the atmosphere through evaporation from over the oceans as well as from other places.** ⭐ **The remainder runs-off on the surface, infiltrates into the ground or a part of it becomes glacier.**

## 2️⃣ ⭐⭐ Relief of the Ocean Floor

⭐ **The oceans are confined to the great depressions of the earth's outer layer.** ⭐ **The ocean floors can be divided into four major divisions:**

```
   ⭐⭐ THE FOUR MAJOR DIVISIONS

   COAST
    │  ⭐ 1. CONTINENTAL SHELF
    │     ⭐ Average width 80 km; average depth <200 m
    │     ⭐ Average GRADIENT 1° or even less
    │     ⭐ Ends in a steep slope — the SHELF BREAK
    ├──────────────────────────────────
    │  ⭐ 2. CONTINENTAL SLOPE
    │     ⭐ 200 m to 3,000 m depth
    │     ⭐ Gradient 2°–5°
    │     ⭐ Marks the boundary between continental
    │        crust and oceanic crust
    │     ⭐ CANYONS and TRENCHES occur here
    ├──────────────────────────────────
    │  ⭐ 3. DEEP SEA PLAIN (Abyssal Plain)
    │     ⭐ 3,000 m to 6,000 m
    │     ⭐ Gently sloping areas
    │     ⭐ Covers nearly 40% of the ocean floor
    │     ⭐ Covered with fine-grained sediment
    │        like clay and silt
    ├──────────────────────────────────
    │  ⭐ 4. OCEANIC DEEPS / TRENCHES
    │     ⭐ The DEEPEST parts — relatively steep-sided,
    │        narrow basins
    │     ⭐ About 3–5 km deeper than the surrounding
    │        ocean floor
    │     ⭐ ~57 deeps known; 32 in the PACIFIC,
    │        19 in the ATLANTIC, 6 in the INDIAN Ocean
    │     ⭐ Associated with VOLCANIC ERUPTIONS and
    │        EARTHQUAKES — hence important in
    │        understanding plate movements
    ▼
```

| Division | ⭐ Key facts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **CONTINENTAL SHELF** | ⭐ **The extended margin of each continent occupied by relatively shallow seas and gulfs.** ⭐ **The average width is about 80 km.** ⭐ **The depth varies between 30 m and 600 m.** ⭐ **Shelves are almost absent or very narrow along some margins like the coasts of Chile, the west coast of Sumatra, etc.** ⭐ **On the contrary, the Siberian shelf in the Arctic Ocean, the largest in the world, stretches to 1,500 km in width.** ⭐ **Covered with variable thicknesses of sediments brought down by rivers, glaciers, wind, from the land and distributed by waves and currents.** ⭐ **Massive sedimentary deposits received over a long time by the continental shelves, become the source of FOSSIL FUELS** |
| ⭐ **CONTINENTAL SLOPE** | ⭐ **Connects the continental shelf and the ocean basins.** ⭐ **It begins where the bottom of the continental shelf sharply drops off into a steep slope.** ⭐ **The gradient of the slope region varies between 2–5°.** ⭐ **The depth of the slope region varies between 200 and 3,000 m.** ⭐ **The slope boundary indicates the end of the continents.** ⭐ **CANYONS and TRENCHES are observed in this region** |
| ⭐ **DEEP SEA PLAIN** | ⭐ **Gently sloping areas of the ocean basins.** ⭐ **These are the FLATTEST and SMOOTHEST regions of the world.** ⭐ **The depths vary between 3,000 and 6,000 m.** ⭐ **These plains are covered with fine-grained sediments like clay and silt** |
| ⭐ **OCEANIC DEEPS / TRENCHES** | ⭐ **The deepest parts of the oceans.** ⭐ **The trenches are relatively steep sided, narrow basins.** ⭐ **They are about 3–5 km deeper than the surrounding ocean floor.** ⭐ **They occur at the bases of continental slopes and along island arcs and are associated with active volcanoes and strong earthquakes.** ⭐ **That is why they are very significant in the study of plate movements.** ⭐ **As many as 57 deeps have been explored so far; of which 32 are in the Pacific Ocean; 19 in the Atlantic Ocean and 6 in the Indian Ocean** |

> ⭐⭐ **The continental shelf is the most economically important part of the ocean, and this is worth knowing for GS-III:**
> - ⭐ **It holds the world's oil and gas reserves** — thick sediment accumulated over geological time. **Mumbai High sits on India's shelf.**
> - ⭐ **It supports the richest fishing grounds** — shallow, sunlit water lets plankton photosynthesise.
> - ⭐ **It is only ~7.5% of the total ocean area** yet supplies nearly all marine resources.
>
> ⭐ **And it is the legal basis of the EEZ** — a country's rights over the seabed extend across its continental shelf under UNCLOS, which is why shelf extent is a matter of international dispute (South China Sea, Arctic).

⚠️ ⭐ **Note the trench distribution: 32 Pacific, 19 Atlantic, 6 Indian.** ⭐ **The Pacific dominates because it is ringed by subduction zones — the "Ring of Fire."** **Trenches are where oceanic crust is being consumed** (Ch.4), so their distribution maps plate boundaries directly.

### ⭐ Minor relief features

| Feature | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **MID-OCEANIC RIDGES** | ⭐ **A mid-oceanic ridge is composed of two chains of mountains separated by a large depression.** ⭐ **The mountain ranges can have peaks as high as 2,500 m and some even reach above the ocean's surface.** ⭐ **Iceland, a part of the mid-Atlantic Ridge, is an example** |
| ⭐ **SEAMOUNT** | ⭐ **A mountain with pointed summits, rising from the seafloor that does not reach the surface of the ocean.** ⭐ **Seamounts are volcanic in origin.** ⭐ **These can be 3,000–4,500 m tall.** ⭐ **The EMPEROR SEAMOUNT, off the Hawaiian Islands in the Pacific Ocean, is a good example** |
| ⭐ **SUBMARINE CANYONS** | ⭐ **These are deep valleys, some comparable to the Grand Canyon of the Colorado river.** ⭐ **They are sometimes found cutting across the continental shelves and slopes, often extending from the mouths of large rivers.** ⭐ **The HUDSON Canyon is the best known canyon in the world** |
| ⭐ **GUYOTS** | ⭐ **It is a FLAT TOPPED seamount.** ⭐ **They show evidences of gradual subsidence through stages to become flat topped submerged mountains.** ⭐ **It is estimated that more than 10,000 seamounts and guyots exist in the Pacific Ocean alone** |
| ⭐ **ATOLL** | ⭐ **These are low islands found in the tropical oceans consisting of coral reefs surrounding a central depression.** ⭐ **It may be a part of the sea (lagoon), or sometimes form enclosing a body of fresh, brackish, or highly saline water** |

> ⭐ **Seamount vs guyot is a classic pairing: ⭐ SEAMOUNT has a POINTED summit; ⭐ GUYOT is FLAT-TOPPED.**
>
> ⭐ **And the reason a guyot is flat is genuinely interesting:** it was once a **volcanic island above sea level**, whose top was **eroded flat by waves**. ⭐ **Then the seafloor beneath it subsided, carrying the flattened top below the surface.** **A guyot is a drowned, beheaded volcano** — and its flat top records the sea level of a vanished era.

## 3️⃣ ⭐⭐ Temperature of Ocean Waters

⭐ **The oceans absorb the sun's heat.** ⭐ **The temperature of ocean water varies from place to place and from the surface downward.**

### ⭐ Factors affecting temperature distribution

| Factor | ⭐ Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Latitude** | ⭐ **The temperature of surface water decreases from the equator towards the poles because the amount of insolation decreases poleward** |
| ⭐ **Unequal distribution of land and water** | ⭐ **The oceans in the northern hemisphere receive more heat due to their contact with larger extent of land than the oceans in the southern hemisphere** |
| ⭐ **Prevailing wind** | ⭐ **The winds blowing from the land towards the oceans drive warm surface water away from the coast resulting in the upwelling of cold water from below. It results into the longitudinal variation in the temperature.** ⭐ **Contrary to this, the onshore winds pile up warm water near the coast and this raises the temperature** |
| ⭐ **Ocean currents** | ⭐ **Warm ocean currents raise the temperature in cold areas while the cold currents decrease the temperature in warm ocean areas.** ⭐ **Gulf stream (warm current) raises the temperature near the eastern coast of North America and the West Coast of Europe while the Labrador current (cold current) lowers the temperature near the north-east coast of North America** |

⭐ **All these factors influence the temperature of the ocean water locally.**

### ⭐⭐ Horizontal and vertical distribution

⭐ **The average temperature of surface water of the oceans is about 27°C and it gradually decreases from the equator towards the poles.**

⭐ **The rate of decrease of temperature with increasing latitude is generally 0.5°C per latitude.**

⭐ **The average temperature is around 22°C at 20° latitudes, 14°C at 40° latitudes and 0°C near poles.**

⚠️ ⭐ **The oceans in the northern hemisphere record relatively higher temperature than in the southern hemisphere.** ⭐ **The highest temperature is not recorded at the equator but slightly towards north of it.**

⭐ **The vertical temperature profile shows how the temperature decreases from the surface towards the bottom of the ocean.**

⭐ **There is a boundary region between the surface waters of the ocean and the deeper layers.** ⭐ **The boundary usually begins around 100–400 m below the sea surface and extends several hundred of metres downward.** ⭐ **This boundary region, from where there is a rapid decrease of temperature, is called the THERMOCLINE.**

⭐ **About 90 per cent of the total volume of water is found below the thermocline in the deep ocean.** ⭐ **In this zone, temperatures approach 0°C.**

```
   ⭐⭐ THE THREE-LAYER VERTICAL STRUCTURE

   0 m    ─────────────────────────────────
          ⭐ 1. SURFACE / MIXED LAYER
             ⭐ 500 m thick; 20°–25°C
             ⭐ Warm, mixed by waves and currents
             ⭐ Only ~2% of ocean volume
   ~100-400m ────────────────────────────────
          ⭐ 2. THERMOCLINE
             ⭐ ⭐ RAPID temperature DECREASE
             ⭐ Extends several hundred metres
   ────────────────────────────────────────
          ⭐ 3. DEEP ZONE
             ⭐ ⭐ ~90% of total ocean volume
             ⭐ Temperature approaches 0°C
             ⭐ Cold, dense, dark, still
   Bottom ─────────────────────────────────
```

> ⭐⭐ **The 90% figure is the striking one.** ⭐ **Nine-tenths of the ocean by volume lies below the thermocline, at close to 0°C, in permanent darkness.** ⭐ **The warm, sunlit ocean we picture is a thin skin on top.**
>
> ⭐ **Why the thermocline matters: it is a DENSITY BARRIER.** ⭐ **Cold water below is denser than warm water above, so the two layers do not readily mix.** 🌱 ⭐ **This is precisely why nutrient upwelling matters so much** — nutrients sink to the deep and are trapped there, and only where upwelling breaks through the thermocline (Peru, Benguela, Somali coast) do they return to the sunlit zone. ⭐ **Those upwelling zones are the world's greatest fisheries** (Ch.14).

> ⚠️ ⭐ **"The highest temperature is not recorded at the equator but slightly north of it" is a favourite trap.** The reason is the **unequal land–water distribution** — the northern hemisphere has more land, which heats the adjacent oceans more.

## 4️⃣ ⭐⭐ Salinity of Ocean Waters

> ⭐ **SALINITY is the term used to define the total content of dissolved salts in sea water.** ⭐ **It is calculated as the amount of salt (in gm) dissolved in 1,000 gm (1 kg) of seawater.** ⭐ **It is usually expressed as parts per thousand (‰) or ppt.**

⭐ **Salinity is an important factor in determining several characteristics of sea water such as compressibility, thermal expansion, temperature, density, absorption of insolation, evaporation and humidity.** ⭐ **It also influences the composition and movements of the sea-water and the distribution of fish and other marine resources.**

### ⭐ Factors affecting salinity

| Factor | ⭐ Effect |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Evaporation** | ⭐ **Salinity is directly proportional to evaporation** — more evaporation, more salinity |
| ⭐ **Precipitation** | ⭐ **Salinity is inversely proportional to precipitation** — more rain, less salinity |
| ⭐ **Freshwater flow from rivers** | ⭐ **Reduces salinity near river mouths** |
| ⭐ **Ice freezing and thawing** | ⭐ **Freezing increases salinity (salt is excluded from ice); melting decreases it** |
| ⭐ **Wind and ocean currents** | ⭐ **Transfer water and mix salinity, affecting its distribution** |

⭐ **The salinity of water in the surface layer of oceans depend mainly on evaporation and precipitation.** ⭐ **Surface salinity is greatly influenced in coastal regions by the fresh water flow from rivers, and in polar regions by the processes of freezing and thawing of ice.**

### ⭐⭐ Distribution — the key figures

⭐ **The salinity for normal open ocean ranges between 33‰ and 37‰.**

| Water body | ⭐ Salinity |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Normal open ocean** | ⭐ **33–37‰** |
| ⭐ **Landlocked RED SEA** | ⭐ **41‰** |
| ⭐ **Estuaries and the Arctic** | ⭐ **Salinity fluctuates from 0–35‰, seasonally** |
| ⭐ **Hot and dry regions, high evaporation** | ⭐ **Salinity may reach 70‰** |
| ⭐ **LAKE VAN (Turkey)** | ⭐ **330‰** |
| ⭐ **DEAD SEA** | ⭐ **238‰** |
| ⭐ **GREAT SALT LAKE** | ⭐ **220‰** |
| ⭐ **Pacific Ocean** | ⭐ **Salinity varies because of its shape and larger areal extent** |
| ⭐ **Atlantic Ocean** | ⭐ **Average salinity around 36‰** |
| ⭐ **Indian Ocean** | ⭐ **Average salinity 35‰.** ⭐ **The low salinity trend is observed in the Bay of Bengal due to influx of river water; on the contrary, the Arabian Sea shows higher salinity due to high evaporation and low influx of fresh water** |

⭐ **The highest salinity is recorded between 15° and 20° latitudes.** ⭐ **Maximum salinity (37‰) is observed between 20° N and 30° N and 20° W — it gradually decreases towards the north.**

⚠️ ⭐ **The North Sea, in spite of its location in higher latitudes, records higher salinity due to more saline water brought by the North Atlantic Drift.** ⭐ **Baltic Sea records low salinity due to influx of river waters in large quantity.** ⭐ **Mediterranean Sea records higher salinity due to high evaporation.**

### ⭐ Vertical distribution — the halocline

⭐ **Salinity changes with depth, but the way it changes depends upon the location of the sea.** ⭐ **Salinity at the surface increases by the loss of water to ice or evaporation, or decreased by the input of fresh waters, such as from the rivers.**

⭐ **Salinity at depth is very much fixed, because there is no way that water is "lost", or the salt is "added."** ⭐ **There is a marked difference in the salinity between the surface zones and the deep zones of the oceans.**

⭐ **The lower salinity water rests above the higher salinity dense water.** ⭐ **Salinity, generally, increases with depth and there is a distinct zone called the HALOCLINE, where salinity increases sharply.**

⭐ **Other factors being constant, increasing salinity of seawater causes its density to increase.** ⭐ **High salinity seawater, generally, sinks below the lower salinity water. This leads to stratification by salinity.**

> ⭐⭐ **The Bay of Bengal vs Arabian Sea contrast is a near-certain Prelims item, and the reason is clean:**
> ⭐ **BAY OF BENGAL — LOWER salinity**, because the Ganga, Brahmaputra, Mahanadi, Godavari and Krishna pour enormous volumes of fresh water into it.
> ⭐ **ARABIAN SEA — HIGHER salinity**, because evaporation is high and freshwater inflow is comparatively small.
>
> ⭐ **Same ocean, adjacent basins, opposite salinity — decided entirely by river discharge.**

> ⭐ **And note the counter-intuitive cases NCERT flags:** ⭐ **the NORTH SEA has high salinity despite its high latitude** (the North Atlantic Drift brings saline water), and ⭐ **the BALTIC has low salinity despite being at a similar latitude** (heavy river influx). ⭐ **Latitude alone does not determine salinity — the water's source does.**

> ⭐ **Two words to keep straight: THERMOCLINE (rapid TEMPERATURE change) and HALOCLINE (rapid SALINITY change).** ⭐ **Both are density barriers, and together they create the ocean's layered structure — a phenomenon called PYCNOCLINE stratification.**

## 🔁 60-Second Recap

1. ⭐ **~71% of planetary water is in the oceans.**
2. ⭐ **Four divisions: continental shelf (avg 80 km wide, 30–600 m, gradient ≤1°) · slope (200–3,000 m, 2–5°, marks the continent's end) · deep sea plain (3,000–6,000 m, flattest on Earth) · trenches (3–5 km deeper than surroundings).**
3. ⭐ **Siberian shelf is the world's largest (1,500 km).** ⭐ **Shelves hold the world's FOSSIL FUELS and richest fisheries** — and are the legal basis of the EEZ.
4. ⭐ **57 trenches known: 32 Pacific, 19 Atlantic, 6 Indian** — reflecting where subduction occurs.
5. ⭐ **Minor features: mid-oceanic ridge (Iceland) · seamount (pointed, Emperor) · GUYOT (flat-topped, a drowned wave-planed volcano) · submarine canyon (Hudson) · atoll.**
6. ⭐ **Surface temperature averages 27°C, falling 0.5°C per latitude — 22°C at 20°, 14°C at 40°, 0°C at the poles.**
7. ⚠️ ⭐ **The highest temperature is slightly NORTH of the equator**, because of unequal land–water distribution.
8. ⭐⭐ **THERMOCLINE begins 100–400 m down; ~90% of ocean volume lies below it, near 0°C.** ⭐ **It is a density barrier — which is why upwelling zones are the great fisheries.**
9. ⭐ **Salinity: normal ocean 33–37‰. Red Sea 41 · Great Salt Lake 220 · Dead Sea 238 · Lake Van 330.**
10. ⭐ **Highest salinity between 15° and 20° latitude; maximum 37‰ around 20–30°N.**
11. ⭐ **Bay of Bengal LOW (river influx); Arabian Sea HIGH (evaporation).** ⭐ **North Sea high despite latitude (N. Atlantic Drift); Baltic low (rivers).**
12. ⭐ **Salinity generally INCREASES with depth; the sharp zone is the HALOCLINE.** ⭐ **Thermocline = temperature, halocline = salinity — both are density barriers.**
