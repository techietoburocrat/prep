# Chapter 7 — Fundamental Rights

[← Chapter 6](06_Citizenship.md) · [Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 8 →](08_DPSP.md)

**Read time ~25 min · split this over 3 sittings** · Part III, Articles 12–35
**The single biggest chapter in Laxmikanth.** Prelims: 2–4 questions every year. Mains: appears every year in some form.

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **Fundamental Rights are the limits you place on your own government.** They are called "fundamental" for two reasons: they are guaranteed by the *Constitution* (not an ordinary law), and they are **justiciable** — you can walk into the Supreme Court directly under Article 32 and force the government to stop.

---

## 📖 The story

Think of the Constitution as a contract where citizens hire a government. Part III is the **clause that says what the employer may never do to the employee — and what the employee may never do to the employer.**

Most of the Constitution tells the government what it *can* do. Part III tells it what it **cannot**. That's the whole idea.

> 💡 **Ambedkar called Article 32 "the heart and soul of the Constitution."** Not Article 14, not 21 — Article 32. Because a right without a remedy is just a nice sentence. Article 32 is the *enforcement engine*.

---

## PART A — The scaffolding: Articles 12 and 13

### Article 12 — What is "the State"?

Fundamental Rights are mostly available **against the State**. So who counts?

- The **Government and Parliament of India**
- The **Government and Legislature of each State**
- **All local authorities** — municipalities, panchayats, district boards
- **All other authorities** — statutory or non-statutory bodies like LIC, ONGC, SAIL

> 💡 **Why this matters practically:** if a private company violates your equality, Article 14 generally does not apply. If a government-controlled corporation does, it does. Courts have widened "other authorities" to cover bodies that are financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the government.

> ⚠️ **The judiciary**, when performing its judicial functions, is generally **not** "State" — but it is when acting administratively.

### Article 13 — Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Any law that violates a Fundamental Right is **void** to the extent of the inconsistency. **This is the express provision for judicial review.**

Three doctrines flow from it:

| Doctrine | What it means | Simple analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Doctrine of Severability** | Only the *offending part* of the law is struck down, not the whole law | A rotten slice is cut off; the rest of the loaf is fine |
| **Doctrine of Eclipse** | A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with FR is not dead, only **dormant** — it revives if the FR is amended | A cloud covers the sun; remove the cloud and the sun shines again |
| **Doctrine of Waiver** | You **cannot** waive your Fundamental Rights, even voluntarily | You cannot sign away your own freedom |

> ⚠️ **Article 13(4)**, inserted by the **24th Amendment (1971)**, says a *constitutional amendment* is **not** a "law" under Article 13. This was the government's attempt to put amendments beyond judicial review — and it is precisely what **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** neutralised through the basic structure doctrine.

## PART B — The six rights

Originally there were **seven**. The **Right to Property** was deleted by the **44th Amendment, 1978**, and became a mere **legal right under Article 300A**.

> 🧠 **Mnemonic for the six: "E-F-E-R-C-C"**
> **E**quality (14–18) · **F**reedom (19–22) · **E**xploitation (23–24) · **R**eligion (25–28) · **C**ultural & Educational (29–30) · **C**onstitutional Remedies (32)

## RIGHT 1 — Equality (Articles 14–18)

### Article 14 — Equality before law & equal protection of laws

Two ideas, two origins:

| Concept | Origin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Equality before law** | **British** | *Negative* — no one is above the law; absence of special privilege |
| **Equal protection of laws** | **American** | *Positive* — like should be treated alike; equals treated equally |

> 💡 **The crucial insight:** equality does **not** mean treating everyone identically. It means **treating equals equally and unequals unequally.** A ramp for a wheelchair user isn't unequal treatment — it's what equality actually requires.

**The test of reasonable classification** — two conditions, and you must know both:
1. **Intelligible differentia** — the classification must be based on a real, understandable distinction
2. **Rational nexus** — that distinction must have a reasonable connection to the object of the law

> 🔑 **This two-part test is the workhorse of Indian constitutional law.** Every reservation case, every CAA argument, every tax-slab challenge runs on it. Learn it word for word.

**Later evolution:** in *E.P. Royappa*, the Court added that **arbitrariness itself violates Article 14** — a law can fail even if it passes the classification test, if it is simply arbitrary.

> 🔑 [Prelims 2021 Q9](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2021.md) asked which Article is violated by "unguided and uncontrolled discretionary power." Answer: **Article 14**. That's the arbitrariness doctrine.

### Article 15 — Prohibition of discrimination

The State shall not discriminate on grounds **only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth**.

> 🧠 **Mnemonic: "R-R-C-S-P"** — *"Really Rude Children Slap Parents."*
> ⚠️ Note **"place of birth"**, not "place of residence." Residence-based rules are treated differently.

**The exceptions — these are the amendments to know:**

| Clause | Provides for | Added by |
|---|---|---|
| **15(3)** | Special provisions for **women and children** | Original |
| **15(4)** | Special provisions for **SEBC, SC and ST** | **1st Amendment, 1951** |
| **15(5)** | Reservation in **admission to educational institutions**, including private ones | **93rd Amendment, 2005** |
| **15(6)** | **EWS** (economically weaker sections) reservation | **103rd Amendment, 2019** |

> 💡 **The 1st Amendment story is worth knowing.** In *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) the Supreme Court struck down caste-based reservation in Madras as violating Article 15. Parliament responded within months with the **1st Amendment**, inserting Article 15(4). This is the **first ever conflict between Parliament and the judiciary over Fundamental Rights** — the opening move in a battle that runs all the way to *Kesavananda*.

### Article 16 — Equality of opportunity in public employment

| Clause | Provides for |
|---|---|
| **16(3)** | Parliament may prescribe **residence** requirements for certain state jobs |
| **16(4)** | Reservation for **backward classes** not adequately represented |
| **16(4A)** | Reservation in **promotion** for SC/ST — **77th Amendment, 1995** |
| **16(4B)** | **Carry forward** of unfilled vacancies — **81st Amendment, 2000** |
| **16(6)** | **EWS** reservation — **103rd Amendment, 2019** |

> 💡 **The Mandal case — *Indra Sawhney* (1992).** After the V.P. Singh government implemented the **Mandal Commission's** 27% OBC reservation, the Supreme Court laid down rules that still govern reservation:
> - **50% ceiling** on total reservation (except in extraordinary circumstances)
> - **Creamy layer** must be excluded from OBC benefits
> - **No reservation in promotions** — *Parliament overturned this within three years via the 77th Amendment, inserting 16(4A)*
> - No reservation on economic criteria alone — *and this too was effectively overtaken by the 103rd Amendment in 2019*
>
> 🔑 **Notice the pattern:** the Court sets a limit → Parliament amends around it. That tug-of-war *is* Indian constitutional history. Use this in any answer on judicial review vs parliamentary supremacy.

### Article 17 — Abolition of untouchability

Untouchability is **abolished** and its practice in any form **forbidden**. Enforcement is a punishable offence.

> ⚠️ **Article 17 is one of the very few Fundamental Rights available against PRIVATE individuals**, not just the State. So is Article 15(2), 23 and 24.

**Laws made under it:** the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** and the **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**.

> 🔑 [Prelims 2020 Q3](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2020.md) — untouchability falls under **Right to Equality**, not Right against Exploitation. A very common mix-up.

### Article 18 — Abolition of titles

The State shall not confer titles, except **military and academic distinctions**.

> ⚠️ **The classic trap:** are **Bharat Ratna and Padma awards** "titles" barred by Article 18?
> **No.** In *Balaji Raghavan* (1996) the Supreme Court held these are **awards, not titles** — but they cannot be used as prefixes or suffixes to a name.
>
> [Prelims 2021 Q5](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2021.md) tested exactly this. Answer: all three statements were **not correct**.

## RIGHT 2 — Freedom (Articles 19–22)

### Article 19 — The six freedoms

Originally **seven**; the right to property (19(1)(f)) was deleted by the **44th Amendment, 1978**.

| Freedom | Reasonable restrictions may be imposed on grounds of |
|---|---|
| **(a) Speech and expression** | Sovereignty & integrity, security of State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence |
| **(b) Assemble peaceably without arms** | Sovereignty & integrity, public order |
| **(c) Form associations, unions or co-operative societies** | Sovereignty & integrity, public order, morality |
| **(d) Move freely throughout the territory** | Interests of the general public, protection of Scheduled Tribes |
| **(e) Reside and settle in any part** | Interests of the general public, protection of Scheduled Tribes |
| **(g) Practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business** | Interests of the general public; State may prescribe professional qualifications or carry on any trade itself |

> 🔑 **"Co-operative societies" was added to 19(1)(c) by the 97th Amendment, 2011.**
> ⚠️ **19(1)(d) and (e) are available only to CITIZENS**, and are restricted for Scheduled Tribe areas — which is why the Sixth Schedule and inner-line permits are constitutional.
> [Prelims 2005 Q8](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2015.md) and [Mains 2022 GS-II Q2](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2022.md) both turn on this.

**What Article 19(1)(a) has been read to include:** freedom of the press, the right to information, the right to silence, the right to fly the national flag, and commercial advertising.

> 💡 **Real case — *Shreya Singhal* (2015).** Section 66A of the IT Act criminalised sending "grossly offensive" or "menacing" messages online. People were being arrested for Facebook posts. The Supreme Court struck it down as **vague, overbroad and violative of Article 19(1)(a)** — the vagueness meant nobody could know what was legal.
>
> 🔑 This was asked as [Mains 2013 GS-II Q2](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2013.md) — *two years before* the judgment came.

### Article 20 — Protection in respect of conviction

Three protections, available to **citizens and foreigners alike**:

1. **No ex-post-facto law** — you cannot be convicted for an act that wasn't an offence when you did it, nor given a heavier penalty than was applicable then
2. **No double jeopardy** — no prosecution and punishment for the same offence more than once
3. **No self-incrimination** — no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself

> ⚠️ **Article 20 (along with Article 21) CANNOT be suspended even during a National Emergency.** More on why below.

### Article 21 — Protection of life and personal liberty ⭐⭐

> *"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law."*

**This is the most litigated, most expanded Article in the Constitution.** Its story is the story of Indian democracy.

| Case | Year | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| **A.K. Gopalan** | 1950 | **Narrow reading.** "Procedure established by law" means *any* procedure a legislature enacts. If Parliament passes it, it's valid |
| ***ADM Jabalpur*** *(Habeas Corpus case)* | **1976** | **The darkest judgment.** During the **Emergency**, the Court held that even the **right to life** could be suspended, and a detainee had no remedy. Justice H.R. Khanna's lone dissent cost him the Chief Justiceship |
| ***Maneka Gandhi*** | **1978** | **The transformation.** The procedure must be **"fair, just and reasonable"** — not arbitrary. Effectively read American **due process** into an Article that had deliberately borrowed the Japanese phrase |
| ***Puttaswamy*** | **2017** | **Right to privacy** is intrinsic to Article 21. Also **expressly overruled *ADM Jabalpur*** |

> 💡 **The Maneka Gandhi story:** the government impounded her passport without giving reasons. She challenged it. The Court could have said "there's a law permitting it, so it's valid" — the *Gopalan* answer. Instead it said a law is not enough; **the procedure itself must be fair.** That one shift converted Article 21 from a narrow protection into the engine of Indian rights jurisprudence.

**Rights read INTO Article 21 over time:**
Livelihood · clean environment · health · education · **privacy** · dignity · speedy trial · free legal aid · shelter · sleep · fair trial · travel abroad · **die with dignity** (*Common Cause*, 2018, permitting passive euthanasia and living wills) · marry a person of one's choice

> 🔑 [Prelims 2019 Q5](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2019.md) — right to marry a person of one's choice → **Article 21**.
> [Prelims 2012](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2013.md), [2018 Q7](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2018.md), [2021 Q17](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2021.md) all test Article 21.
> Mains: [2023 GS-II Q11](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2023.md) and [2024 GS-II Q12](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2024.md).

### Article 21A — Right to Education

Free and compulsory education for all children aged **6 to 14**. Added by the **86th Amendment, 2002**; implemented via the **Right to Education Act, 2009**.

> 💡 Before the amendment this was a Directive Principle (Article 45). **This is the clearest example of a DPSP being promoted into a Fundamental Right** — a perfect illustration for Chapter 8.

### Article 22 — Protection against arrest and detention

Two halves, and candidates constantly confuse them.

| **Ordinary arrest** (punitive) | **Preventive detention** |
|---|---|
| Right to be **informed of grounds** of arrest | Detention **without trial**, to prevent a future offence |
| Right to **consult and be defended by a lawyer** | Detention beyond **3 months** requires an **Advisory Board** of High Court judges |
| Must be **produced before a magistrate within 24 hours** | Grounds must be communicated, but facts against the public interest may be withheld |
| Not available to enemy aliens or those under preventive detention | Detainee gets the earliest opportunity to make a representation |

> ⚠️ **India is one of very few democracies with preventive detention written into the Constitution itself, applicable in peacetime.** That is a genuine criticism worth stating in a Mains answer.

## RIGHT 3 — Against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

**Article 23** — Prohibits **traffic in human beings**, **begar** (forced labour) and other similar forms of forced labour. Available against **private individuals** too. Exception: the State may impose compulsory service for public purposes (like military conscription), but without discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste or class.

**Article 24** — No child below **14 years** shall be employed in any **factory, mine or other hazardous employment**.

> ⚠️ **Note the age asymmetry:** Article 24 bars work below **14**, and Article 21A guarantees education from **6 to 14**. They were designed to fit together.
>
> 🔑 [Prelims 2017 Q13](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2017.md) — the Right against Exploitation covers **trafficking/forced labour and child labour** (Articles 23, 24), **not** untouchability (that's Article 17, Right to Equality) and **not** minority interests (Articles 29–30).

## RIGHT 4 — Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

| Article | What it guarantees |
|---|---|
| **25** | Freedom of **conscience** and the right freely to **profess, practise and propagate** religion |
| **26** | Freedom of every religious denomination to **manage its own religious affairs** |
| **27** | **No person shall be compelled to pay taxes** for the promotion of any particular religion |
| **28** | **No religious instruction** in educational institutions **wholly maintained out of State funds** |

**The two explanations under Article 25 that get tested:**
- Wearing and carrying **kirpans** is deemed included in the profession of the **Sikh** religion
- Reference to Hindus includes **Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists**

> 💡 **Article 25(2)(b) is the heart of Indian secularism.** It empowers the State to make laws for **social welfare and reform**, and for **throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus.**
>
> 🔑 **This is why the Indian state can reform religion while a Western secular state cannot.** Temple-entry laws, the abolition of untouchability in worship, the banning of triple talaq — all sit here. When you write about "principled distance" in a Mains answer, **Article 25(2)(b) is your evidence.**

> ⚠️ **28 has four situations — know the distinction:**
> | Institution type | Religious instruction |
> |---|---|
> | **Wholly** State-funded | **Completely prohibited** |
> | Administered by State but established under a **religious endowment/trust** | **Permitted** |
> | **Recognised** by the State | Permitted with **consent** |
> | Merely **aided** by the State | Permitted with **consent** |

## RIGHT 5 — Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

**Article 29** — Any section of citizens with a **distinct language, script or culture** has the right to conserve it. No citizen shall be denied admission to a State-maintained or State-aided institution on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste or language.

**Article 30** — All **minorities**, whether based on religion or language, have the right to **establish and administer educational institutions** of their choice. The State cannot discriminate against them in granting aid.

> ⚠️ **Article 29 speaks of "any section of citizens" — which can include the majority. Article 30 is exclusively for MINORITIES.** That asymmetry is a favourite examiner trap.

> 💡 ***T.M.A. Pai Foundation* (2002)** held that a "minority" for Article 30 is determined **State-wise**, not nationally. So Hindus can be a minority in a state like Mizoram or Punjab for these purposes.

## RIGHT 6 — Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

> **Ambedkar: *"If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important — an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity — I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it."***

Article 32 gives you the **right to move the Supreme Court directly** for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. **It is itself a Fundamental Right.**

### The five writs

| Writ | Literally | What it does | Against whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "To have the body" | Produce the detained person; release if detention is illegal | Both **public and private** |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Orders a public official to **perform** a duty they have failed to do | Public only — **not** against a private individual unless entrusted with a public duty |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Issued by a **higher court to a lower court** to **stop** exceeding jurisdiction — *preventive* | Judicial/quasi-judicial only |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | Higher court **transfers a case or quashes an order** of a lower court — *curative* | Judicial/quasi-judicial (and now administrative too) |
| **Quo Warranto** | "By what authority" | Asks a person **by what authority they hold a public office** | Public offices only |

> 🧠 **Memory hook:** *Habeas = body · Mandamus = do it · Prohibition = stop now (before) · Certiorari = undo it (after) · Quo Warranto = who are you?*
>
> 🔑 **Prohibition vs Certiorari is the most-asked pair.** Prohibition is issued **while** proceedings are pending (prevent); Certiorari **after** the order is made (cure).
>
> 🔑 [Prelims 2022 Q8](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2022.md) tested exactly this: Mandamus doesn't lie against a private body unless it has a public duty, and **any public-minded person** can seek Quo Warranto.

### Article 32 vs Article 226 — a guaranteed exam question

| | **Article 32** (Supreme Court) | **Article 226** (High Courts) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **A Fundamental Right** itself | A **constitutional right**, not a fundamental right |
| Purpose | **Only** for enforcement of Fundamental Rights | For Fundamental Rights **AND for any other legal right** |
| Scope | **Narrower** | **WIDER** |
| Discretion | SC **cannot refuse** to entertain | HC **may refuse** — it is discretionary |
| Territorial reach | All India | Within its own jurisdiction (plus where the cause of action arises) |

> ⚠️ **So the High Court's writ jurisdiction is WIDER than the Supreme Court's.** Counter-intuitive, and therefore frequently tested — including as [Prelims 2006 Q55](../UPSC_Prelims_Polity_MCQs/2015.md).

### Articles 33, 34, 35

- **Article 33** — Parliament may **restrict or abrogate** Fundamental Rights for members of the **armed forces, paramilitary, police and intelligence agencies**
- **Article 34** — restriction on Fundamental Rights while **martial law** is in force
- **Article 35** — only **Parliament**, not state legislatures, can make laws to give effect to certain Fundamental Rights (ensures uniformity)

## PART C — Suspension during Emergency ⭐

| | |
|---|---|
| **Article 358** | **Article 19 is automatically suspended** the moment a National Emergency is declared on grounds of **war or external aggression** *(not armed rebellion — a 44th Amendment change)* |
| **Article 359** | The President may suspend the **right to move courts** for enforcement of other Fundamental Rights |

> ⚠️⚠️ **The single most important exception:** under Article 359, **Articles 20 and 21 CAN NEVER be suspended**, even during a National Emergency.

> 💡 **Why this rule exists — the *ADM Jabalpur* scar.** During the 1975–77 Emergency, thousands were detained without trial. In *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) the Supreme Court held that during an Emergency a detainee had **no right even to approach a court to enforce the right to life**. The Attorney General argued that even if a policeman shot a detainee, there would be no remedy.
>
> When the Emergency ended, the **44th Amendment Act, 1978** was passed to make sure it could never happen again. It:
> - Put **Articles 20 and 21 permanently beyond suspension**
> - Changed the ground for Emergency from "internal disturbance" to **"armed rebellion"**
> - Required Emergency to be declared only on the **written advice of the Cabinet**
> - Deleted the **Right to Property** from Fundamental Rights
>
> 🔑 **This is the single best real-world illustration in all of Part III.** A judgment failed the country; a constitutional amendment fixed it; forty-one years later *Puttaswamy* (2017) formally overruled the judgment. Use this story in any answer on emergency, rights, or judicial accountability.

## PART D — Criticisms of Fundamental Rights

| Criticism | Substance |
|---|---|
| **Excessive limitations** | Too many "reasonable restrictions" — critics call it a right given with one hand and taken with the other |
| **No social and economic rights** | Right to work, social security, health are in the DPSP, not Part III |
| **No clarity** | Terms like "public order", "morality", "reasonable restrictions" are vague and judge-dependent |
| **No permanency** | Parliament can curtail or abridge them by amendment (limited now by basic structure) |
| **Suspension during Emergency** | Except Articles 20 and 21 |
| **Expensive remedy** | Enforcement requires litigation — costly and slow. PIL was the judiciary's answer to this |
| **Preventive detention** | Constitutionally sanctioned detention without trial in peacetime is rare among democracies |

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "The transformation of Article 21 from *A.K. Gopalan* to *Puttaswamy* is the story of Indian constitutionalism itself." Discuss. (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* (i) *Gopalan* 1950 — literal reading, procedure = any enacted procedure. (ii) *ADM Jabalpur* 1976 — the nadir; rights suspendable, Khanna's dissent. (iii) *Maneka Gandhi* 1978 — procedure must be fair, just and reasonable; due process read in. (iv) Expansion — livelihood, environment, education, dignity, privacy, death with dignity. (v) *Puttaswamy* 2017 overrules *ADM Jabalpur*. (vi) Conclude: the Court's own correction of its worst moment shows constitutional self-repair.

**2. "Reservation jurisprudence in India has been a continuous dialogue between Parliament and the Supreme Court." Examine. (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* *Champakam* 1951 → 1st Amendment (15(4)). *Indra Sawhney* 1992 → 50% cap, creamy layer, no promotion quota → 77th Amendment (16(4A)), 81st (16(4B)). 103rd Amendment 2019 (EWS) crossing the 50% logic. Argue this is dialogue, not conflict — each corrects the other, bounded by basic structure.

**3. Examine the scope of Fundamental Rights in the light of the Supreme Court's judgement on the Right to Privacy. (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2017 GS-II Q12](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2017.md); revisited in [2024 GS-II Q12](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2024.md).*
> *Skeleton:* *Puttaswamy* — privacy intrinsic to Art 21 and Part III freedoms. Three-fold test: legality, legitimate aim, proportionality. Consequences — Aadhaar, decriminalisation of Section 377 (*Navtej Johar*), data protection law, DNA testing. Note the overruling of *ADM Jabalpur*.

**4. "Article 32 is the heart and soul of the Constitution." Discuss with reference to the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts. (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* Ambedkar's quote and why remedy defines right. The five writs with their distinct functions. Article 32 vs 226 — the HC's wider scope. PIL as the democratisation of Article 32. Criticism — cost, delay, judicial overreach concerns. Conclude on remedy as the test of a right's reality.

**5. "Indian secularism empowers the State to reform religion; Western secularism forbids it." Examine with constitutional provisions. (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* Articles 25–28, and specifically **25(2)(b)**. Examples — Article 17, temple entry, triple talaq, Sabarimala. Contrast with the US First Amendment and French *laïcité*. Argue the Indian model was necessary because religion here structures social hierarchy. Note criticism of selective interference. Links to [Mains 2019 Q5](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2019.md) and [2024 Q15](../UPSC_Mains_Polity_PYQs/2024.md).

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **Part III, Articles 12–35.** Six rights (**originally 7** — property deleted by **44th Amendment 1978** → Art 300A legal right)
- **Art 12** = State (govt, legislatures, local + "other authorities") · **Art 13** = judicial review; **severability, eclipse, no waiver**; 13(4) via 24th Amendment
- **Equality (14–18):** 14 = equality before law (British) + equal protection (American); **intelligible differentia + rational nexus**; arbitrariness (*Royappa*) · 15 = religion, race, caste, sex, **place of birth**; 15(4) 1st Amd, 15(5) 93rd, 15(6) 103rd · 16(4A) promotion 77th, 16(4B) 81st · **17 untouchability (also against private persons)** · 18 titles — **Bharat Ratna is an award, not a title**
- ***Indra Sawhney* 1992:** 50% cap, creamy layer, no promotion quota (overturned by 77th Amd)
- **Freedom (19–22):** six freedoms; **co-operative societies added by 97th Amd**; 19(1)(d)(e) citizens only, restricted for STs · **Art 20** ex-post-facto, double jeopardy, self-incrimination · **Art 21** *Gopalan* → *ADM Jabalpur* → ***Maneka Gandhi* 1978** → ***Puttaswamy* 2017** · **21A** education 6–14, **86th Amd 2002** · **Art 22** 24-hour rule; preventive detention 3 months + Advisory Board
- **Exploitation (23–24):** trafficking/begar; **no child under 14** in factory/mine/hazardous work
- **Religion (25–28):** **25(2)(b) = the reform power**; kirpan explanation; Art 28's four institution types
- **Cultural & Educational (29–30):** 29 = any section (can include majority); **30 = minorities only**; *T.M.A. Pai* — minority determined **State-wise**
- **Art 32** = "heart and soul"; five writs — **Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition (preventive), Certiorari (curative), Quo Warranto**. **Art 226 is WIDER than Art 32**
- **Emergency:** Art 358 suspends **Art 19** (war/external aggression only) · Art 359 suspends the right to move courts · **Articles 20 and 21 can NEVER be suspended** — the **44th Amendment 1978** response to ***ADM Jabalpur***
