## The Big Idea
Markets, left to themselves, tend to be **exploitative** — of workers, consumers, and producers alike — because private companies chase maximum profit. The government's job is to make **laws** that protect people from this exploitation, and just as importantly, to **enforce** those laws — because a law that exists only on paper protects no one, as the horrifying Bhopal Gas Tragedy showed.

---

## Recalling "A Shirt in the Market"
This chapter picks up directly from the Class VII story of a shirt's journey from cotton farmer to shop shelf. In that chain — the small cotton farmer, the weavers of Erode, the workers in the garment-exporting factory — nearly everyone directly or indirectly involved in production faced **exploitation** or an unfair situation. **Markets everywhere tend to be exploitative of people, whether as workers, consumers, or producers.** To protect people from this, the government makes laws that try to keep unfair practices to a minimum.

---

## Laws That Protect Workers: the Minimum Wages Act
Private companies, contractors, and businesspersons naturally want to maximise profit — and in that drive, they might deny workers their rights, including fair wages. In the eyes of the law, **denying workers their wages is illegal.**

- The **Minimum Wages Act** ensures a worker cannot legally be paid less than a specified minimum. Minimum wages are **revised upward every few years.**
- Beyond wages, laws also govern the relationship between the **three parties in a market — worker, consumer, and producer** — so that none of them is exploited by the others.

### Table 1 — key laws protecting different interests in the market
| Law | Why is it necessary? | Whose interests does it protect? |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimum Wages Act** | Many workers are denied fair wages because they badly need work and have no bargaining power | All workers — especially farm labourers, construction workers, factory workers, domestic workers |
| **Workplace safety laws** (alarm systems, emergency exits, functioning machinery) | Unsafe workplaces put workers' lives and health at serious risk | Workers |
| **Product quality/safety standards** (e.g., electrical appliances) | Consumers can be put at risk by poor-quality products (appliances, food, medicines) | Consumers |
| **Price control on essential goods** (e.g., sugar, kerosene, foodgrains) | Without this, prices could rise beyond what poor people can afford | The poor, who would otherwise be unable to afford these goods |
| **Anti-pollution laws** (factories must not pollute air/water) | Unchecked pollution harms public health and the environment | The general public/community around factories |
| **Laws against child labour** | Children are especially vulnerable to exploitation in workplaces | Children |
| **Right to form workers' unions/associations** | By organising into unions, workers gain combined bargaining power to demand fair wages and better conditions | Workers |

**On child labour specifically:** According to the **2011 Census, over 4 million children in India aged 5–14** work in various occupations, including hazardous ones. In **2016, Parliament amended the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**, banning the employment of children below 14 in **all** occupations, and of adolescents (14–18 years) in hazardous occupations and processes. It made employing such children/adolescents a **cognizable offence**, punishable with a jail term of six months to two years and/or a fine of ₹20,000 to ₹50,000. An online portal, **PENCIL** (Platform for Effective Enforcement for No Child Labour, pencil.gov.in), became functional in 2017 for filing complaints, tracking children, and monitoring the National Child Labour Project.

## Why Making Laws Isn't Enough: the crucial role of enforcement
**Merely making laws is not enough — the government must ensure these laws are actually implemented (enforced).** This matters even more when a law is meant to protect the **weak from the strong.**

- To ensure every worker gets fair wages, the government must **regularly inspect worksites** and punish violators.
- When workers are poor or powerless, **fear of losing future earnings or facing reprisals** often forces them to silently accept unfairly low wages — employers know this and exploit it. This is exactly why **enforcement** is crucial, not just the existence of a law.
- Many of these laws are rooted in the Constitution's **Fundamental Rights** — for instance, the **Right against Exploitation** states no one can be forced to work for low wages or under bondage, and the Constitution specifically states: *"no child below the age of 14 years shall be employed to work in any factory or mines or engaged in any other hazardous employment."*

## Case Study: The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (2 December 1984)
This is the chapter's central, devastating case study — described by the book as **"the world's worst industrial tragedy."**

**What happened:** Union Carbide (UC), an American company, ran a pesticide factory in Bhopal. At midnight on 2 December 1984, **methyl isocyanate (MIC)** — a highly poisonous gas — began leaking from the plant.

> Survivor **Aziza Sultan** recalled: *"At about 12.30 am I woke to the sound of my baby coughing badly. In the half-light I saw that the room was filled with a white cloud. I heard people shouting 'run, run'. Then I started coughing, with each breath seeming as if I was breathing in fire. My eyes were burning."*

- Within **three days, more than 8,000 people were dead**; hundreds of thousands were **maimed** (permanently injured).
- Most victims came from **poor, working-class families**; nearly **50,000 people remain too sick to work even today.** Survivors developed severe respiratory disorders, eye problems, and other disorders; children were born with **peculiar abnormalities.**

**This was not an accident — it was negligence.** Union Carbide had **deliberately ignored essential safety measures to cut costs**, and there had been earlier gas-leak incidents at the plant (killing one worker, injuring several) even before the main disaster.

**The fight for justice — and its failure:**
- Despite overwhelming evidence, **UC refused to accept responsibility.**
- The Indian government represented the victims in a civil case, filing a **$3 billion compensation claim in 1985** — but accepted a settlement of just **$470 million in 1989.**
- Survivors appealed against this low settlement, but the **Supreme Court ruled the settlement amount would stand.**
- UC stopped operations but left behind tons of toxic chemicals that seeped into the ground and contaminated water. **Dow Chemical**, which now owns the plant, has refused to take responsibility for the clean-up.
- Even decades later, people continue fighting for **safe drinking water, healthcare facilities, jobs for those poisoned**, and for **Warren Anderson** (the UC chairman, who faced criminal charges) to be prosecuted.

### What is a Worker's Worth? Why did this happen in India?
The chapter asks a hard question: why did Union Carbide set up this plant in India in the first place?
- **Cheaper labour:** Wages in India are far lower than in the USA, so companies save money — and can demand longer hours and spend less on worker facilities like housing.
- **Cost-cutting through lower safety standards:** Every safety device at the Bhopal plant was malfunctioning or in short supply. Between 1980 and 1984, the MIC plant's work crew was **cut in half (from 12 to 6 workers)**, safety training was reduced from **6 months to 15 days**, and the **night-shift safety supervisor post was abolished entirely.**

**A telling comparison — UC's own plants:**
| | West Virginia, USA plant | Bhopal, India plant |
|---|---|---|
| Warning/monitoring system | Computerised | Manual gauges and human senses only |
| Emergency evacuation plan | In place | Nonexistent |

**Why such a stark difference?** The chapter's answer: it comes down to what is perceived as the **"worth" of a worker.** In India, high unemployment means one worker can easily be replaced by another — so employers, exploiting this vulnerability, feel free to ignore safety. This is why, even decades after Bhopal, **accidents at construction sites, mines, and factories due to employer negligence continue to be reported regularly.**

### Enforcement failures that led to the disaster
The government, as lawmaker and enforcer, is responsible for ensuring safety laws are implemented and that the **Right to Life under Article 21** is not violated. In Bhopal's case, enforcement failed on two levels:
1. **Safety laws themselves were lax (weak)** in India.
2. **Even these weak laws were not enforced.** Government officials refused to formally recognise the plant as "hazardous," which allowed it to operate in a populated locality. When municipal officials in Bhopal objected to an MIC production unit being installed in 1978 (calling it a safety violation), the government's position was that the state needed the plant's continued investment and the jobs it provided — it was considered "unthinkable" to ask UC to adopt cleaner technology. Government inspectors kept approving plant procedures even as repeated leaks made it obvious something was seriously wrong.

> **The core lesson:** This is the *opposite* of what a law-making and enforcement agency should do — instead of protecting people's safety, both the government and the private company disregarded it.

## New Laws to Protect the Environment
Before 1984, India had very few environmental protection laws, and almost no enforcement — the environment was treated as a "free" resource that industries could pollute without restriction. Union Carbide, as a result, did not have to spend any money cleaning up pollution — something it *would* have had to do as a standard part of production in the USA.

**The Bhopal disaster brought environmental issues to the forefront**, because thousands of people who had no connection to the factory as workers were still badly affected by the leaked gases — revealing that existing laws, weak as they were, only covered individual workers, not the wider public harmed by industrial accidents. In response to sustained pressure from environmental activists, **the Indian government introduced new environmental laws after Bhopal**, based on the principle that **the polluter must be held accountable** for environmental damage — since the environment is a shared resource across generations, not something to be destroyed for the sake of industrial development.

**Courts have repeatedly upheld the right to a healthy environment as part of the Right to Life:**
- ***Subhash Kumar vs. State of Bihar* (1991):** The Supreme Court held that the **Right to Life under Article 21** includes the **right to enjoyment of pollution-free water and air**, for the full enjoyment of life. The government is responsible for setting up laws and procedures to check pollution, clean rivers, and impose heavy fines on polluters.
- In a series of rulings from 1998 onwards, the Supreme Court ordered **all public transport vehicles in Delhi to switch to CNG (Compressed Natural Gas)** instead of diesel/petrol, which reduced air pollution in the city considerably — though a later report by the Centre for Science and Environment found toxic substance levels rising again due to increased diesel car emissions and growing vehicle numbers.

### Environment as a Public Facility: a complicated trade-off
While courts have issued strong environmental orders, these have sometimes had unintended, harsh consequences for workers' livelihoods:
- Courts directed polluting industries in **residential areas of Delhi** (which were also polluting the river Yamuna) to close down or relocate outside the city.
- This solved one problem (urban pollution) but created another: **many workers lost their jobs**, or had to move far away to relocated factory sites — and often, the *same* pollution problem simply reappeared in these new, previously unpolluted areas. Meanwhile, the underlying issue of **workers' safety conditions** remained unaddressed throughout.
- Research shows a troubling pattern: the growing environmental concern of the **middle class** is often achieved **at the expense of the poor** — for example, slums being cleared as part of a city's "beautification drive," or a polluting factory simply being pushed to the city's outskirts, displacing the pollution (and the workers) rather than truly solving it.

**The chapter's proposed way forward:** the real challenge is finding solutions where **everyone benefits from a clean environment** — for instance, by gradually shifting factories to **cleaner technologies and processes**, with the government both encouraging/supporting this shift and fining polluters. This way, workers' livelihoods are protected *and* both workers and surrounding communities get to enjoy a safe environment — rather than treating environmental protection and workers' rights as a trade-off against each other.

*(The chapter also briefly notes that advanced countries sometimes relocate hazardous industries — like pesticide/asbestos production, or hazardous **ship-breaking**, seen in Alang, Gujarat — to developing countries like India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, precisely to take advantage of weaker safety and environmental laws there.)*

## Conclusion: The Government's Twin Role
Private companies, contractors, and businesspersons, in the drive for higher profits, often resort to unfair practices — paying workers low wages, employing children, ignoring dangerous working conditions, and ignoring environmental damage. The government's central role, therefore, is **twofold**: it must both **make appropriate laws** *and* **enforce them** to control such practices and ensure social justice. As the Bhopal Gas Tragedy tragically demonstrated, **laws that are weak or poorly enforced can cause serious, irreversible harm.**

While government has the leading role, **ordinary people also have power** — they can exert pressure so that both private companies and the government act in the interests of society, as seen in how environmental activism led to new laws after Bhopal. The chapter closes urging that people must keep demanding **stronger laws protecting workers' interests** — the right to work, to a fair wage, and to decent working conditions — so that the Right to Life is genuinely achieved for all, not just those with power and money.

## Postscript from the Textbook: "The Constitution as a Living Ideal"
The book's final reflection (found at the very end, after this chapter) ties every chapter together: the **Right to Life (Article 21)** has, over the years, been expanded by ordinary citizens taking their grievances to court —
- The case of injured farmer **Hakim Sheikh** *(Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samiti vs. State of West Bengal, 1996)* established the **right to health** as part of the Right to Life.
- ***Olga Tellis vs. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985)** established the **right to livelihood** as part of the Right to Life (Chapter 4).
- ***Subhash Kumar vs. State of Bihar* (1991)** established the right to a **pollution-free environment** as part of the Right to Life (this chapter).
- Courts have also included the **right to education** and the **right to shelter** within this expanding understanding of Article 21.

None of these specific rights (health, livelihood, environment, shelter) were written out explicitly in the Constitution's original 1949 text — but they were present **in spirit**, through the democratic ideals the Constitution established. This is why the Constitution is often called a **"living document"** — its core, constitutive principles of dignity and justice keep expanding to meet the real, evolving needs of citizens, through the ongoing efforts of ordinary people fighting for their rights.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consumer | An individual who buys goods for personal use, not for resale |
| Producer | A person or organisation that produces goods for sale in the market (sometimes keeping part of the produce for personal use, e.g., a farmer) |
| Investment | Money spent to purchase new machinery, buildings, or training, in order to increase or modernise production in the future |
| Workers' unions | Associations of workers (common in factories/offices, but also among groups like domestic workers) whose leaders bargain and negotiate with employers over wages, work rules, hiring/firing, benefits, and workplace safety |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. Markets tend to be **exploitative** of workers, consumers, and producers; the government makes **laws** — like the Minimum Wages Act, workplace safety laws, and product quality standards — to keep this exploitation to a minimum.
2. A law is meaningless without **enforcement**, especially when it protects the weak from the strong — because poor/powerless workers often cannot resist exploitation on their own out of fear of losing their livelihood.
3. The **Bhopal Gas Tragedy (2 December 1984)** — caused by Union Carbide's deliberate cost-cutting on safety — killed over 8,000 people within three days and left nearly 50,000 too sick to work even decades later; the $470 million settlement (1989) is widely seen as grossly inadequate.
4. Bhopal happened due to a **double enforcement failure**: India's safety laws were weak, and even those weak laws were not properly enforced — government officials prioritised the plant's "investment value" over safety.
5. The **worth assigned to Indian workers' safety** (linked to high unemployment and easy replaceability) helps explain why UC applied far lower safety standards in Bhopal than at its US plants.
6. Bhopal spurred **new environmental laws** based on "polluter must be accountable"; courts have since ruled the **right to a pollution-free environment** is part of the Right to Life (*Subhash Kumar vs. State of Bihar*, 1991) — though environmental crackdowns can sometimes hurt workers' livelihoods if not handled carefully.
7. The Constitution is a **"living document"** — the Right to Life (Article 21) has been expanded over time by citizens' court battles to include health, livelihood, a clean environment, education, and shelter, even though these weren't explicitly written into the original 1949 text.
