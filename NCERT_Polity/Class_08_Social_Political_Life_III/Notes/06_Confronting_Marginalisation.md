## The Big Idea
Marginalised groups are not powerless victims who simply accept their situation — Adivasis, Dalits, Muslims, women, and other marginal groups have a long history of **struggle and resistance.** This chapter looks at how they confront marginalisation by invoking the Constitution's Fundamental Rights, and how the government responds through specific laws and welfare schemes.

---

## Setting the Stage
In the previous chapter, you read about two groups (Adivasis and Muslims) and their experiences of inequality and discrimination. Though structurally powerless, such groups have **fought, protested, and struggled** against being excluded or dominated. Over their long histories, they have adopted a range of strategies — **religious solace, armed struggle, self-improvement and education, economic uplift** — there is no single way of doing things. The choice of strategy has always depended on the specific circumstances the marginalised group finds itself in.

This chapter looks at how groups and individuals challenge existing inequalities. Adivasis, Dalits, Muslims, women, and other marginal groups argue that **simply by being citizens of a democratic country, they possess equal rights that must be respected.** Many of them look to the **Constitution** to address their concerns — so this chapter examines why the Constitution is something marginalised groups invoke in the course of their struggles, how rights get translated into laws to protect groups from continued exploitation, and how the government formulates policies to promote these groups' access to development.

---

## Invoking Fundamental Rights
As you learnt in Chapter 1, the Constitution lays down the principles that make our society and polity democratic, defined through the list of **Fundamental Rights** available equally to all Indians. Marginalised groups have drawn on these rights in **two ways**:
1. By **insisting on their Fundamental Rights**, they have forced the government to recognise the injustice done to them.
2. They have insisted that the government **enforce these laws.** In some instances, the struggles of the marginalised have even influenced the government to frame **new laws**, in keeping with the spirit of the Fundamental Rights.

**Article 17** of the Constitution states that **untouchability has been abolished** — meaning no one can henceforth prevent Dalits from educating themselves, entering temples, using public facilities, and so on. It also signals that practising untouchability is wrong and **will not be tolerated by a democratic government** — untouchability is now a **punishable crime.**

**Article 15** strengthens this further: no citizen of India can be discriminated against on the basis of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (as you learnt in the Class VII chapter *On Equality*). This has been used by Dalits to seek equality where it has been denied to them.

### A poem: questioning the idea of "purity"
The chapter includes a powerful poem by **Soyrabai**, wife of the 14th-century Bhakti poet **Chokhamela** (both belonged to the Mahar caste, considered untouchable at the time):
> *"A body is unclean, they say / Only the soul is untainted / But the impurity of the body / Is born within the body / ...By which ritual does the body become pure? / Not a creature has been born except in a bloody womb. / This is the glory of God, / Defilement exists within. / The body is polluted from within..."*

Soyrabai questions the very idea of purity — arguing that since every human being is born in the same way, nothing makes one body more or less "pure" than another. She suggests that **pollution — the key tool the caste system uses to deny people access to spaces, work, knowledge, and dignity — does not come from the nature of one's work, but from within a person's own thoughts, values, and beliefs.**

Therefore, Dalits can **"invoke" or "draw on"** a Fundamental Right whenever they feel they have been treated badly by an individual, a community, or even the government — using the Constitution to demand that the government abide by it and deliver justice. Similarly, other minority groups draw on the Fundamental Rights section, particularly the **right to freedom of religion** and **cultural and educational rights**: distinct cultural/religious groups like Muslims and Parsis have the right to be guardians of their own culture's content and to decide how best it is preserved — ensuring **cultural justice**, so their culture is not dominated or wiped out by the majority community's culture.

## Laws for the Marginalised
The government doesn't only make general laws — it also takes action through **specific laws and policies** targeted at the marginalised, sometimes emerging from a committee's recommendations or a survey.

### Promoting Social Justice: schemes
Both state and central governments create specific schemes for tribal areas or areas with a high Dalit population — for example, **free or subsidised hostels** for students from Dalit and Adivasi communities, so they can access education facilities that may not be available in their own localities.

### The Reservation Policy
One especially significant (and highly contentious/debated) policy tool is **reservation** — laws that reserve seats in education and government employment for Dalits and Adivasis. This is based on the argument that in a society where certain sections of the population were **denied opportunities to learn and work for centuries**, a democratic government must actively step in to assist these sections.

**How reservation works:**
- Governments (state and central) maintain their own official lists of **Scheduled Castes (Dalits), Scheduled Tribes, and backward/most backward castes.**
- Students applying to educational institutions, and those applying for government posts, must furnish **proof of caste/tribe status** (caste and tribe certificates).
- If a particular caste or tribe is on the official list, a candidate from that group can avail the benefit of reservation.
- For admission to professional colleges (e.g., medical colleges), the government sets **"cut-off" marks** — meaning not all Dalit/tribal candidates qualify automatically; only those who score reasonably well and clear the cut-off can be admitted through reservation. Governments also offer **special scholarships** for these students.

*(You will read more about reservation for backward classes in the Class IX Political Science textbook.)*

## Protecting the Rights of Dalits and Adivasis: a case study
Beyond policies, the country also has specific **laws** guarding against discrimination and exploitation of marginalised communities. The chapter presents a detailed (real-life-based) case study to show how these laws are actually used.

### The story of Rathnam, Jakmalgur village
Once every five years, the village of Jakmalgur honours its local deity in a big five-day festival, with priests from 20 neighbouring villages attending. As part of the ceremony, a member of the **Dalit community** washes the feet of all the priests and then bathes in the same water. In Jakmalgur, this task had always fallen to the men of **Rathnam's family** — his father and grandfather had both performed it before him. Though never allowed to enter the temple, this ritual was viewed by the village as a "great honour" bestowed on them for the occasion.

This time, it was Rathnam's turn — now 20 years old and studying engineering at a nearby college. **Rathnam refused to perform the ritual.** He explained that he had no faith in the practice, and that his family had only ever been forced to perform it *because* they were Dalits.

**The backlash:**
- Both the powerful castes in the village **and** some families from his own community were angered by his refusal.
- The powerful castes were shocked that "such a young boy had the guts to refuse" — they believed it was Rathnam's **education** that had allowed him to imagine he could compare himself to them as an equal.
- Even people from Rathnam's own caste were **afraid to support him**, fearing the anger of the powerful — many worked as daily-wage labourers on the dominant castes' fields, and worried about losing their livelihood if the powerful castes refused to employ them. Some also feared the "wrath of the local deity" if the ritual was not performed — to which Rathnam pointed out that since **no Dalit had ever entered the temple**, the deity could hardly be "angry with them" for a broken ritual.
- The powerful castes decided to **"teach Rathnam a lesson"**: his community was ordered to **ostracise** (socially boycott) him and his family — no one was to speak to them or work for/with them. One night, men set fire to his family's hut; Rathnam escaped with his mother.

### Rathnam's response: using the law
Rathnam then went to the local police station and **filed a case under the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.** Other Dalit families still did not openly support him, fearing a similar fate — but the case was picked up by the media, bringing many journalists to the village; Rathnam became known as a **symbol of Dalit action.** The forced ritual was eventually called off, though his family was still compelled to move away, continuing to face ostracism from the powerful castes.

## The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989
This Act was framed in **1989** in response to demands by Dalits and others that the government take seriously the everyday ill-treatment and humiliation faced by Dalit and tribal groups. While such mistreatment had persisted for a long time, it took on an increasingly **violent character** in the late 1970s and 1980s.

**Historical background — Dalit assertion in the 1970s–80s:** In parts of southern India, a number of **assertive Dalit groups** emerged during this period, refusing to perform their so-called "caste duties" and insisting on equal treatment — much like Rathnam, they refused to follow practices rooted in the humiliation and exploitation of Dalits. This resulted in **violence unleashed against them by the more powerful castes.** In order to make clear to the government that untouchability was still being practised, and in the most hideous ways, Dalit groups demanded new laws that would specifically **list various forms of violence against Dalits and prescribe stringent punishment** for perpetrators. Similarly, throughout the 1970s and 1980s, **Adivasi communities also organised themselves** and demanded equal rights and the return of their land and resources — they too faced the anger and violence of powerful social groups.

**Why the Act's list of crimes is so long and specific:** The Act contains a very long list of crimes — some too horrible even to contemplate — because it seeks **both to punish these acts and to influence the way people think and act.** The Act distinguishes several levels of crimes:

1. **Modes of humiliation** — both physically horrific and morally reprehensible — such as: (i) forcing a member of a Scheduled Caste/Tribe to drink or eat any inedible or obnoxious substance; (iii) forcibly removing their clothes or parading them naked/with a painted face, or any similarly degrading act.
2. **Actions that dispossess Dalits and Adivasis** of their meagre resources, or force them into slave labour — for example: (iv) wrongfully occupying or cultivating land owned by, or allotted to, a member of a Scheduled Caste/Tribe, or getting land allotted to them transferred away.
3. **Crimes against Dalit and tribal women specifically** — recognised as being of a distinct kind, punishing anyone who (xi) assaults or uses force on a Scheduled Caste/Tribe woman with intent to dishonour her.

> **Two different, connected purposes:** The Act does not just describe terrible crimes — it also forces society to confront "what dreadful deeds human beings are capable of." In this sense, such laws work to both **punish** wrongdoers and gradually **influence and shift social attitudes.**

## A Closer Look: The Scourge of Manual Scavenging
**Manual scavenging** refers to the degrading practice of removing human and animal waste/excreta — using brooms, tin plates, and baskets — from dry latrines, and carrying it on the head to disposal grounds some distance away. A **manual scavenger** is the person who does this job — **mainly Dalit women and young girls.**

- According to the **Safai Karamchari Andolan** (an Andhra Pradesh-based organisation working with manual scavengers), around **one lakh (100,000) people** from Dalit communities remain employed in this job, working across **26 lakh** private and community dry latrines managed by municipalities.
- Manual scavengers face **subhuman conditions**, constantly exposed to infections affecting their eyes, skin, respiratory, and gastro-intestinal systems — and are paid **very low wages** (around ₹200/day in urban municipalities, even less if working privately).
- Even though untouchability has been abolished by the Constitution, manual scavengers in different parts of the country — such as the **Bhangis** in Gujarat, **Pakhis** in Andhra Pradesh, and **Sikkaliars** in Tamil Nadu — continue to be treated as **untouchable.** They often live in separate settlements on the outskirts of villages, denied access to temples and public water facilities.

**Legal action against manual scavenging:**
- **1993:** The government passed the **Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act**, banning both the employment of manual scavengers and the construction of dry latrines.
- **2003:** The **Safai Karamchari Andolan and 13 other organisations/individuals**, including seven scavengers, filed a **PIL (Public Interest Litigation)** in the Supreme Court, arguing that manual scavenging still existed and continued even in government undertakings like the railways — violating their Fundamental Rights.
- The court found that the **number of manual scavengers had actually increased** since the 1993 law. It directed every department/ministry of the union and state governments to verify the facts within six months, and where manual scavenging was found to exist, to actively take up a **time-bound programme for liberation and rehabilitation.**
- **6 December 2013:** The **Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act** came into force.

## Adivasi Demands and the 1989 Act
The **1989 SC/ST Act** matters for Adivasis too — Adivasi activists invoke it to defend their right to occupy land traditionally theirs. As you read in the previous chapter, Adivasis are often unwilling to move from their land and are forcibly displaced. Activists argue that:
- Those who **forcibly encroach on tribal lands should be punished** under this law.
- The Act simply **confirms what the Constitution already promised** tribal people — that land belonging to tribal people **cannot be sold to or bought by non-tribal people.** Where this has happened anyway, the Constitution guarantees tribal people the **right to re-possess their land.**

**C.K. Janu**, a well-known Adivasi activist, has pointed out that one of the biggest violators of these constitutional rights are, ironically, **the governments of various Indian states themselves** — since it is state governments that allow non-tribal encroachers (timber merchants, paper mills, etc.) to exploit tribal land, and that forcibly evict tribal people from their traditional forests when declaring areas as reserved forests or wildlife sanctuaries. She has also argued that where tribals have already been evicted and cannot return to their original lands, they **must be compensated** — the government must draw up plans for them to live and work elsewhere. Her reasoning: governments already spend huge sums building industrial or other projects on land taken from tribals — so **why should they be reluctant to spend even modest amounts rehabilitating the displaced?**

*(The chapter also notes, in a side box, that the central government passed the **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006** — meant to undo historical injustices done to forest-dwelling populations by not recognising their rights to land and resources. This Act recognises tribal people's right to homestead, cultivable and grazing land, and non-timber forest produce, and points out that the rights of forest dwellers include the conservation of forests and bio-diversity.)*

## Conclusion
The existence of a right, a law, or even a policy **on paper does not mean it exists in reality.** People have had to constantly work at, and make efforts to, translate these into principles that actually guide the actions of their fellow citizens — and even their leaders. **The desire for equality, dignity, and respect is not new** — it has existed in different forms throughout history, as this chapter's examples show (from Chokhamela and Soyrabai's 14th-century poetry, to Rathnam's 21st-century refusal). Similarly, even in a democratic society, similar processes of **struggle, writing, negotiation, and organising** must continue.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Assertive | Able to express oneself and one's views strongly |
| Confront | To come face to face with, or to challenge, someone or something — in this chapter, groups challenging their marginalisation |
| Dispossessed | To be deprived of ownership; having to give up something one owns or has authority over |
| Ostracise | To exclude or banish an individual or group — here, a social boycott of a person and their family |
| Morally reprehensible | An act that violates all norms of decency and dignity a society believes in — usually referring to a hideous, repugnant act that goes against a society's accepted values |
| Policy | A stated course of action that provides direction for the future, sets goals, or lays out principles/guidelines to be followed — governments, but also schools and companies, have policies |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. Marginalised groups **confront** their situation by **invoking Fundamental Rights** — forcing the government to recognise injustice, insisting laws be enforced, and sometimes pushing for entirely new laws.
2. **Article 17** abolishes untouchability (making it a punishable crime); **Article 15** bans discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth — both are key tools Dalits use to seek equality.
3. The government also promotes social justice through **schemes** (hostels, scholarships) and the **reservation policy** — reserved seats in education/employment for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, based on official caste/tribe lists and (for professional colleges) cut-off marks.
4. **Rathnam's story** shows a Dalit individual using the **law** (the SC/ST Prevention of Atrocities Act, 1989) to resist a humiliating, caste-based ritual — and the real social cost (ostracism, arson) he faced for doing so.
5. The **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989** emerged from Dalit and Adivasi assertion and resulting violence in the 1970s–80s; it lists specific crimes (humiliation, dispossession of resources, crimes against women) to both punish and shift social attitudes.
6. **Manual scavenging** — mainly done by Dalit women in degrading, unsafe, low-paid conditions — remains a stark example of ongoing untouchability despite being banned; the 1993 Act failed to stop it, leading to a 2003 PIL and finally the **2013 Rehabilitation Act.**
7. Adivasi activists like **C.K. Janu** invoke the 1989 Act and the Constitution to demand protection from land encroachment and displacement — arguing governments themselves are often the biggest violators of tribal land rights.
