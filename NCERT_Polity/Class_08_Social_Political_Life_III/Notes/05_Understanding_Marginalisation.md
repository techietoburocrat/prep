# Chapter 5: Understanding Marginalisation

> **Note:** The PDF file in this folder is named `05_Judiciary.pdf`, following an older edition's title. ## The Big Idea
To be **marginalised** means being pushed to the sides or fringes of society rather than being at its centre — losing access to resources, opportunities, and the power to assert one's rights. Marginalisation is never caused by just one factor; economic, social, cultural, and political disadvantages combine and reinforce each other. This chapter looks closely at two communities that experience marginalisation in India today: **Adivasis** and **Muslims.**

---

## What Does It Mean to Be Socially Marginalised?
You may already have felt a small version of this in your own classroom or playground: if your taste in music or films is different, if your accent marks you out, if you dress differently or don't play the same sport as most of your classmates, you may feel that you're not quite "in" — as if what you say, feel, and do isn't considered acceptable by others.

Communities can experience this same feeling of exclusion on a much larger, more serious scale:
- They may speak a different language, follow different customs, or belong to a different religion from the majority.
- They may be marginalised simply because they are **poor**, considered to be of "low" social status, or viewed with hostility and fear by others.

This sense of difference and exclusion leads marginalised communities to **lack access to resources and opportunities**, and to feel **powerless** to assert their rights compared to more powerful, dominant sections of society — those who own land, are wealthier, better educated, and politically influential. **Marginalisation is rarely experienced in just one sphere of life** — economic, social, cultural, and political factors work together to push certain groups to the margins.

## Part 1: Adivasis and Marginalisation

### A storyboard: an Adivasi family in Delhi
The chapter opens with a moving story. Soma and Helen are watching the Republic Day parade on TV with their grandfather (Dadu), and see an "adivasi float" showing tribal people dancing. Helen asks why adivasis are always shown only dancing on TV. Dadu explains that the lives of adivasis are actually very rich, but most people don't know anything else about them — and then tells his own story:

> "When I was young, our village in Odisha was beautiful. We got everything we needed from the land and the forests around us. We in turn respected the land, the forest, the river."

But then:
- **Forest officials and contractors** told the community the forest was "not theirs" and cut down large parts of it. When villagers protested, they were beaten and taken to court, where they had no lawyers and could not fight their cases.
- **"Companywallahs" (company representatives)** arrived, saying there was iron ore under the land and they wanted to mine it. They promised jobs and money in exchange for the land. Some villagers were excited; others warned this would destroy their lives. Some gave thumbprints without realising they were signing away their land; only a few token jobs were actually given. Most villagers refused to sell — **until they were beaten and threatened until everyone was forced to sell and abandon the land of their forefathers,** with the authorities supporting the companies. Their entire way of living vanished overnight.
- The family received a little money, which barely lasted in the city. They lost their means of livelihood entirely, and were "cramped into a tiny rented room," desperately missing their open, carefree village life.
- Dadu's children (Soma and Helen's parents) didn't attend school for several years during this difficult period. When they finally did go back, they had missed so much and other children made fun of them — they spoke Santhali at home and didn't know Hindi.
- Eventually, the father got a job in Delhi, and the family moved there. Helen, moved by the story, says she wishes she could show her friends the village before it was destroyed, and dreams of one day making a movie about the adivasi story.

> **This is not just one family's story.** Dadu's experience mirrors the lives of millions of Adivasis across India, and the rest of the chapter explains why.

### Who are Adivasis?
**Adivasi** literally means "original inhabitants" — communities who lived, and often continue to live, in close association with **forests**.

- Around **8% of India's population** is Adivasi.
- They are **not a single, homogeneous population** — there are over **500 different Adivasi groups** in India.
- They are particularly numerous in states like Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, and the North-Eastern states (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura). Odisha alone is home to more than 60 different tribal groups.
- Many of India's most important **mining and industrial centres** (Jamshedpur, Rourkela, Bokaro, Bhilai) are located in Adivasi areas.
- Adivasi societies are distinctive because there is often **very little hierarchy** among them — quite different from communities organised around *jati-varna* (caste) or those historically ruled by kings.
- Adivasis practise a wide range of belief systems, often involving the worship of ancestors, village and nature spirits ('mountain-spirits,' 'river-spirits,' 'animal-spirits'). They have also long been influenced by, and have influenced, surrounding religions (Shakta, Buddhist, Vaishnav, Bhakti); significant numbers converted to Christianity during the 19th century.
- Adivasis have their own languages — often radically different from, and possibly as old as, Sanskrit. **Santhali** has the largest number of speakers and even has a significant body of publications, including online magazines.

> **A note on terminology:** Adivasis are also officially referred to as **Scheduled Tribes (ST)** — the term used by the Indian government in official documents, often grouped together with **Scheduled Castes (SC)** in the combined category "SC and ST."

### Adivasis and Stereotyping
In India, Adivasi communities tend to get "showcased" in very particular, narrow ways — at school functions, official events, in books, and in movies, they are almost always portrayed through colourful costumes, headgear, and dancing. Beyond this, most people know very little about the actual realities of Adivasi lives. This narrow portrayal often wrongly leads people to believe Adivasis are **exotic, primitive, and backward** — and Adivasis are frequently, unfairly, blamed for their own "lack of advancement," as though they are simply resistant to change. As you read in Class VI, **stereotyping a community like this can lead directly to discrimination against them.**

### Adivasis and Development
This section reveals a striking historical irony. Forests have always been absolutely crucial to the development of empires and settled civilisations in India — metal ores (iron, copper, gold, silver), coal, diamonds, timber, medicinal herbs, and animal products (wax, lac, honey) — and even elephants, the mainstay of imperial armies — all came from forests. Forests also recharge many of India's rivers and remain crucial to air and water quality even today.

Until the middle of the 19th century, **Adivasis had deep knowledge of, access to, and control over most of these vast forest tracts** — and were largely *not* ruled by large states and empires, who instead often *depended* on Adivasis for access to forest resources. This is the radical opposite of how we tend to picture Adivasis today, as marginal and powerless.

**But over the past 200 years**, Adivasis have been increasingly forced — through economic changes, forest policies, and political force applied by the State and private industry — to migrate and become workers on plantations, at construction sites, in industries, and as domestic workers. For the first time in their history, they largely do **not** control or have direct access to forest territories.

**A historical example — the Assam tea migrations:** From the 1830s onwards, Adivasis from Jharkhand and nearby areas moved in very large numbers to plantations across India and the world (Mauritius, the Caribbean, even Australia). India's tea industry became possible through their labour in Assam — today there are **70 lakh Adivasis in Assam alone.** This migration involved extreme hardship, torture, heartbreak, and death — in the 19th century alone, an estimated **five lakh Adivasis perished** during these migrations. A folk song from the period captures both the hope and the harsh reality faced by migrants:
> *"Come Mini, let's go to Assam... The Sardar says work, work / The Babu says catch and bring them in / The Saheb says I'll take off the skin of your back / Hey Jaduram, you deceived us by sending us to Assam."*

**Today's reality — displacement:** Forest lands have been cleared for timber, agriculture, and industry. Adivasi areas, often rich in minerals and natural resources, are taken over for mining and large industrial projects, frequently through forceful and improper procedures.
- **More than 50% of persons displaced due to mines and mining projects are tribals** (official figures).
- A recent survey found **79% of persons displaced** from Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, and Jharkhand are tribals.
- Huge tracts of tribal land have gone underwater due to the hundreds of dams built in independent India.
- In the North-East, tribal lands remain **highly militarised.**
- India has **106 national parks (44,402.9 sq km)** and **573 wildlife sanctuaries (123,762.56 sq km)** (Wildlife Institute of India, November 2023) — areas where tribals originally lived but were evicted from. If they continue to stay, they are officially termed **"encroachers."**

**Example — Niyamgiri Hill, Odisha:** This sacred mountain of the Dongarria Konds (an Adivasi community) has been strongly resisted from being turned into an aluminium mine and refinery by a major company, joined by environmentalists — with a case still pending in the Supreme Court.

**The consequences of displacement go far beyond lost income:**
- **45% of tribal groups in rural areas and 35% in urban areas** live below the poverty line.
- Many tribal children are **malnourished**, and **literacy rates among tribals are very low.**
- Adivasis use around **10,000 plant species** (8,000 for medicine, 325 as pesticides, 425 as gums/resins/dyes, 550 as fibres, 3,500 as edible food) — this entire body of traditional knowledge is wiped out when Adivasis lose access to forest lands.
- Displacement means losing far more than income: as Gobindha Maran, displaced by a refinery project in Odisha, put it — *"They took our farming land. They left some houses. They took the cremation ground, temple, well and pond. How will we survive?"* Adivasis lose their **traditions and customs — a whole way of living and being**, not just a paycheck.

> **The key insight:** Economic and social dimensions of marginalisation are deeply interconnected. Destruction in one sphere (losing land) inevitably impacts the other (losing culture, traditions, and community life), and this process of dispossession and displacement can be painful and even violent.

## Part 2: Minorities and Marginalisation — the Muslim Community

### Why does the Constitution protect minorities?
As you read in Unit 1, the Constitution provides safeguards to religious and linguistic minorities as part of the Fundamental Rights. The term **"minority"** most commonly refers to communities that are numerically small relative to the rest of the population — but the concept goes well beyond just numbers; it also involves **power, access to resources, and social/cultural dimensions.**

Since the culture of the majority often shapes how society and government express themselves, being numerically smaller can become a real disadvantage — leading to the marginalisation of smaller communities. **Safeguards exist to protect minority communities from being culturally dominated by the majority**, and from any discrimination or disadvantage they might otherwise face — this insecurity can be worse if relations between minority and majority communities are strained. The Constitution provides these protections because it is committed to protecting India's cultural diversity and promoting equality and justice for all. As you read in Chapter 4, the judiciary plays a crucial role here too — every citizen can approach the courts if their Fundamental Rights are violated.

### Muslims and Marginalisation: what the data shows
According to the **2011 Census, Muslims make up 14.2% of India's population** and are considered a marginalised community due to comparatively lower socio-economic development. Three data tables illustrate this:

**I. Access to Basic Amenities (2008–2009, %)**
| Religious Community | *Pucca* House | Electricity | Tap Water |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hindu | 65.4 | 75.2 | 43.7 |
| **Muslim** | **63.8** | **67.5** | **35.8** |
| Christian | 69.3 | 86.2 | 48.0 |
| Sikh | **91.3** | **96.0** | **49.3** |

*(Muslims have the least access among these four communities to all three basic amenities; Sikhs have the most.)*

**II. Literacy Rate by Religion (2011, %)**
| All | Hindus | Muslims | Christians | Sikhs | Buddhists | Jains |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 74 | 63 | **57** | 74 | 67 | 71 | **86** |

*(Muslims have the lowest literacy rate among these communities; Jains have the highest.)*

**III. Public Employment of Muslims (%)**
| Population | IAS | IPS | IFS | Central PSU | State PSU | Banks & RBI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 13.5 | 3 | 4 | 1.8 | 3.3 | 10.8 | 2.2 |

*(Muslims make up 13.5% of the population but hold only a small fraction of positions in elite services like the IAS, IPS, and IFS — clear underrepresentation.)*

### The Sachar Committee (2005)
Recognising that Muslims lagged behind on various development indicators, the government set up a **high-level committee in 2005, chaired by Justice Rajindar Sachar**, to examine the social, economic, and educational status of Muslims in India. Its report found that, across a range of indicators, the Muslim community's situation was **comparable to that of other marginalised communities like Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.** For example, the average years of schooling for Muslim children (ages 7–16) was found to be much lower than for other socio-religious communities.

**Key data from the Sachar Report on schooling:**
- **25% of Muslim children aged 6–14** have either never been enrolled in school or have dropped out — a much higher percentage than any other socio-religious community.
- The report also **debunked a common myth**: it is often assumed that Muslims prefer sending their children to *Madarsas*, but the data shows only **4% of Muslim children attend Madarsas**, while **66% attend government schools** and **30% attend private schools.**

### Beyond economics: social marginalisation
Muslim marginalisation has dimensions beyond just economic data. Like other minorities, some (not all) Muslim customs and practices — wearing a *burqa*, sporting a long beard, wearing a *fez* — are visibly distinct from what is seen as "mainstream." This visible difference sometimes becomes an excuse to treat Muslims differently and discriminate against them. In some instances, this social marginalisation has led Muslims to migrate away from certain areas, sometimes resulting in the **"ghettoisation"** of the community — clustering together for a greater sense of security. This prejudice can, at its worst, lead to hatred and violence.

## Conclusion
Marginalisation is a **complex phenomenon** with different causes for different communities — Adivasis experience it largely through the loss of land, forest, and livelihood; Muslims experience it through a mix of economic underdevelopment and social/cultural discrimination. Both cases show that marginalisation is linked to experiencing **disadvantage, prejudice, and powerlessness**, and that it is rarely just economic *or* just social — the two reinforce each other.

Importantly, the chapter closes on a note of hope: **the lives of marginalised people can and do change.** No community is marginalised in exactly the same way forever. Both Adivasis and Muslims have long histories of **struggle and resistance**, and want to maintain their cultural distinctiveness while gaining full access to rights, development, and opportunities. ## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Marginalisation | Being pushed to the sides/fringes of society, away from the centre, with reduced access to resources and power |
| Displaced | Forced or compelled to move from one's home, often due to large development projects like dams or mining |
| Hierarchy | A graded system where some have more power/status than others; the caste system is a hierarchy, with Dalits traditionally placed at the lowest end |
| Ghettoisation | The process by which a community, due to social/cultural/economic pressures or fear/hostility, comes to live clustered together in one area, sometimes leading to alienation from the rest of society |
| Mainstream | The cultural context whose customs and practices are seen as dominant or "central"; also refers to the people/communities considered powerful or dominant in society |
| Militarised | An area with a considerable presence of armed forces |
| Malnourished | A person who does not get adequate nutrition or food |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. **Marginalisation** means being pushed to the fringes of society and losing access to resources and power; it results from a mix of **economic, social, cultural, and political** factors working together.
2. **Adivasis ("original inhabitants")** make up about 8% of India's population across 500+ groups; their societies are historically marked by very little hierarchy, unlike caste-based communities.
3. Historically, Adivasis had deep knowledge of and control over India's forests — the opposite of their marginalised position today, which developed largely over the **past 200 years** through displacement for mining, dams, plantations, and industry.
4. Displacement costs Adivasis far more than income — they lose their **traditions, customs, and entire way of life** (Dadu's story; Gobindha Maran's quote).
5. **Minorities** are protected by the Constitution not just because of their numbers, but because being culturally different from the majority can lead to marginalisation and insecurity.
6. **Muslims (14.2% of the population per the 2011 Census)** are a marginalised community on socio-economic indicators — shown by data on basic amenities, literacy, and public employment, and studied in depth by the **Sachar Committee (2005).**
7. Marginalisation is not permanent or uniform — marginalised communities have long histories of **struggle and resistance**, and their situations can and do change over time.
