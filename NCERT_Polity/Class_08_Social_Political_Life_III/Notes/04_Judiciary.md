## The Big Idea
India follows the **rule of law** — laws apply equally to everyone, and fixed procedures must be followed when a law is broken. The **judiciary** (our system of courts) exists to enforce this rule of law. It can only do this job properly because it is **independent** — free from the control of the legislature and the executive.

---

## What is the Role of the Judiciary?
Courts handle an enormous range of issues — from ruling that no teacher can beat a student, to settling river-water disputes between states, to punishing people for crimes. Broadly, the judiciary's work falls into three categories:

1. **Dispute Resolution:** The judicial system provides a mechanism for resolving disputes — between citizens, between citizens and the government, between two state governments, and between the centre and state governments.
2. **Judicial Review:** As the final interpreter of the Constitution, the judiciary has the power to **strike down laws passed by Parliament** if it believes they violate the basic structure of the Constitution. This power is called judicial review.
3. **Upholding the Law and Enforcing Fundamental Rights:** Every citizen of India can approach the Supreme Court or a High Court directly if they believe their Fundamental Rights have been violated.

---

## What is an Independent Judiciary?
**Imagine this:** A powerful politician has encroached on land belonging to your family. But within this judicial system, that same politician has the power to appoint and dismiss the judge hearing your case. Unsurprisingly, the judge turns out to be clearly partial towards the politician.

This scenario shows exactly *why* independence matters: if a judge's job depended on staying in someone's good books, they could never make a fair, impartial decision against that person. Although we often hear about rich and powerful people in India trying to influence judicial outcomes, **the Indian Constitution protects against this by providing for the independence of the judiciary.**

This independence rests on two pillars:

1. **Separation of Powers:** As you read in Chapter 1, this is a key feature of the Constitution. It means the legislature and the executive **cannot interfere** in the judiciary's work — courts are not under the government's control and do not act on its behalf.
2. **Protected Appointments and Tenure:** For separation of powers to actually work, judges of the High Courts and the Supreme Court must be appointed with **very little interference** from the legislature or executive. Once appointed, it is also **very difficult to remove a judge** from office.

It is precisely this independence that allows courts to play a central role in checking misuse of power by the legislature and executive, and in protecting citizens' Fundamental Rights — because anyone can approach the courts if they believe their rights have been violated.

---

## What is the Structure of Courts in India?
India's court system has **three levels**, shaped like a pyramid — many courts at the bottom, narrowing to a single court at the top.

```
                    SUPREME COURT (New Delhi)
              Presided over by the Chief Justice of India
              Decisions are binding on ALL other courts
                            ▲
                            │
                  HIGH COURTS (one per state,
              some states/UTs share a common High Court)
                            ▲
                            │
        SUBORDINATE / DISTRICT COURTS (Tehsil / district level)
       Also called: Trial Court, Court of the District Judge,
     Additional Sessions Judge, Chief Judicial Magistrate, etc.
```

- **Subordinate (District) Courts:** These are the courts most people actually interact with — usually at the district or Tehsil level. There are several of these in every state, and they hear the widest range of cases. Each district is presided over by a **District Judge**.
- **High Courts:** Each state has a High Court, the highest court of that state. (High Courts were first set up in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras in 1862; the Delhi High Court came in 1966. There are now **25 High Courts** — some states share a common High Court, e.g., Punjab and Haryana share one at Chandigarh, and four North-Eastern states share one at Guwahati.)
- **The Supreme Court:** Located in New Delhi, this is the **apex (topmost) court**, presided over by the **Chief Justice of India**. It was established on **26 January 1950** — the same day India became a Republic. **Decisions made by the Supreme Court are binding on every other court in the country.**

### The courts are connected: the appellate system
India has an **integrated judicial system** — decisions of higher courts bind lower courts, and the levels are connected through the **appellate system**. This means that if a person believes a lower court's judgment was unjust, **they can appeal to a higher court** to have the case reheard.

---

## Case Study: State (Delhi Administration) vs Laxman Kumar and Others (1985)
This real case shows the appellate system in action, moving from the lowest court all the way to the Supreme Court.

**The story:** In February 1980, Laxman Kumar married 20-year-old Sudha Goel; they lived in a flat in Delhi with Laxman's brothers and their families. On 2 December 1980, Sudha died in hospital due to burns.

**Trial Court (lowest level):** Sudha's family filed a case. Four neighbours testified as witnesses — they said that on the night of 1 December, they heard Sudha scream, forced their way into the flat, and found her sari in flames; they extinguished the fire. Sudha told them, before she died, that her mother-in-law Shakuntala had poured kerosene oil on her, and her husband Laxman had lit the fire. Witnesses also testified that Sudha's in-laws had tortured her, demanding more cash, a scooter, and a fridge after the birth of her first child. In their defence, Laxman and his mother claimed Sudha's sari had caught fire accidentally while she was heating milk. **Based on the evidence, the Trial Court convicted Laxman, his mother Shakuntala, and his brother-in-law Subash Chandra, sentencing all three to death.**

**High Court (middle level):** In November 1983, the three accused appealed to the High Court. After hearing all the lawyers' arguments, **the High Court decided Sudha had died due to an accidental fire** caused by the kerosene stove, and **acquitted** all three.

**Public reaction:** This verdict deeply troubled women's groups, who had been speaking out against "dowry deaths" throughout the 1980s (as you read in the Class VII photo essay on the Women's Movement). Protesting the High Court's decision, the **Indian Federation of Women Lawyers** filed a separate appeal against it in the Supreme Court.

**Supreme Court (top level):** In 1985, the Supreme Court heard this appeal against the acquittal. After hearing the arguments, it reached a decision **different from the High Court's**: it found Laxman and his mother **guilty**, but acquitted the brother-in-law Subash for lack of sufficient evidence against him. The Supreme Court sentenced the accused to **prison for life.**

> **Why this case matters:** It shows the appellate system clearly — a case can travel from Trial Court → High Court → Supreme Court, with each level capable of reaching a *different* verdict than the one before it, and the highest court's decision being final and binding.

## What are the Different Branches of the Legal System?
The dowry-death case above falls under **criminal law**, since it deals with a crime against society. But the legal system also handles **civil law** cases — recall from Chapter 3 how the 2005 domestic violence law was a civil law. Here's how the two differ:

| | Criminal Law | Civil Law |
|---|---|---|
| **What it deals with** | Conduct/acts the law defines as offences — theft, harassment for dowry, murder | Harm or injury to individuals' rights — disputes over land sale, purchase of goods, rent matters, divorce cases |
| **How a case starts** | Usually begins with an **FIR (First Information Report)** lodged with the police, who investigate before a case is filed in court | A **petition** is filed before the relevant court by the affected party (e.g., either the landlord or the tenant in a rent dispute) |
| **What the court can do** | If found guilty, the accused can be sent to **jail** and/or **fined** | The court gives the **specific relief asked for** — e.g., ordering a flat to be vacated and pending rent to be paid |

## Does Everyone Have Access to the Courts?
In principle, **every** citizen of India can approach the courts — this is what "access to justice" means. But in reality, **access to courts has always been difficult for the vast majority of the poor in India.** Legal procedures involve significant money, paperwork, and time. For a poor person who cannot read and whose family depends on daily wages, the idea of going to court for justice often feels completely out of reach.

### The solution: Public Interest Litigation (PIL)
In response to this problem, the Supreme Court, in the early 1980s, devised a mechanism called **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** to widen access to justice.
- It allows **any individual or organisation** to file a case in the High Court or Supreme Court **on behalf of** people whose rights are being violated — even if the filer is not personally affected.
- The legal process was **greatly simplified**; even a simple **letter or telegram** addressed to the Supreme Court or High Court could be treated as a PIL.
- PILs have been used to secure justice on issues such as **rescuing bonded labourers** from inhuman work conditions, and **securing the release of prisoners** in Bihar who had been kept in jail even after completing their sentences.

### PIL in action: how the mid-day meal scheme was born
Did you know the mid-day meal served in government schools exists **because of a PIL**? Here's how it happened:
1. In **2001**, severe drought in Rajasthan and Orissa caused millions of people to face an acute shortage of food.
2. Meanwhile, **government godowns (storehouses) were overflowing with grain**, much of it being eaten away by rats — a case of "hunger amidst plenty."
3. An organisation called the **People's Union for Civil Liberties (PUCL)** filed a **PIL in the Supreme Court**, arguing that the **Fundamental Right to Life (Article 21)** includes the **Right to Food**, and that the government's excuse of not having adequate funds was clearly false, since the godowns were overflowing.
4. **The Supreme Court agreed** and ruled that the State has a duty to provide food to all. It directed the government to **provide more employment, provide food at cheaper prices through ration shops, and provide mid-day meals to children** — and appointed two Food Commissioners to monitor implementation.

### Another landmark case: Olga Tellis vs Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)
This case established the **Right to Livelihood as part of the Right to Life** (Article 21). In the judgment, the court explained that "life" under Article 21 means far more than mere animal existence — it includes the right to livelihood, because "no person can live without the means of living." The judges reasoned that evicting a person from a pavement or slum where they live *inevitably* deprives them of their livelihood (since people choose to live near their workplace), and this, in turn, deprives them of life itself.

**An important caution from the chapter:** Not every court judgment is seen as protecting the common person. Activists working on the right to shelter and housing for the poor believe that more **recent judgments on evictions** are a "far cry" from earlier ones like *Olga Tellis* — while the 1985 judgment tried to protect slum-dwellers' livelihoods, some recent rulings instead treat slum-dwellers merely as "encroachers." This shows that judicial interpretation isn't fixed forever — it can shift over time.

## The Problem of Delay: "Justice Delayed is Justice Denied"
Even when people *can* access the courts, another serious problem affects them: cases can take an **extremely long time** to be heard. This extended delay is captured in the well-known phrase: **"Justice delayed is justice denied."**

**A striking real example:** The Hashimpura case — 43 Muslims from Hashimpura, Meerut were killed on 22 May 1987. Their families fought for justice for **over 31 years.** Due to long delays, the Supreme Court transferred the trial from Uttar Pradesh to Delhi in September 2002; by 2007, only three prosecution witnesses had been examined. The Delhi High Court finally convicted the accused only on **31 October 2018** — 31 years after the crime.

**Data on judicial vacancies (as of 19 December 2022):**
| Court | Sanctioned strength | Working strength | Vacancies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supreme Court | 34 | 34 | 0 |
| High Courts | 1,108 | 775 | 333 |
| District & Subordinate Courts | 25,042 | 19,192 | 5,850 |

These large vacancy numbers, especially in the lower and High Courts, are a significant reason why cases take so long to be resolved.

**Despite these challenges,** the chapter closes by affirming that the judiciary has played a crucial role in democratic India — serving as a check on the executive and legislature, and protecting citizens' Fundamental Rights. The Constituent Assembly's decision to build an independent judiciary into the Constitution's design has proven to be a correct and vital one.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Rule of law | The principle that laws apply equally to all persons, and fixed procedures must be followed when a law is violated |
| Judicial review | The Supreme Court's power, as final interpreter of the Constitution, to strike down laws that violate its basic structure |
| Acquit | The court declaring that a person is not guilty of the crime they were tried for |
| To Appeal | Filing a petition before a higher court to hear a case that has already been decided by a lower court |
| Compensation | Money given to make amends for an injury or loss |
| Eviction | The removal of persons from land or homes they are currently living in |
| Violation | Breaking a law, or the breach/infringement of a Fundamental Right |
| PIL (Public Interest Litigation) | A simplified legal mechanism, introduced by the Supreme Court in the early 1980s, allowing anyone to file a case on behalf of those whose rights are being violated |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. The judiciary enforces the **rule of law** through three roles: **dispute resolution**, **judicial review**, and **upholding the law and enforcing Fundamental Rights.**
2. An **independent judiciary** — free from control by the legislature and executive, with protected judicial appointments and tenure — is essential for fair, impartial decisions.
3. India's courts form a pyramid: **Subordinate/District Courts → High Courts → Supreme Court**, connected through the **appellate system**, with Supreme Court decisions binding on all other courts.
4. The **Laxman Kumar case (1985)** shows the appellate system in action — the Trial Court convicted, the High Court acquitted, and the Supreme Court delivered yet another, final verdict.
5. **Criminal law** (crimes against society, starts with an FIR) is different from **civil law** (disputes over individual rights, starts with a petition).
6. **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, introduced in the early 1980s, widened access to justice for the poor — leading to landmark outcomes like the **mid-day meal scheme** (from the PUCL right-to-food case) and the **Right to Livelihood** (Olga Tellis case).
7. **"Justice delayed is justice denied"** — long delays (worsened by judicial vacancies) and the high cost of legal procedures remain major obstacles to access to justice, as shown by the 31-year-long Hashimpura case.
