# Chapter 4: Growing Up as Boys and Girls

## The Big Idea (in one line)
The way boys and girls are told to behave, dress, play, and work is **taught by society** — it is *not* natural or fixed. And most societies unfairly **value men's work more than women's work**, which creates inequality.

---

## The key idea first: "gender roles" are made, not born
> **Analogy — the two water bottles:** Imagine two identical empty bottles. One you fill with orange juice and label "boy," the other with apple juice and label "girl." Later, someone says "See? Boys are *naturally* orange and girls are *naturally* apple!" But that's silly — *we* filled them. Society does the same thing: it pours different rules into boys and girls from birth, then pretends those differences came naturally.

From a very young age, society sends signals:
- Boys are given **cars** to play with; girls are given **dolls.**
- Girls are told to **dress a certain way** and **talk softly**; boys are told to be **tough.**
- These small, everyday things quietly teach children they will have **different futures** — and later this affects **what subjects they study and what careers they choose.**

Both a car and a doll are fun toys. So *why* is one "for boys" and one "for girls"? Because toys are a sneaky way of preparing children for the roles society expects them to play as adults.

---

## Two real examples: growing up is different in different places & times

### 🏝️ Samoa in the 1920s (Pacific islands)
- Children **did not go to school** — they learned by doing.
- As soon as babies could walk, **older children (as young as 5!)** looked after them — *both* boys and girls.
- At about age **9**, boys went off to learn **fishing and planting coconuts**; girls kept caring for little children until they were teenagers.
- As **teenagers**, girls got *more* freedom — they went on fishing trips, worked in plantations, and learned to **weave baskets.**
- Interesting twist: in the cooking-houses, **boys did most of the cooking work**, and girls only helped! (The opposite of what many Indian families assume.)

### 🏫 Madhya Pradesh in the 1960s (a small Indian town)
- From Class VI, boys and girls went to **separate schools.**
- The **girls' school** had a protected central courtyard where they played "in total seclusion and safety."
- The **boys' school** had an open playground.
- After school, **boys used the streets** to hang around, play, and do bicycle tricks. **Girls walked straight home in groups**, moving quickly — carrying a **fear of being teased or attacked.**

> **The lesson:** There is **no single "natural" way** to grow up. In Samoa boys cooked; in 1960s India the streets belonged to boys and girls hurried home. This proves gender roles are decided by *society*, and they *change* across time and place.

> **A question worth thinking about:** Why are there fewer women and girls out on the streets, parks, and markets late in the evening? (Not because girls "don't like" it — but because society has made public spaces feel unsafe for them.)

## The big story: "My Mother Does Not Work" 👩‍🍳
Two friends, **Shonali and Harmeet**, argue. Harmeet says his mother "**doesn't work**" — she's a housewife. Shonali is shocked: "But Jaspreet aunty is up at **5 a.m.** doing ALL the housework!" Harmeet shrugs: *"That's not real work, it's just housework."*

So Jaspreet (Harmeet's mother) decides to go **on strike for one day** to prove a point. Watch what happens:

| Time | What went wrong when Mom stopped working |
|---|---|
| 7:30 a.m. | No breakfast ready, children not dressed, the domestic helper Mangala also on leave — Dad has to drive kids to school himself |
| Morning | Lunch boxes forgotten! Dad has to give extra money |
| 6:00 p.m. | Dad is **exhausted**, has to make his *own* tea, can't even find the tea leaves |
| That evening | "The house looks like it was hit by a hurricane!" — and guests are coming for dinner |

> **The punchline:** Jaspreet's "invisible" housework was actually holding the entire family together. When she stopped, everything collapsed in a single day. **Housework IS work** — hard, essential work — it's just not *seen* or *paid*, so society pretends it isn't work at all.

---

## Three things society refuses to see about housework
The chapter gives housework three important labels:

1. **Invisible** — it's done quietly at home, unpaid, so no one counts it as "work."
2. **Physically demanding** — fetching water, carrying heavy firewood headloads, bending to wash and sweep, standing for hours at a hot stove. These are "strenuous" tasks — the very word we usually reserve for men's jobs!
3. **Time consuming** — it eats up huge amounts of time, leaving women far less leisure than men.

---

## The proof in numbers 📊 (Study by India's Central Statistical Organization, 1998–99)
| State | Women: paid work hrs/week | Women: housework hrs/week | **Women TOTAL** | Men: paid work | Men: housework | **Men TOTAL** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haryana | 23 | 30 | **53** | 38 | 2 | **40** |
| Tamil Nadu | 19 | 35 | **54** | 40 | 4 | **44** |

**Read the last columns:** In both states, **women work MORE total hours than men** — because on top of any paid job, they do almost all the housework. This is called the **"double burden"** — women carry two loads: work outside *and* work inside the home.

---

## The lives of domestic workers 🧹
Housework doesn't only affect housewives. Many homes hire **domestic workers** (like Mangala) — to sweep, clean, wash, cook, and care for children/elders. **Most are women**, sometimes even young children.
- Their **wages are low** because society doesn't value this work.
- Their day can start at **5 a.m.** and end at **midnight.**
- They often get **little respect.**

**Melani's story (a real domestic worker in Delhi):** Her first job was with a rich family. The *memsahib* shouted at her, gave her only tea and two dry rotis (never a third), made her work barefoot even in winter until her feet swelled with cold. Melani asks the question at the heart of this chapter:
> *"Did we not work all day? Did we not deserve to be treated with some respect?"*

---

## Why this is a government issue (not just a family one)
Here's the crucial jump: unfair treatment of women's work is **not** just one family's problem — it's part of a **larger system of inequality** across society. So it must be fixed by the **government** too, not left to individual families.

- The **Constitution** says being male or female should **never** be a reason for discrimination.
- But since inequality still exists in reality, the government takes **positive steps** to fix it:
  - Set up **anganwadis** (child-care centres) in villages, so mothers are freed to work and **girls can attend school** instead of babysitting siblings.
  - Passed a **law**: any organisation with **more than 30 women employees must provide crèche (baby-care) facilities** — helping more women work outside the home.

> **The connect-back:** When the burden of child-care and housework falls only on girls, they miss school (remember the Samoan and MP examples). Freeing girls from that burden = giving them an equal chance at education and careers. That's why gender equality and the government's job are linked.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Identity | A sense of who you are; a person can have several (a girl, a sister, and a musician all at once) |
| Gender roles | The behaviours, jobs, and rules society assigns to men vs women (learned, not natural) |
| Double burden | Women's situation of working *both* inside the home (housework) *and* outside |
| Care-giving | Looking after and nurturing others — has both physical and strong emotional parts |
| De-valued | Not being given due recognition for a task, so the work (and person) is treated as worth less |
| Anganwadi | A government child-care centre in villages |

## Quick Recap (6 points to remember)
1. Gender roles (how boys/girls should behave, dress, play, work) are **taught by society**, not natural — proven by how they differ in Samoa (1920s) vs Madhya Pradesh (1960s).
2. From a young age, toys, clothes, and rules quietly prepare children for different adult roles — affecting future studies and careers.
3. **Housework is real work** — the "strike" story shows how essential it is — but it's treated as invisible, unpaid, physically demanding, and time-consuming.
4. Women carry a **"double burden"**: data shows they work *more* total hours than men once housework is counted.
5. **Domestic workers** (mostly women) do hard, long, low-paid work with little respect (Melani's story).
6. Because this inequality is society-wide, the **government** must act — via the Constitution, anganwadis, and crèche laws — to give women and girls an equal chance.
