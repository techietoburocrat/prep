# Chapter 8: A Shirt in the Market

> **Note:** In the current (2024–25) NCERT edition, this is **Chapter 8** and the **final chapter**. The PDF in this folder is named `08_Markets_Around_Us.pdf`, but that name is from an older edition — its actual content is *A Shirt in the Market*.

## The Big Idea (in one line)
Follow one shirt's journey — from a cotton farmer's field to a shop in America — and you'll see that at **every step, some people are squeezed (the poor) while others make huge profits (the rich and powerful).** The market is **not** fair to everyone.

---

## The journey of a shirt 👕 (the whole chain)
Let's trace one shirt from start to finish:

```
Swapna (cotton farmer) → Local Trader → Ginning Mill → Spinning Mill
   → Yarn Dealer → Weavers (Erode) → Cloth Merchant → Garment Export Factory (Delhi)
   → Foreign Businessperson (USA) → Shop → Customer
```

At **every arrow**, buying and selling happens. Now let's meet the people and see **who earns what.**

---

## Stop 1: Swapna, the cotton farmer in Kurnool 🌱
Swapna is a **small farmer** in Kurnool (Andhra Pradesh) who grows cotton.
- Before the season, she **borrowed ₹2,500** from a local trader (at a **very high interest rate**) to buy seeds, fertilisers, and pesticides — cotton farming needs lots of costly inputs.
- **The catch:** the trader made her **promise to sell all her cotton only to him.**
- At harvest, her cotton was worth ₹6,000 (at ₹1,500/quintal). But the trader **deducted ₹3,000** for loan + interest and paid her only **₹3,000.**

**Why couldn't Swapna argue?** She knew cotton should sell for at least **₹1,800/quintal** elsewhere, but:
- The trader is a **powerful man** in the village.
- She depends on him for **future loans** — for farming, illness, school fees, and survival when there's no work.

> **The trap (a debt cycle):** Swapna borrows → must sell only to the lender → gets underpaid → stays poor → must borrow again. Her earning from months of hard work was **barely more than a daily wage labourer's.** This is how the poor get **exploited.**

---

## Stop 2: The weavers of Erode & the "putting-out system" 🧵
Erode (Tamil Nadu) has one of the world's largest cloth markets. Here, **cloth merchants** control everything through the **putting-out system**:

**Putting-out system = the merchant supplies the raw material (yarn) and collects the finished cloth from weavers who work at home.**

- **Seems good for weavers:** they don't spend their own money on yarn, and they don't have to worry about selling the cloth.
- **But it's actually a trap:** because weavers depend on the merchant for *both* raw materials *and* the market, the **merchant holds all the power** — he decides what to make and pays a **very low price.** Weavers don't even know who will finally wear the cloth or at what price it sells.

**The harsh numbers:** A small weaver invests **₹40,000** (₹20,000 per loom × 2 looms). A weaver and one family member work **up to 12 hours a day**… and earn only about **₹3,500 a month.**

### The way out: Weaver's Cooperatives 🤝
> **A cooperative = people with a common interest joining together to work for their mutual benefit.**

In a weaver's cooperative, weavers **buy yarn together and do their own marketing** — cutting out the merchant, so they get a **fair price.** The government helps by buying their cloth (e.g., Tamil Nadu's **Free School Uniform** programme; government **Co-optex** stores sell handloom cooperative cloth).

---

## Stop 3: The garment factory near Delhi 🏭
The Erode cloth goes to a **garment exporting factory** near Delhi, which stitches it into shirts for **foreign buyers** (big store chains in the US and Europe).

**The foreign buyers are demanding:**
- They insist on the **lowest possible price**, the **highest quality**, and **on-time delivery** — punishing any defect or delay.

**So how does the exporter cope? By squeezing the workers:**
- The **Impex factory** has 70 workers, **mostly women**, mostly **temporary** (can be fired anytime).
- Highest paid are **tailors (~₹3,000/month)**; women doing thread-cutting, buttoning, ironing, and packing get the **lowest wages.**

> **The pattern repeats:** Powerful foreign buyers press the exporter → the exporter presses the workers → the workers (especially women) earn the least. The pressure always rolls **downhill onto the weakest.**

---

## Stop 4: The shirt in the United States 🇺🇸 — follow the money!
The finished shirt sells in a US shop for **$26 (about ₹1,800).** Let's break down where that ₹1,800 goes:

**What the US businessperson does:**
| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Buys shirt from Delhi exporter | ₹300 |
| Advertising | ₹400 |
| Storage, display, etc. | ₹200 |
| **His total cost** | **₹900** |
| **Sells shirt for** | **₹1,800** |
| **His PROFIT** | **₹900** 🤑 |

**What the Delhi garment exporter earns per shirt:**
| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sells shirt to businessperson for | ₹300 |
| Cloth & raw materials | −₹100 |
| Workers' wages | −₹25 |
| Office running cost | −₹25 |
| **His PROFIT** | **₹150** |

> **The shocking comparison:** The foreign businessperson pockets **₹900 profit** on one shirt. The exporter makes **₹150.** The worker who actually *sewed* the shirt earns just a tiny share of that **₹25** in wages. The person who did the **least physical work made the most money.**

---

## Who gains, and who doesn't? (Market and Equality)
Let's line everyone up:

| 🤑 Gained the MOST | 😟 Gained the LEAST |
|---|---|
| Foreign businessperson (huge profit) | Swapna, the cotton farmer |
| *(Garment exporter — moderate profit)* | Weavers of Erode |
| *(Merchants/traders — in between)* | Garment factory workers |

**The big lesson:** **Not everyone gains equally in the market.**
- On one side, the market gives people work and a chance to sell what they grow or make.
- On the other side, the **rich and powerful** (who own the factories, big shops, and large lands) take the **maximum earnings.**
- The **poor depend on the rich** — for loans (Swapna), for raw materials and selling (weavers), and for jobs (factory workers). This **dependence lets the poor be exploited.**

> **The democracy connection:** Democracy isn't only about voting — it's also about getting a **fair wage.** If people like Swapna and the weavers can't earn enough, how can they ever feel **equal** to others?

**Two ways to make markets fairer:**
1. **Form cooperatives** of producers (like the weaver's cooperative).
2. **Ensure laws are followed strictly** (fair wages, workers' rights).

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ginning mill | A factory that removes seeds from cotton bolls and presses the cotton into bales for spinning into thread |
| Exporter | A person who sells goods abroad |
| Profit | What's left from earnings after deducting all costs (if costs are higher, it's a **loss**) |
| Putting-out system | An arrangement where a merchant supplies raw material (yarn) and receives the finished product (cloth) from home-based weavers |
| Cooperative | A group of people with a common interest working together for mutual benefit |
| Exploitation | Taking unfair advantage of someone (e.g., paying the poor far less than their work is worth) |

## Quick Recap (6 points to remember)
1. A shirt travels through a long **chain**: cotton farmer → trader → ginning/spinning mills → weavers → merchant → garment factory → foreign buyer → shop → customer.
2. **Swapna** (cotton farmer) is trapped in **debt** by a powerful trader who underpays her — she earns barely more than a wage labourer.
3. **Erode weavers** are stuck in the **putting-out system**, where merchants hold all the power and pay very little (₹3,500/month for 12-hour days).
4. **Garment factory workers** (mostly women, temporary) get the lowest wages, as pressure from foreign buyers is passed down to them.
5. Following the money: the **foreign businessperson profits ₹900** per shirt, the exporter ₹150, while the actual makers earn the least.
6. **Markets are unequal** — the rich and powerful gain most, the poor are exploited; **cooperatives** and **strict laws** can make things fairer.
