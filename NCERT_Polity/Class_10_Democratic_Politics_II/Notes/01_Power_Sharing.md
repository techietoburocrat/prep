# Chapter 1: Power-sharing

## The Big Idea
Modern democracies don't let power rest with just one person, one group, or one organ of government. This chapter uses two contrasting real-world stories — Belgium and Sri Lanka — to show why sharing power is not a weakness but a genuine strength of democracy, and then maps out the different forms this sharing can take.

---

## Belgium and Sri Lanka: two very different paths

### Belgium
Belgium is a small European country (smaller in area than Haryana), with a population of just over one crore. Its ethnic and linguistic makeup is complex:
- **59%** live in the Flemish region and speak **Dutch.**
- **40%** live in the Wallonia region and speak **French.**
- **1%** speak **German.**
- In the capital, **Brussels**, 80% speak French and 20% speak Dutch — the *reverse* of the national picture, since the French-speaking minority nationally is a majority in the capital.

The minority French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful, which was resented by the Dutch-speaking community, who gained economic development and education much later. This caused rising tension between the two communities through the 1950s and 60s, especially acute in Brussels, where Dutch-speakers were a majority nationally but a minority locally.

**Belgium's response — accommodation:** Between 1970 and 1993, Belgian leaders amended their constitution **four times** to work out a power-sharing arrangement, one of the most innovative in the world:
- The Constitution requires the number of Dutch-speaking and French-speaking ministers in the central government to be **equal.**
- Many powers of the Central Government have been given to **State Governments** of the two regions — these State Governments are **not subordinate to** the Central Government.
- **Brussels has a separate government**, with equal representation for both communities — the French-speaking community accepted equal representation in Brussels in exchange for the Dutch-speaking community accepting equal representation in the Central Government.
- A unique third layer exists: a **"community government,"** elected by people belonging to one language community (Dutch, French, or German) *no matter where they live*, with power over cultural, educational, and language-related matters.

> **The result:** This arrangement is genuinely complicated, even for Belgians — but it worked. It helped avoid civic strife and a possible break-up of the country along linguistic lines.

### Sri Lanka
Sri Lanka is an island nation just off Tamil Nadu's coast, with about two crore people. Its major social groups are **Sinhala-speakers (74%)** and **Tamil-speakers (18%)** — the Tamils further divided into "Sri Lankan Tamils" (13%, native to the country) and "Indian Tamils" (brought as plantation workers during colonial rule). Most Sinhala-speakers are Buddhist; most Tamils are Hindu or Muslim; about 7% are Christians from both communities.

**Sri Lanka's response — majoritarianism:** Sri Lanka became independent in 1948. The Sinhala community's leaders sought to secure dominance through their majority, resulting in a series of **majoritarian** measures:
- **1956:** An Act was passed recognising **Sinhala as the only official language**, disregarding Tamil.
- Government policies favoured **Sinhala applicants** for university admissions and government jobs.
- A new constitution stipulated that the **state shall protect and foster Buddhism.**

These measures, one after another, deepened Tamils' sense of **alienation** — they felt the constitution and government policies discriminated against them, ignored their interests, and that no major Sinhala-led party was sensitive to their language and culture. Sri Lankan Tamils demanded recognition of Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy, and equal opportunity — but these demands were **repeatedly denied.** By the 1980s, several political organisations began demanding an independent **Tamil Eelam** (state) in the north and east.

**The result — civil war:** Growing distrust turned into a widespread **civil war.** Thousands from both communities were killed; many became refugees or lost their livelihoods; the war caused a terrible setback to Sri Lanka's social, cultural, and economic life. **The civil war ended in 2009.**

> **The core lesson from comparing the two:** Both are democracies, yet they handled power-sharing very differently. Belgium's leaders realised national unity was only possible by respecting the feelings and interests of different communities, leading to power-sharing arrangements. Sri Lanka shows the opposite: when a majority community tries to force its dominance and refuses to share power, it can **undermine the very unity of the nation** it claims to protect.

## Why is Power-sharing Desirable?

### 1. Prudential reasons: power-sharing produces better outcomes
- Power-sharing helps **reduce the possibility of conflict** between social groups, since imposing the will of a majority community may look attractive in the short run, but **undermines the unity of the nation** in the long run.
- **"Tyranny of the majority" is not just oppressive for the minority — it often affects the majority adversely too**, since unchecked, unstable, conflict-ridden governance harms everyone.

### 2. Moral reasons: power-sharing is the very spirit of democracy
- Democratic rule involves sharing power with those affected by its exercise, and who have to live with its effects. **People have a right to be consulted on how they are governed.**
- A **legitimate government** is one where citizens, through participation, acquire a genuine **stake** in the system.

> **Remember the distinction:** *Prudential* reasons stress that power-sharing brings *better outcomes*; *moral* reasons stress that the very *act* of power-sharing is valuable in itself, regardless of outcome.

## Khalil's Dilemma: a third example (Lebanon)
The chapter includes a thought-provoking story about **Lebanon**, where a young man named Khalil, born to an Orthodox Christian father and Sunni Muslim mother, grows frustrated with his country's power-sharing system. After Lebanon's own bitter civil war, its leaders agreed on strict power-sharing rules: the **President must be from the Maronite Catholic Christian sect**, the **Prime Minister from the Sunni Muslim community**, and the **Speaker from the Shi'a Muslim community.** Khalil, who doesn't identify strongly with either parent's religion, feels this rigid system locks him out of the country's top posts, and wonders why Lebanon can't just hold ordinary elections where "whoever wins maximum votes becomes president, no matter which community he comes from" — but his elders, who lived through the civil war, insist this careful power-sharing system is the best guarantee of peace.

> **Why this story matters:** It shows power-sharing arrangements are never perfect or permanent solutions — they involve real trade-offs (like Khalil's blocked ambitions) even while preventing worse outcomes (renewed civil war).

## Forms of Power-sharing
The idea of power-sharing developed in opposition to the old notion that all power must be concentrated in one person or group. In a democracy, since **people are the source of all political power**, and diverse groups and views deserve respect, political power should be distributed among as many citizens as possible. This can happen in **four main forms:**

### 1. Power shared among different organs of government (horizontal distribution)
Power is shared among the **legislature, executive, and judiciary**, placed at the same level, so no single organ can exercise unlimited power. Each organ **checks** the others, creating a **system of checks and balances.** (For example, ministers and officials exercise power but remain responsible to Parliament; judges are appointed by the executive but can check the legislature's laws and the executive's actions.)

### 2. Power shared among governments at different levels (vertical division)
A general government for the whole country (called a **federal** government — in India, the **Central/Union Government**) shares power with governments at the provincial/regional level (in India, **State Governments**). In such systems, the constitution clearly lays down the powers of each level — as Belgium did, though Sri Lanka refused to. This is studied further in the next chapter, on Federalism.

### 3. Power shared among different social groups
This is meant to give space in government and administration to diverse social groups who might otherwise feel alienated from the government — for example, Belgium's "community government," or, as studied earlier, the system of **reserved constituencies** in India's assemblies and Parliament for weaker/socially disadvantaged sections.

### 4. Power shared among political parties, pressure groups, and movements
In a democracy, citizens must have the freedom to choose among various contenders for power — in practice, this takes the form of **competition among different political parties**, ensuring power does not remain in just one hand. Sometimes parties form an alliance to contest elections; if elected, this becomes a **coalition government**, sharing power among multiple parties. **Interest/pressure groups** (traders, businessmen, industrialists, farmers, industrial workers) also get a share of influence over decision-making, through participation in government committees or by influencing policy.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethnic | A social division based on shared culture; people of the same ethnic group believe in their common descent due to similarities of physical type or culture, and need not share the same religion or nationality |
| Majoritarianism | The belief that the majority community should be able to rule a country in whatever way it wants, disregarding the wishes and needs of the minority |
| Civil war | A violent conflict between opposing groups within a country, so intense it appears like a war |
| Prudential | Based on prudence, or careful calculation of gains and losses — contrasted with decisions based purely on moral considerations |
| Horizontal distribution of power | Power shared among different organs of government (legislature, executive, judiciary) placed at the same level |
| Vertical division of power | Power shared among governments at higher and lower levels (e.g., Central and State governments) |
| Coalition government | A government formed by two or more political parties sharing power together |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. **Belgium** chose accommodation — through four constitutional amendments (1970-1993), it built a complex but successful power-sharing system among Dutch, French, and German speakers, including a unique "community government."
2. **Sri Lanka** chose majoritarianism — a series of Sinhala-favouring measures (the 1956 official-language Act, preferential jobs/education policies, state protection of Buddhism) alienated Tamils and led to a civil war that ended only in 2009.
3. Power-sharing is desirable for two kinds of reasons: **prudential** (it produces better, more stable outcomes and reduces conflict) and **moral** (it is the very spirit of democracy — people have a right to be consulted on how they are governed).
4. "Tyranny of the majority" harms not just the minority but often the majority too, by undermining national unity.
5. **Khalil's story (Lebanon)** shows power-sharing arrangements involve real trade-offs, even while preventing worse outcomes like renewed civil war.
6. There are **four forms of power-sharing**: among different organs of government (horizontal, via checks and balances), among governments at different levels (vertical, federal), among different social groups (e.g., reserved constituencies), and among political parties/pressure groups/movements (via competition and coalitions).
7. A **legitimate government** is one where citizens acquire a real stake in the system through participation — this is central to why power-sharing matters.
