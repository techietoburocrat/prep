## The Big Idea
Political parties are the most visible face of democracy — and therefore the most blamed for its problems. This chapter asks four questions: Why do we need parties? How many should a democracy have? What is wrong with parties (their challenges)? And how can they be reformed?

---

## Why Do We Need Political Parties?

### What is a political party?
A **political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in the government.** They agree on some policies and programmes for the society, with a view to promote the **collective good.** Since there can be different views on what is good for all, parties try to persuade people why their policies are better.

A political party has **three components:**
1. **The leaders**
2. **The active members**
3. **The followers**

Parties reflect fundamental political divisions in a society. A party is about a *part* of the society, and thus involves **partisanship** — it is known by which part it stands for, which policies it supports, and whose interests it upholds.

### Functions of political parties (what parties do)
1. **Parties contest elections.** In most democracies, elections are fought mainly among candidates put up by parties. (In the USA, members/supporters choose candidates; in India, top party leaders choose candidates.)
2. **Parties put forward different policies and programmes**, and voters choose from them. A party reduces a vast multitude of opinions into a few basic positions it supports. (A government is expected to base its policies on the line taken by the **ruling party.**)
3. **Parties make laws.** Formally laws are debated and passed in the legislature, but since most members belong to a party, they go by the party leadership's direction.
4. **Parties form and run governments.** Parties recruit leaders, train them, and make them ministers to run the government.
5. **Those that lose play the role of opposition** — voicing different views and criticising the government for its failures.
6. **Parties shape public opinion.** They raise and highlight issues; pressure groups are often extensions of parties; parties sometimes launch movements.
7. **Parties provide people access to government machinery and welfare schemes** — it is easier for a citizen to approach a local party leader than a government officer.

### Necessity: why can't democracies exist without parties?
Imagine a situation *without* parties:
- Every candidate would be **independent** — no one could make promises about *major policy changes* to the people.
- The government could be formed, but its **utility would remain uncertain.**
- Elected representatives would be **accountable only to their constituency** for local issues, but **no one would be responsible for how the country is run.**

The rise of political parties is **directly linked to the emergence of representative democracies.** Large societies need representative democracy; as societies became large and complex, they needed agencies to gather diverse views, present them to government, and bring representatives together so a **responsible government** could be formed. **Parties are a necessary condition for democracy.**

## How Many Parties Should We Have?
Any group of citizens is free to form a party, so there are a large number of parties (over **750 registered with the Election Commission of India**). But only a handful are serious contenders. The question is: how many *major/effective* parties are good for a democracy?

| Party system | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **One-party system** | Only one party is allowed to control and run the government | China (only the Communist Party allowed) — **not a democratic option**, since democracy requires at least two parties competing fairly |
| **Two-party system** | Power usually changes between two main parties; several others exist but only two have a serious chance of winning a majority | USA, UK |
| **Multiparty system** | Several parties compete, and more than two have a reasonable chance of coming to power, either on their own or in alliance | **India** |

**Alliances/Fronts:** When several parties in a multiparty system join hands to contest elections and win power, it is called an **alliance** or **front.** For example, in India's 2004 parliamentary elections there were three major alliances — the **National Democratic Alliance (NDA)**, the **United Progressive Alliance (UPA)**, and the **Left Front.**

> **Key conceptual point:** The multiparty system often appears messy and leads to political instability, but it allows a variety of interests and opinions to enjoy political representation. **No party system is ideal for all countries and all situations** — a party system is *not* something a country can choose; it evolves over a long time, depending on the nature of society, its social and regional divisions, its history, and its system of elections. India evolved a multiparty system because its huge social and geographical diversity is not easily absorbed by just two or three parties.

## National and State Parties

### Recognised parties and Election Commission criteria
Every party must register with the **Election Commission.** The Commission treats all parties equally but offers some special facilities to large, established parties — notably, these "**recognised parties**" get a reserved **election symbol** that only their official candidates can use.

**Criteria to be a recognised State party:** A party that secures **at least 6% of the total votes** in an election to the Legislative Assembly of a state AND wins **at least 2 seats** is recognised as a State party.

**Criteria to be a recognised National party:** A party that secures **at least 6% of the total votes in Lok Sabha or Assembly elections in four states** AND wins **at least 4 seats in the Lok Sabha** is recognised as a national party.

### The Six National Parties (as per Election Commission notification, 2023)
| Party | Founded | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Aam Aadmi Party (AAP)** | 2012 | Grew from the 2011 anti-corruption movement; stands for accountability, clean administration, transparency; formed governments in Delhi and Punjab |
| **Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)** | 1984, under Kanshi Ram | Represents the *bahujan samaj* (Dalits, Adivasis, OBCs, religious minorities); draws on Sahu Maharaj, Phule, Periyar, Ambedkar; main base in Uttar Pradesh |
| **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)** | 1980 (reviving the Bharatiya Jana Sangh of 1951) | Cultural nationalism ("Hindutva"); integral humanism & Antyodaya; wants uniform civil code, full integration of J&K; largest party (303 seats) in 2019; currently leads NDA at the Centre |
| **Communist Party of India–Marxist (CPI-M)** | 1964 | Marxism-Leninism; supports socialism, secularism, democracy; strong in West Bengal (ruled 34 years), Kerala, Tripura; critical of unchecked foreign capital |
| **Indian National Congress (INC)** | 1885 | One of the world's oldest parties; a **centrist** party (neither rightist nor leftist); secularism and welfare of weaker sections; led UPA government 2004–2019 |
| **National People's Party (NPP)** | 2013, under P.A. Sangma | First national party from North-East India; core philosophy is education and employment for all, empowerment of all sections; formed government in Meghalaya |

### State (regional) parties
Most other major parties are classified as **State parties** (commonly "regional parties") — though they need not be regional in ideology (e.g., Samajwadi Party, Rashtriya Janata Dal have organisation in several states; Biju Janata Dal, Sikkim Democratic Front, Telangana Rashtra Samithi are conscious of their state identity).
- Over the last three decades, the **number and strength of regional parties has expanded**, making Parliament more diverse.
- Since **no single national party has been able to win a majority in the Lok Sabha (until 2014)**, national parties are compelled to form alliances with State parties. Since 1996, nearly every State party has had a chance to be part of a national coalition government — this has **contributed to the strengthening of federalism and democracy.**

## Challenges to Political Parties
Popular dissatisfaction with parties focuses on **four problem areas:**

1. **Lack of internal democracy.** Power tends to concentrate in one or few leaders at the top. Parties don't keep membership registers, don't hold organisational meetings or internal elections regularly. Ordinary members lack information and influence; leaders make decisions in the party's name, and those who disagree find it hard to continue — **personal loyalty to the leader becomes more important than loyalty to party principles.**

2. **Dynastic succession.** Since most parties lack open, transparent procedures, ordinary workers have few ways to rise to the top. Leaders unfairly favour people close to them or their own family members — in many parties, top positions are controlled by one family. This is unfair to other members and **bad for democracy**, as people without adequate experience or support come to occupy positions of power. (Found even in older democracies.)

3. **Money and muscle power.** Since parties focus only on winning elections, they use short-cuts — nominating candidates who **have or can raise a lot of money.** Rich people and companies who fund parties gain influence over policies. In some cases, **parties support criminals** who can win elections. Democrats worldwide worry about the growing role of rich people and big companies in politics.

4. **Lack of meaningful choice.** To offer a meaningful choice, parties must be significantly different — but there has been a **decline in ideological differences** among parties in most parts of the world (e.g., Labour vs Conservative in Britain, or the major parties in India on economic policy). Those who want *really* different policies have no option; sometimes voters can't even elect very different leaders, because the same leaders **shift from one party to another.**

## How Can Parties Be Reformed?
Reforming parties is difficult because the final decision rests with the very leaders who would need to reform. Recent measures and suggestions:

### Measures already taken (laws and orders)
- **Anti-Defection Law:** The Constitution was amended to prevent elected MLAs and MPs from changing parties, because many were indulging in **defection** (switching parties) to become ministers or for cash. Now, **if an MLA or MP changes parties, they lose their seat** in the legislature. → This *brought defection down*, but it also made **any dissent even more difficult** — MPs/MLAs must accept whatever the party leaders decide.
- **Affidavit requirement:** The Supreme Court ordered that every candidate must file an **affidavit** declaring their **property and criminal cases pending.** → This made a lot of information public, but there's no system to verify the truth, and it's unclear if it has reduced the influence of money and criminals.
- **Election Commission order:** The EC made it mandatory for parties to hold **organisational elections and file income tax returns.** → Parties comply, but sometimes it's mere formality; it's unclear if internal democracy has genuinely improved.

### Further suggestions (not yet accepted)
- A **law to regulate parties' internal affairs** — compulsory membership registers, following their own constitution, an independent authority to settle disputes, and holding open elections to top posts.
- Making it mandatory for parties to give a **minimum number of tickets (about one-third) to women candidates**, and a quota for women in decision-making bodies.
- **State funding of elections** — the government giving parties money (in kind, like petrol/paper/telephone, or in cash based on votes secured) to support election expenses.

> **A note of caution:** We must be careful about *legal* solutions to *political* problems. **Over-regulation** can be counter-productive — it may force parties to find ways to cheat the law, and parties won't agree to pass a law they don't like.

### Two other, deeper ways to reform parties
1. **People can put pressure** on parties through petitions, publicity, and agitations. Ordinary citizens, pressure groups, movements, and the media play an important role. If parties feel they'd lose public support by not reforming, they'll take reform more seriously.
2. **Parties improve if those who want reform join politics.** The quality of democracy depends on the **degree of public participation** — it's hard to reform politics if good citizens don't take part and simply criticise from the outside. **The problem of bad politics can be solved by more and better politics.**

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Partisan | A person strongly committed to a party, group, or faction; marked by a tendency to take a side and an inability to take a balanced view |
| Ruling party | The political party that runs the government |
| Alliance / Front | Several parties joining hands to contest elections and win power together |
| Defection | Changing party allegiance from the party on which a person got elected, to a different party |
| Affidavit | A signed document submitted to an officer, containing a sworn statement of personal information (assets, criminal cases) |
| Anti-Defection Law | The law under which an MLA/MP who changes parties loses their legislative seat |

## Quick Recap (8 points to remember)
1. A **political party** is a group that contests elections and holds power, with three components — **leaders, active members, and followers** — and works to promote the collective good (involving **partisanship**).
2. Parties perform key **functions**: contesting elections, offering policies/programmes, making laws, forming/running governments, providing opposition, shaping public opinion, and giving access to welfare schemes — making them a **necessary condition for democracy.**
3. Party systems are of three kinds — **one-party** (not democratic), **two-party** (USA, UK), and **multiparty** (India); no system is ideal for all countries, as party systems *evolve* rather than being chosen.
4. **Alliances/fronts** (like NDA, UPA, Left Front in 2004) form when parties in a multiparty system join hands.
5. EC criteria: a **State party** needs ≥6% of state votes + 2 seats; a **national party** needs ≥6% of votes in four states + 4 Lok Sabha seats. There are **six recognised national parties (2023)**: AAP, BSP, BJP, CPI-M, INC, NPP.
6. The rise of **regional/State parties** has strengthened federalism, since national parties must ally with them to form coalition governments.
7. Parties face **four challenges**: lack of internal democracy, dynastic succession, money and muscle power, and lack of meaningful choice.
8. Reforms include the **Anti-Defection Law**, the **affidavit** requirement, and mandatory EC organisational elections — plus suggestions (regulating internal affairs, women's quota, state funding); but **legal over-regulation can backfire**, and the deepest reform comes from **public pressure and better participation.**
