# Chapter 7: Urban Administration

## The Big Idea (in one line)
Just as villages have a Panchayat, towns and cities have their own local government — called a **Municipal Corporation** or **Municipality** — that manages city services like water, roads, and garbage collection.

## Why do cities need a different kind of local government?
Cities are much bigger and more crowded than villages, with tall buildings, heavy traffic, and thousands (or millions) of people living close together. This requires a bigger, more organised local government to manage things like sewage systems, big roads, and large-scale garbage collection.

## Types of Urban Local Bodies
There are three types of urban local bodies in India, and which one a town gets depends mainly on its **population size**:

| Type | Where it applies |
|---|---|
| Municipal Corporation | Big cities with a large population (e.g., Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai) |
| Municipality / Municipal Council | Medium-sized towns with a smaller population |
| Nagar Panchayat | Very small towns that are just transitioning from a village into a town |

A city with a population of **more than 10 lakh (1 million)** is officially called a **metropolitan area**, and usually needs extra coordination between its Municipal Corporation and the surrounding smaller towns' municipalities.

## The 74th Constitutional Amendment (1992)
Just as the 73rd Amendment gave constitutional status to Panchayats in villages, a companion law — the **74th Constitutional Amendment**, which came into force on **1 June 1993** — did the same thing for towns and cities. It made urban local government a permanent, constitutionally guaranteed part of India's system, and made it compulsory to:
- Hold **municipal elections regularly, every 5 years.**
- **Reserve seats for women** (at least one-third) and for **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes**, in proportion to their population in that town.
- Set up **Ward Committees** in larger cities, so that even within one big Municipal Corporation, each ward (or a group of wards) has its own smaller committee to look after very local issues.

### The Twelfth Schedule — what subjects can Municipalities handle?
The 74th Amendment added a new **Twelfth Schedule** to the Constitution — a list of **18 subjects** that state governments can hand over to Municipal Corporations and Municipalities to plan and manage. These include:
- Urban planning, and regulation of land use and construction of buildings
- Water supply for domestic, industrial, and commercial purposes
- Public health, sanitation, and solid waste management
- Fire services
- Urban forestry, protection of the environment, and promotion of ecological aspects
- Slum improvement and upgradation
- Urban poverty alleviation
- Provision of urban amenities like parks, gardens, and playgrounds
- Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries
- Public conveniences, including public toilets

Just like with Panchayats, not every Municipality actually handles all 18 subjects — it depends on how much the state government has **devolved (handed down)** to it.

## How is a City Divided and Governed?
- A city is divided into smaller areas called **wards**.
- People in each ward vote to elect one representative, called a **Councillor**.
- All the Councillors together form the **Municipal Corporation / Council**.

### Key People and Their Roles
| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Councillor** | Elected representative of one ward; raises local issues and represents residents |
| **Mayor** | The head of the Municipal Corporation; mostly holds a ceremonial (symbolic/honorary) role in most Indian cities |
| **Commissioner** | The actual executive/administrative head; an appointed government officer (not elected) who is responsible for implementing decisions and running the day-to-day administration |

So while the Mayor is often the public face of the city's government, it is usually the **Commissioner** (an appointed officer) who manages the real day-to-day functioning of the Municipal Corporation.

## What Does the Municipal Corporation Actually Do?
The Municipal Corporation/Municipality is responsible for many essential city services, including:
- **Water supply** to homes and buildings.
- **Sanitation** and sewage systems.
- **Garbage collection and disposal.**
- **Building roads, streetlights, and maintaining them.**
- **Sanctioning (approving) building plans** — checking and approving construction plans before people build houses or buildings, to ensure safety rules are followed.
- **Maintaining parks and other public spaces.**
- **Registering births and deaths.**
- Running some **primary schools and public hospitals/dispensaries**, along with the state government.
- General **public health** measures, like controlling the spread of diseases.

## Where does the money come from?
The Municipal Corporation gets funds mainly through:
- **Property tax** (tax paid by owners of houses/buildings).
- **Water tax / other local taxes and fees.**
- **Grants from the state government.**

## A Real Example from the Chapter: The Garbage Problem
The textbook uses a story about a neighbourhood where garbage is not collected regularly, causing bad smells and health problems for residents. Frustrated residents:
- Complain to their local **Councillor**.
- The Councillor raises the issue with the Municipal Corporation.
- This shows how ordinary citizens can get civic problems fixed by contacting their elected Councillor, who works with the Corporation's officers to solve the issue.

## Street Vendors and the City
Every Indian city has thousands of **street vendors** — people who sell things like food, fruit, clothes, or small household items from a cart, stall, or basket on the street, without a permanent shop. Street vendors are an essential part of city life: they provide affordable goods within easy reach of people's homes and workplaces, and this is often their only source of livelihood.

For a long time, street vendors had no legal protection — they could be chased away or have their goods confiscated by municipal staff or police at any time, even though they were doing honest work. To fix this, the Indian government passed the **Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014.** This law:
- Requires the Municipal Corporation to set up a **Town Vending Committee**, which includes government officials as well as street vendors themselves, to survey and officially register vendors.
- Gives every registered vendor a **Certificate of Vending**, which is legal proof that they have the right to vend in a specific spot.
- Protects vendors from being evicted or having their goods confiscated **without proper notice**, once they are registered.

## Sanitation Workers and Dignity of Labour
The chapter also highlights the essential and often under-appreciated work of **sanitation workers** — the people who collect garbage and keep the city clean.
- Their work is **extremely important** for public health — without them, cities would be full of disease-causing waste.
- However, this work is sometimes looked down upon in society, even though every kind of honest work deserves **respect and dignity**.
- This connects back to the idea from Chapter 2 (Diversity and Discrimination) — no one should be discriminated against because of the job they do.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Municipal Corporation | The local government body for a big city |
| Municipality / Municipal Council | The local government body for a smaller town |
| Ward | A small division of a city/town, electing one Councillor |
| Councillor | Elected representative of one ward in the city |
| Mayor | Head of the Municipal Corporation, mostly a ceremonial role |
| Commissioner | Appointed officer who runs the actual day-to-day administration |
| Property Tax | Tax paid by property/building owners, a major source of municipal funds |
| Sanitation | Systems and practices that keep an area clean and hygienic |

| Twelfth Schedule | The list of 18 subjects (added by the 74th Amendment) that states can hand over to Municipalities |
| Ward Committee | A smaller committee for one ward or a group of wards within a big city, set up to handle very local issues |

## Quick Recap (8 points to remember)
1. Urban local government has three types — Municipal Corporation (big cities), Municipality/Municipal Council (medium towns), and Nagar Panchayat (very small towns) — decided mainly by population.
2. The **74th Constitutional Amendment (1992)** gave urban local bodies constitutional status, mandated elections every 5 years, reserved seats for women and SC/ST, and created **Ward Committees** in big cities.
3. It also added the **Twelfth Schedule** — a list of 18 subjects (water supply, sanitation, urban planning, slum improvement, and more) that states can devolve to Municipalities.
4. Cities are divided into wards; each ward elects a Councillor, and all Councillors form the Corporation/Council.
5. The Mayor is mostly a ceremonial head, while the Commissioner (an appointed officer) actually manages daily administration.
6. Municipal Corporations provide services like water supply, sanitation, garbage collection, roads, parks, and approving building plans; funded mainly by property tax and government grants.
7. The **Street Vendors Act, 2014** protects vendors' livelihoods through a Town Vending Committee and a Certificate of Vending, preventing arbitrary eviction.
8. Sanitation workers do essential work for public health and deserve dignity and respect, just like any other job.
