# Chapter 2 · Practice Set 01 — Origins of the Idea (1934–1946)

[← Chapter Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Set 02 →](Set_02.md)

**25 questions · 30 minutes · +2 / −0.66 · Level 🟢 Foundation**
**Covers:** M.N. Roy's proposal · Congress demand · Nehru's 1938 declaration · the missions · the Cabinet Mission Plan's composition formula

> **The idea behind this set.** The Constituent Assembly did not appear in 1946 out of nowhere — it is the last link in a twelve-year chain, and every link in that chain is separately examinable. The chain also carries a built-in irony: Nehru in 1938 demanded an Assembly on **adult franchise**; what arrived in 1946 was **indirect election on a limited franchise**. That gap is the seed of every criticism you will meet in Set 10.

🔥 = high probability · 📰 = current-affairs linked · ⚠️ = classic trap

---

### 1. 🔥
Who among the following was the first to propose the idea of a Constituent Assembly for India?

- (a) Jawaharlal Nehru
- (b) M.N. Roy
- (c) Mahatma Gandhi
- (d) B.R. Ambedkar

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **M.N. Roy**, a pioneer of the communist movement in India, floated the idea of a Constituent Assembly in **1934** — before any mainstream party had adopted it.

**Why not the others:** Nehru gave the demand its sharpest definition in **1938** ("elected on adult franchise, without outside interference") but did not originate it. Gandhi never headed this particular demand. Ambedkar's role begins only in 1947, as Drafting Committee chairman.

> 🔑 **Concept:** The idea began outside the Congress mainstream and was adopted by it only a year later. Movements often borrow their sharpest institutional ideas from their margins before making them official policy.

</details>

---

### 2. 🔥
In which year did the Indian National Congress officially demand a Constituent Assembly for India for the first time?

- (a) 1934
- (b) 1935
- (c) 1938
- (d) 1940

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The **Congress officially adopted the demand in 1935**, a year after Roy first proposed it.

**Why not the others:** 1934 is Roy's individual proposal, not a party demand. 1938 is Nehru's later, sharper definition of what such an Assembly should look like. 1940 is when the British first conceded the demand in principle, in the August Offer.

> 🔑 **The four-word chain:** **Roy proposed (1934) → Congress demanded (1935) → Nehru defined (1938) → Britain conceded (1940).** Each step moves the idea one level up — individual, then party, then precise content, then adversary's acceptance.

</details>

### 3. 🔥 ⚠️
In 1938, Jawaharlal Nehru declared that the Constitution of free India must be framed by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of

- (a) separate electorates, to protect minority interests
- (b) adult franchise, without any outside interference
- (c) proportional representation among the princely states alone
- (d) nomination by the outgoing provincial legislatures

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Nehru's 1938 declaration insisted on an Assembly elected on **adult franchise**, with **no outside interference** — meaning no British hand in shaping it.

**Why not (a):** Separate electorates were the very device the nationalist movement was fighting, not a demand of its own leadership.

**Why not (d):** Nomination is close to what was *actually delivered* in 1946 — which is exactly why it is a trap here. The question asks what was **demanded**, not what was received.

> ⚠️ **The gap that defines the whole chapter.** Nehru demanded adult franchise in 1938. What the Cabinet Mission Plan delivered in 1946 was indirect election by provincial legislators, chosen on the limited franchise of the Government of India Act, 1935. That gap between demand and delivery is the single fact every criticism in Set 10 traces back to.

</details>

### 4.
The demand for a Constituent Assembly was first conceded **in principle** by the British through the

- (a) Cripps Mission, 1942
- (b) August Offer, 1940
- (c) Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946
- (d) Government of India Act, 1935

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The **August Offer of 1940** was the first British document to accept the demand for a Constituent Assembly, even though only in principle and without any concrete machinery.

**Why not (a):** The Cripps Mission of 1942 went further — it proposed an actual **constitution-making body** after the war — but that is a second, more concrete step, not the first concession.

**Why not (c):** The Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 is where the Assembly was finally **constituted** — the last step in the chain, not the first.

> 🔑 **Three different verbs, three different Acts:** 1940 **conceded** the idea; 1942 **proposed** a body; 1946 **constituted** it. If a question asks which one merely "accepted in principle," the answer is always 1940.

</details>

### 5. 🔥
The Cripps Mission's proposal for a constitution-making body was rejected primarily by the

- (a) Indian National Congress, over the question of dominion status
- (b) Muslim League, which wanted a separate constitution-making process for Muslim-majority areas
- (c) princely states, who refused to send any representatives
- (d) Depressed Classes, over the absence of separate electorates

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Muslim League rejected the Cripps proposal because it wanted the option of a **separate constitution-making body for Muslim-majority provinces** — an early demand for what would eventually become Pakistan's own Constituent Assembly.

**Why not (a):** Congress's objections to Cripps centred on the *scope* of the offer (dominion status only after the war, and provinces free not to accede) — this is genuine Chapter 1 material, but the *League's* rejection is the fact this chapter needs.

> 🔑 **Cross-reference:** This is the same Cripps Mission from Chapter 1 — one offer, two different rejections, for two different reasons. Congress objected to *when and how much*; the League objected to the body being *singular* at all.

</details>

### 6. 🔥
The Constituent Assembly of India was finally constituted under the scheme recommended by the

- (a) Government of India Act, 1935
- (b) August Offer, 1940
- (c) Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946
- (d) Indian Independence Act, 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** The Assembly was constituted in **November 1946** strictly under the scheme laid out by the **Cabinet Mission Plan**.

**Why not (d):** The 1947 Act did not create the Assembly — it **transformed** its status, making it fully sovereign and letting it double as the interim legislature. Creation and transformation are two different facts, tested separately.

> 🔑 **Concept:** "Constituted under" and "made sovereign by" are two different verbs attached to two different statutes, four months apart. UPSC tests exactly this seam.

</details>

### 7. 🔥
Under the scheme of the Cabinet Mission Plan, the total strength of the Constituent Assembly was fixed at

- (a) 296
- (b) 389
- (c) 299
- (d) 320

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **389** — of which **296** were to represent British Indian provinces and **93** the princely states.

**Why not (a):** 296 is only the provincial share, not the total — a classic partial-number trap.

**Why not (c):** 299 is the strength **after Partition**, once Muslim League members withdrew — a later figure, not the original one.

> 🔑 **Three numbers, three moments — memorise them as a sequence:** **389** (original total, 1946) → **296 + 93** (its two halves) → **299** (post-Partition, once the League's members left for Pakistan's own Assembly).

</details>

### 8. 🔥 ⚠️
After Partition, the strength of the Constituent Assembly of India was reduced to

- (a) 296
- (b) 389
- (c) 299
- (d) 284

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **299**, once the Muslim League's members withdrew to join Pakistan's separate Constituent Assembly.

**Why not (a):** 296 was the original provincial share *before* Partition, not the post-Partition total — provinces themselves were split (Punjab and Bengal), so the post-Partition figure is not simply the old provincial number.

**Why not (d):** **284** is a different number entirely — it is the count of members who **signed the Constitution** on 26 November 1949, not the sitting strength after Partition.

> ⚠️ **Three "member count" numbers get confused constantly: 389 → 299 → 284.** Original total, post-Partition total, and signatories on adoption day. Keep them in that order and in that shrinking direction.

</details>

### 9. 🔥
Seats in the Constituent Assembly were allotted to each British Indian province roughly in proportion to its population, at approximately

- (a) one seat per five million people
- (b) one seat per one million people
- (c) one seat per five hundred thousand people
- (d) one seat per two million people

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Roughly **one seat per million** of population — the basic formula behind the 296 provincial seats.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This ratio is why larger provinces like the United Provinces sent far more members than smaller ones — representation was population-proportional at the *provincial* level, even though the *national* electorate was never universal. Proportionality within an unequal franchise is exactly the tension critics later attacked.

</details>

### 10. 🔥
Within each province, seats were further divided among three communities in proportion to their population. Those three communities were

- (a) Hindus, Muslims and Christians
- (b) General, Muslims and Sikhs
- (c) General, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes
- (d) Hindus, Muslims and the Depressed Classes

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **General, Muslims and Sikhs** — the three communal categories the Cabinet Mission Plan used to divide provincial seats.

**Why not (c):** Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes were **not** a separate communal category for Assembly elections; they fell within the "General" seats, though the Assembly later created an Advisory Committee specifically to safeguard their interests.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This three-way communal division is a leftover of the separate-electorate logic from the Government of India Act, 1935 — even a body about to write a Constitution abolishing separate electorates was itself elected along communal lines. The irony is worth remembering for a Mains answer on criticisms.

</details>

### 11. 🔥 ⚠️
Representatives of each community in the Constituent Assembly were elected by

- (a) the direct vote of all adult members of that community
- (b) the members of that community in the Provincial Legislative Assembly
- (c) nomination by the Governor of the province
- (d) the Indian National Congress's central leadership

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Community representatives were elected by the members of **that community sitting in the Provincial Legislative Assembly** — an indirect election, one level removed from the general public.

**Why not (a):** This is the single most-tested trap in the whole chapter. The general public never directly voted for Constituent Assembly members.

> ⚠️ **Asked directly as [Prelims 2013 Q2]** — the correct answer was exactly this indirect route through the Provincial Legislative Assemblies. If you remember only one fact from this set, make it this one.

</details>

### 12. 🔥
The method used to elect representatives of each community within the Provincial Legislative Assembly was

- (a) simple majority, first-past-the-post
- (b) proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote
- (c) block voting by the entire Assembly
- (d) a show of hands, recorded by the Speaker

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **Proportional representation with the single transferable vote (PR-STV)** — the same method still used today to elect the President of India and members of the Rajya Sabha from State Assemblies.

> 🔑 **Concept — a method that outlived the Assembly that first used it.** PR-STV was chosen because it lets a minority within the electing body still secure representation proportional to its numbers. The Constituent Assembly used it to elect itself, and the Constitution it wrote then prescribed the same method for the Rajya Sabha and the President — a rare case of a process becoming a permanent constitutional principle.

</details>

### 13. 🔥
How were representatives of the princely states chosen for the Constituent Assembly?

- (a) Elected by the subjects of each princely state
- (b) Nominated by the heads of the princely states
- (c) Elected by the Chamber of Princes as a single body
- (d) Appointed by the Constituent Assembly itself

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Princely state representatives were **nominated by the rulers** of the respective states — not elected by any process at all.

**Why not (c):** The Chamber of Princes was a consultative body (created in 1921, covered in Chapter 1) with no power to elect anyone to the Constituent Assembly.

> 🔑 **Concept — three different routes into one Assembly.** General/Muslim/Sikh seats: elected indirectly via PR-STV. Princely state seats: nominated by a ruler with no election at all. The same body, the most and least democratic entry routes sitting side by side — a fact worth quoting when discussing representativeness.

</details>

### 14.
Consider the following statements about the composition of the Constituent Assembly under the Cabinet Mission Plan:

1. Provincial seats were allotted roughly in proportion to population.
2. Seats within a province were divided among General, Muslim and Sikh communities.
3. Princely state representatives were elected by universal adult suffrage within each state.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statements 1 and 2 are correct.

**Why not 3:** Princely state representatives were **nominated by the rulers**, not elected by any suffrage, universal or otherwise — the exact opposite of statement 3.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** A statement that inserts "universal adult suffrage" anywhere in this chapter is worth checking twice — genuine universal suffrage does not arrive until Article 326 and the 1951–52 general election. Nothing in the Assembly's own formation used it.

</details>

### 15. 🔥
Arrange the following in correct chronological order:

1. Nehru declares the Constitution must be framed by an Assembly on adult franchise
2. M.N. Roy first proposes a Constituent Assembly for India
3. The Cabinet Mission Plan constitutes the Assembly
4. The Congress officially demands a Constituent Assembly

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 2 – 4 – 1 – 3
- (b) 4 – 2 – 1 – 3
- (c) 2 – 1 – 4 – 3
- (d) 2 – 4 – 3 – 1

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Roy **1934** → Congress **1935** → Nehru **1938** → Cabinet Mission **1946**.

**Why not the others:** Every wrong option either puts the Congress demand before Roy's individual proposal (impossible, since the Congress adopted an idea that already existed) or puts the Cabinet Mission somewhere other than last, when it is by definition the final, delivering step in this chain.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** In a chain-of-causation chronology like this one, the *causing* body (an individual) almost always precedes the *institutional* body (a party), which in turn precedes the *delivering* event (a government scheme). Structure, not just dates, can settle the order.

</details>

### 16. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Constituent Assembly of India was not directly elected by the adult population of the country.

**Statement-II:** Representatives of British Indian provinces were elected indirectly by members of the Provincial Legislative Assemblies, which were themselves elected on the limited franchise of the Government of India Act, 1935.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Both are correct, and II is exactly why I is true. Two layers of indirection compound: the electors themselves (provincial legislators) were chosen on a franchise covering only a small fraction of the adult population, and the Assembly's members were then chosen by *those* electors rather than by the public.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This is the mechanism behind the most common criticism of the Assembly — not that it was *undemocratic in spirit*, but that it was **two removes** from the electorate: limited franchise chooses legislators, legislators choose Assembly members. Being able to explain the mechanism, not just cite the criticism, is what separates a strong Mains answer from a weak one.

</details>

### 17.
Which one of the following provinces would, under the Cabinet Mission Plan's formula, be expected to send the **fewest** representatives to the Constituent Assembly?

- (a) A large, densely populated province
- (b) A small province with a low population
- (c) A province with an entirely Muslim population
- (d) A province with no princely states within its borders

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Since seats were allotted at roughly one per million of population, a small, low-population province would naturally send the fewest members — this is a direct application of the formula from Q9, not a new fact.

**Why not (c):** Religious composition affected *how* a province's seats were split internally (General/Muslim/Sikh), not *how many* seats the province received overall — that depended purely on population.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** Some questions test whether you can *apply* a formula rather than merely recall it. Whenever a question describes a hypothetical province, translate it back to the rule you already know instead of searching for a new fact.

</details>

### 18. 📰
Which one of the following best explains why the Assembly's own members later defended its legitimacy despite the limited franchise that elected it?

- (a) The Assembly's decisions were later ratified by a national referendum
- (b) The Assembly's legitimacy was said to rest on its deliberation and the breadth of opinion it accommodated, not on the breadth of its electorate
- (c) The British government formally certified the Assembly as fully representative
- (d) All members of the Assembly were elected unopposed, showing national consensus

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The standard defence — made by members of the Assembly itself and later formalised by scholars such as Granville Austin — is that legitimacy came from **how the Assembly worked** (public, extensive, accommodating debate over nearly three years) rather than from **how it was elected**.

**Why not (a):** There was no referendum on the Constitution, then or since — India's Constitution has never been popularly ratified by direct vote, unlike some other democracies'.

> 📰 **Why this still matters today.** This exact argument — legitimacy through deliberation rather than through electoral origin — resurfaces whenever a body's representativeness is publicly debated, from the Constitution Day discourse around **75 years of the Constitution in 2024** to any modern debate about the composition of drafting or review committees. It is a transferable argument, not just a historical footnote.

</details>

### 19.
The Cabinet Mission Plan's scheme for Constituent Assembly elections deliberately avoided using which one of the following?

- (a) Population as the basis for provincial seat allocation
- (b) Communal categories for dividing seats within a province
- (c) Direct election by the general population
- (d) Indirect election through existing legislatures

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Direct election by the general population is precisely what the scheme **avoided** — every route into the Assembly ran through an existing legislature or a ruler's nomination.

**Why not (a) and (b):** Both population-based allocation and communal division were **used**, not avoided — they are documented features of the scheme, covered in Q9 and Q10.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** A "which was avoided" question is really a "which of these features is absent" question dressed differently. Cross-check each option against the features you already know were present; whichever one is nowhere on that list is the answer.

</details>

### 20. 🔥
Which one of the following statements about the Constituent Assembly's relationship with the Government of India Act, 1935 is correct?

- (a) The Assembly was elected on a franchise that traces back to the 1935 Act
- (b) The Assembly was created by an express provision of the 1935 Act
- (c) The Assembly replaced the 1935 Act as India's working constitution the moment it was constituted
- (d) The 1935 Act was repealed on the day the Assembly first met

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The Provincial Legislative Assemblies that elected Constituent Assembly members were themselves elected on the franchise created by the **Government of India Act, 1935** — roughly 10% of the population, as covered in Chapter 1.

**Why not (b):** The 1935 Act contains no provision for a Constituent Assembly at all; it is a colonial statute with no concept of constitution-making by Indians.

**Why not (c):** The 1935 Act continued to be India's working constitution **even after independence**, until 26 January 1950 — the Assembly's existence did not displace it.

> 🔑 **Concept — the thread that connects Chapters 1 and 2.** The 1935 Act's restricted franchise is the invisible root of the Assembly's most-quoted flaw. A chapter that seems to be about 1946–1950 is, at its base, still explained by a law from eleven years earlier.

</details>

### 21.
Which one of the following best describes the overall shape of Constituent Assembly elections?

- (a) A single, uniform direct election across all of British India
- (b) A layered process — population-based provincial allocation, communal division within provinces, and election by sitting legislators
- (c) A lottery among registered voters in each province
- (d) Selection entirely by the outgoing Viceroy's Executive Council

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Three layers stacked on top of each other: population decided **how many** seats a province got, communal categories decided **how those seats split**, and sitting Provincial Legislative Assembly members decided **who filled them**. Every earlier question in this set is really about one layer of this same structure.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Whenever a chapter's facts feel scattered, look for the single structure that organises them. Here, three layers explain everything from Q7 through Q13 — learn the structure once and the individual facts become easy to reconstruct rather than needing separate memorisation.

</details>

### 22.
The Muslim League's decision to boycott the Constituent Assembly at its first sitting was consistent with its position during the

- (a) August Offer of 1940
- (b) Cripps Mission of 1942
- (c) Simon Commission's boycott of 1928
- (d) Poona Pact of 1932

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The League's objection to a *single* constitution-making body — first raised against the Cripps Mission in 1942 — is the same objection that led it to boycott the Assembly's first sitting in December 1946. It wanted a separate body, not merely better terms within one.

**Why not (c):** The Simon Commission boycott of 1928 (Chapter 1) was a joint nationalist protest against an all-British commission — a completely different issue, and one the League joined alongside Congress rather than opposed.

> 🔑 **Cross-reference:** This links Q5 of this set directly to the eventual boycott — the League's objection was consistent for four years, from Cripps in 1942 to the first sitting in December 1946. A position stated once and then acted upon later is a favourite structure for "consistent with" questions.

</details>

### 23.
Which one of the following is the correct sequence of British initiatives that progressively addressed the demand for Indian constitution-making, from the least to the most concrete?

- (a) August Offer → Cripps Mission → Cabinet Mission Plan
- (b) Cripps Mission → August Offer → Cabinet Mission Plan
- (c) Cabinet Mission Plan → August Offer → Cripps Mission
- (d) August Offer → Cabinet Mission Plan → Cripps Mission

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** August Offer **(1940, concedes in principle)** → Cripps Mission **(1942, proposes a body)** → Cabinet Mission Plan **(1946, actually constitutes it)**. Each step is both later in time and more concrete in substance — the chronological order and the order of increasing concreteness happen to coincide.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** When a question asks for an order by *degree* (least to most concrete, weakest to strongest, etc.) rather than by date, check first whether the chronological order already satisfies it — in a steadily escalating chain like this one, it usually does, and you can skip re-deriving a separate logical order.

</details>

### 24.
Which one of the following statements about the size of the Constituent Assembly is correct?

- (a) It remained fixed at 389 throughout its existence
- (b) It fell from 389 to 299 because of Partition, and further changed as individual members died or resigned
- (c) It increased after Partition as new members were nominated to replace departing Muslim League members
- (d) It was fixed permanently at 299 by the Cabinet Mission Plan itself

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Assembly's strength was never static — it fell to 299 after Partition, and continued to shift in smaller ways afterward, as individual members (including two Drafting Committee members, covered in Set 3) died, resigned, or were replaced.

**Why not (d):** The Cabinet Mission Plan fixed the *original* figure of 389; **299 is a post-Partition consequence**, not something the Plan itself specified.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Treat "the strength of the Assembly" as a variable across its life, not a single fixed fact — 389 at birth, 299 after Partition, and smaller fluctuations after that. A question can test any snapshot of that variable.

</details>

### 25. 🔥
Which one of the following best captures the constitutional significance of the twelve years between 1934 and 1946?

- (a) They were a period of pure agitation with no constitutional development at all
- (b) They show a demand progressively defined, conceded in principle, and then finally institutionalised — with the final institution falling short of the original demand
- (c) They show the British government consistently refusing any Indian role in constitution-making
- (d) They are significant only because they precede the 1947 transfer of power

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The twelve years trace a demand from an individual's proposal, through party adoption and precise definition, through British concession in principle, to actual institutional delivery — and the institution finally delivered (indirect election, limited franchise) fell short of what had been demanded (adult franchise, no outside interference).

**Why not (a) and (c):** Both describe a static picture — pure agitation, or pure refusal — when the actual twelve years show **movement on both sides**: the demand sharpening, and the British position shifting from silence to partial concession to actual delivery.

> 🔑 **The big idea of this set.** Everything in it — Roy, Congress, Nehru, the three missions, the final composition formula — is one continuous negotiation, not a list of unrelated facts. Read it as a negotiation and the individual dates almost memorise themselves, because each one is simply the next move in an argument you already understand.

</details>
