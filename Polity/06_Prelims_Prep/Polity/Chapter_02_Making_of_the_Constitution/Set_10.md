### 1. 🔥
Which one of the following is the standard counter-argument to the criticism that the Constituent Assembly was "not a representative body"?

- (a) The franchise used to elect it was, in fact, universal adult suffrage
- (b) Its legitimacy rested on the breadth of opinion it accommodated and the depth of its public deliberation, not on the breadth of its electorate
- (c) The criticism is factually baseless and has never been seriously made
- (d) The British government certified the Assembly as fully representative

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The standard defence concedes the **procedural** point (limited, indirect franchise) but argues legitimacy came from **how the Assembly worked** — nearly three years of open, accommodating debate covering every significant shade of opinion.

**Why not (a):** This denies a fact the criticism correctly relies on — the franchise was genuinely limited (Chapter 1's ~10% figure, via the Government of India Act, 1935) and election was genuinely indirect (Set 1). A good counter-argument concedes true facts and answers them, rather than denying them.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Notice the structure of a *good* defence — it does not dispute the criticism's facts, it reframes what those facts are supposed to prove. This is the model for every counter-argument in this set.

</details>

### 2. 🔥
The criticism that the Constituent Assembly was "not a sovereign body" is best answered by which one of the following facts?

- (a) The Assembly had always been sovereign, from its first sitting in December 1946
- (b) After the Indian Independence Act, 1947, the Assembly became fully sovereign and free to frame any constitution it wished, including the power to repeal any British statute
- (c) Sovereignty was never relevant to the Assembly's work
- (d) The United Nations formally recognised the Assembly's sovereignty in 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Assembly's sovereignty is a **1947** fact, not a **1946** one — it was created under a British-sponsored scheme (the Cabinet Mission Plan), but the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** transformed it into a fully sovereign body, free even to repeal the very Act that granted it that power (a fact developed fully in Chapter 1).

**Why not (a):** This is the trap — claiming sovereignty from the very beginning ignores the genuine, important legal transition that occurred specifically in 1947, which is the actual answer to this criticism.

> 🔑 **Cross-reference:** This is Chapter 1's "sovereignty" material, redeployed here as the direct answer to a Chapter 2 criticism — a clean example of why treating Chapters 1 and 2 as one continuous story, rather than two sealed units, pays off under exam pressure.

</details>

### 3. 🔥
Which one of the following is the standard counter-argument to the criticism that the Assembly was "time-consuming," having taken nearly three years?

- (a) The Assembly actually completed its work faster than any other constitution-making body in history
- (b) Comparable bodies elsewhere, such as the US Constitutional Convention, took far less time — but also produced a vastly shorter document for a far less diverse society
- (c) The delay was entirely the fault of British obstruction, which continued after 1947
- (d) The Assembly deliberately extended its work to increase members' allowances

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The honest counter-argument is comparative and proportionate, not a denial: the **US Convention took about four months**, but produced a **much shorter** document for a **far less diverse** society — India's Assembly, writing **395 Articles** for an immense and varied population, needed correspondingly more time.

**Why not (c):** Britain had no authority over a sovereign Assembly after 1947 (Q2) — this explanation is simply untrue for most of the period in question.

> 🔑 **Concept — the shape of the "proportionate effort" defence.** Do not just recall that the US took four months; recall **why** that comparison favours India once document length and social diversity are factored in. A bare number without its context proves nothing, and examiners know this.

</details>

### 4. 🔥
Which one of the following historians is associated with the observation that the Congress, despite dominating the Assembly numerically, was itself an "umbrella" containing wide ideological diversity?

- (a) Winston Churchill
- (b) Granville Austin
- (c) B.N. Rau
- (d) Ivor Jennings

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **Granville Austin**, the most cited historian of the Indian Constitution's making, made this point directly in answer to the "Congress-dominated" criticism — Congress's numerical dominance did not translate into ideological uniformity, since the party itself housed socialists, conservatives, Gandhians and more.

**Why not (a):** Churchill is associated with the **opposite** side of a different criticism — his sneer that the Assembly represented "only one major community in India," answered separately in Q5.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Keep the two named-critic facts apart: **Churchill** criticised the Assembly as **Hindu-dominated**; **Austin** *defended* it against the **Congress-dominated** charge. One is an attacker, one is a defender, and they are answering two different criticisms.

</details>

### 5. 🔥
Winston Churchill's criticism that the Constituent Assembly represented "only one major community in India" is most directly answered by which one of the following facts?

- (a) The Assembly had no Muslim members at all, proving the point
- (b) The Muslim League chose to boycott the Assembly; it was not excluded by the Assembly itself, which never closed its doors to any community
- (c) Churchill's remark was made after the Constitution was adopted and is therefore irrelevant
- (d) The Assembly amended its rules specifically to exclude Muslim members

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The League's absence was a matter of the League's **own choice** — a boycott, sustained from the first sitting onward (Set 1, Q22; Set 2, Q15) — not an act of exclusion by the Assembly, which remained formally open throughout.

**Why not (a):** The Assembly did include Muslim members who did not belong to the League — the absence was of the League specifically, not of the entire Muslim community.

> 🔑 **Concept:** The distinction between "excluded" and "chose not to attend" carries real weight here — the same underlying fact (few League voices in the Assembly) supports two very different judgements about the Assembly's own conduct, depending on which side of that distinction you land on.

</details>

### 6. 🔥
Which one of the following is the standard response to the criticism that the Assembly was dominated by "lawyer-politicians"?

- (a) This is denied outright — the Assembly had almost no lawyers among its members
- (b) The Assembly's legal expertise produced a long, precise, technical document — a strength as much as a flaw
- (c) Lawyer-politicians were deliberately excluded from the Drafting Committee
- (d) This criticism has no relevance to the finished Constitution's quality

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** This is a "double-edged" defence — the same characteristic (heavy legal expertise) that produces the criticism (a technical, lawyer's document, unlike shorter, more political constitutions elsewhere) is reframed as exactly what gave the Constitution its precision and durability.

**Why not (c):** The Drafting Committee was, if anything, **especially** dominated by lawyers — Ambedkar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar and others were all trained lawyers, which is a documented fact, not something to deny.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Notice that this defence, unlike the others in this set, does not distinguish a "true fact" from a "wrong conclusion" — it accepts both the fact and its usual negative framing, then supplies an *additional* positive framing alongside it. Two readings of one fact, both defensible, is itself a sophisticated exam-ready idea.

</details>

### 8.
Which one of the following criticisms is **not** among the six standard criticisms of the Constituent Assembly developed in this chapter?

- (a) Not a representative body
- (b) Dominated by the Congress
- (c) Excessive Fundamental Rights, insufficient Directive Principles
- (d) Hindu-dominated, per Churchill's remark

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** No such criticism of an imbalance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles appears among the six standard criticisms of the Assembly's **composition and process** — that kind of debate belongs to discussions of the **finished Constitution's content** (Parts III and IV), a different question from how the Assembly itself was formed and conducted.

**Why the others belong:** representativeness, Congress domination, and Churchill's remark are all genuine, named criticisms covered earlier in this set.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** Distinguish criticisms of **process** (how the Assembly was elected and how it worked) from criticisms of **content** (what the finished Constitution actually says). This chapter's six criticisms are entirely about process; content criticisms belong to later chapters.

### 9. 🔥
Which one of the following best frames a strong Mains answer on the Assembly's legitimacy, according to the approach this chapter recommends?

- (a) List every criticism flatly, with no attempt at synthesis
- (b) Argue the Assembly was "procedurally imperfect but substantively legitimate," and support that framing with specific evidence for both halves
- (c) Argue the Assembly faced no genuine criticism worth discussing
- (d) Argue the Assembly's legitimacy is entirely unresolved and unknowable

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** This exact framing — **procedurally imperfect, substantively legitimate** — lets a single sentence hold both the real limitation (limited, indirect franchise) and the real strength (extensive, accommodating deliberation and responsible conduct) without pretending either half does not exist.

> 🔑 **Concept:** A synthesis framing like this is worth more marks than a flat list of "criticism, then counter-criticism, then criticism" precisely because it shows you can hold two true things together and draw a conclusion from their combination — exactly what a 250-word Mains answer needs to do in its final paragraphs.

</details>

### 10.
Which one of the following statements correctly distinguishes the "not representative" criticism from the "not sovereign" criticism?

- (a) They are the same criticism, phrased differently
- (b) "Not representative" concerns how members were chosen; "not sovereign" concerns whether the Assembly had genuine constitution-making authority, independent of any external power
- (c) "Not sovereign" concerns how members were chosen; "not representative" concerns constitution-making authority
- (d) Neither criticism has ever actually been made against the Assembly

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** One is about **composition** (who got to choose the members, and how), the other about **authority** (whether the body's power to write a constitution was genuinely its own, or still subject to an external power). A body could in principle be representative but not sovereign, or sovereign but not representative — they are logically independent questions, even though both happen to apply to the Assembly.

</details>

### 11. 📰
Which one of the following best explains why the Assembly's legitimacy debate remains relevant to constitutional discourse in India today, well beyond its purely historical interest?

- (a) It has no ongoing relevance; the debate ended in 1950
- (b) The same underlying question — whether an institution's legitimacy depends more on how it is formed or on how it deliberates and conducts itself — recurs whenever the composition or process of a modern committee, commission or drafting body is publicly debated
- (c) It is relevant only to specialists in constitutional history
- (d) The debate was permanently settled by a Supreme Court judgment in 1950

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Assembly's legitimacy argument is a **transferable template** — "formed imperfectly, but legitimised by deliberation and conduct" is a framing invoked whenever a modern body's composition draws criticism, and the Assembly remains the most commonly cited historical precedent for making that argument.

> 📰 **Where this surfaces in practice.** Discussions around **Constitution Day (26 November)** each year, and especially the extensive coverage around **75 years of adoption in 2024**, routinely revisit this exact legitimacy debate — using the Assembly's own history as the benchmark against which modern institutional design and representativeness questions get measured.

</details>

### 12.
Which one of the following correctly identifies the relationship between this chapter's criticisms and the "kept, refused, added" framework used to analyse the Constitution's colonial inheritance in Chapter 1?

- (a) The two frameworks are unrelated
- (b) The Assembly's own composition inherited a colonial-era limited franchise (kept), while its final product — universal adult suffrage under Article 326 — deliberately broke from that inheritance (refused/added)
- (c) The Assembly refused to inherit anything from the colonial period
- (d) The Assembly's franchise was itself an entirely new invention with no colonial precedent

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Assembly's own limited, indirect franchise (Chapter 1's Government of India Act, 1935 franchise, inherited and "kept" for the Assembly's own election) sits in ironic tension with the Constitution it produced, which then **refused** that same limited-franchise principle and **added** universal adult suffrage instead.

> 🔑 **Concept — the sharpest single insight connecting Chapters 1 and 2.** The body elected under a colonial-era restricted franchise is the very body that abolished restricted franchise for everyone who came after it. This irony is worth a full sentence in any strong Mains answer on either chapter.

</details>

### 13. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why the Assembly's response to communal representation — abolishing separate electorates while retaining reserved seats — is relevant to evaluating its legitimacy?

- (a) It shows the Assembly refused to engage with minority representation at all
- (b) It shows a body elected partly along communal lines (Set 1's General/Muslim/Sikh formula) chose, once sovereign, to dismantle that very principle for the country's future, while still preserving targeted safeguards through reserved seats
- (c) It shows the Assembly retained separate electorates unchanged
- (d) It has no connection to the Assembly's own election

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** This mirrors the franchise irony from Q12 exactly, but for communal representation instead: the Assembly was itself elected using communal categories (Set 1), yet chose to abolish separate electorates for the country going forward (Article 325, Chapter 1), while keeping reserved seats as a narrower safeguard — a genuine act of self-correcting institutional design.

> 🔑 **Concept:** A body is not merely a product of the rules that created it — it can also examine and reject those same rules for the future. This is arguably the single strongest piece of evidence for the Assembly's legitimacy: it did not simply perpetuate the colonial system that elected it, it dismantled key parts of that system by its own sovereign choice.

</details>

- (b) The US Convention took longer than the Indian Assembly
- (c) The US Convention produced a document of comparable length and detail to India's Constitution

**Why not (d):** The comparison's validity rests on document length, scope and social diversity — not on the historical period in which each event occurred; nothing about comparing institutions across time makes a comparison automatically invalid.

### 15.
Which one of the following is the most balanced assessment of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's role in the Constitution's making, as the chapter frames it?

- (a) He was the sole author of the entire document, with no meaningful contribution from anyone else
- (b) He was the chief architect of the constitutional text as Drafting Committee chairman, working from B.N. Rau's initial draft, within a process that also depended heavily on Nehru's, Patel's and the wider Assembly's collective work
- (c) His role was purely ceremonial and had no bearing on the final text
- (d) He played no role until after the Constitution was adopted

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** "Chief architect of the **text**, not sole author of the **document**" is the chapter's own balanced formulation — Ambedkar's central role in turning Rau's draft into final Articles is real and well-documented, but it sat within, and depended on, an extensive collective process involving many others across the Assembly's committee structure.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This same "chief architect, not sole author" framing is a template for evaluating any single figure's role within a genuinely collective institutional effort — precise enough to give credit accurately, without either erasing a real central contribution or inflating it into sole authorship.

</details>

### 16.
Which one of the following best explains why the criticism that the Assembly was "Congress-dominated" is considered only a partial truth?

- (a) Congress numerically dominated the Assembly, but contained wide internal ideological diversity, so numerical dominance did not translate into uniform viewpoints
- (b) Congress had no members in the Assembly at all
- (c) The criticism is entirely false and has no basis whatsoever
- (d) Congress dominance only affected the Drafting Committee, not the wider Assembly

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The **numerical** fact (Congress dominance) is true and undisputed; what the counter-argument disputes is the **inference** typically drawn from it (that this dominance produced ideological uniformity) — Austin's "umbrella" observation (Q4) directly targets that inference, not the underlying number.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** "Partial truth" criticisms — true premise, contested conclusion — are the hardest kind to argue against by simple denial. The correct response, modelled throughout this set, is always to accept the premise and dispute only the inference drawn from it.

</details>

### 22.
Which one of the following statements correctly applies the "kept, refused, added" framework specifically to the Assembly's own composition (as distinct from the Constitution's content, covered in Chapter 1)?

- (a) Kept: indirect election via existing legislatures. Refused: separate electorates for the Assembly's own future successor bodies. Added: nothing, since the Assembly's composition had no lasting effect
- (b) Kept: the colonial-era limited franchise, for electing the Assembly itself. Refused and added: universal adult suffrage and the abolition of separate electorates, but only for the country's future, not retroactively for the Assembly's own composition
- (c) The framework applies only to Chapter 1 and has no relevance here
- (d) The Assembly refused every colonial-era practice from the outset, including for its own election

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Assembly's **own** composition genuinely **kept** colonial-era features (limited franchise, communal seat categories) — it could not retroactively re-elect itself on the fairer principles it was simultaneously writing into law for the future. The refusal and addition apply **forward**, to the country the Assembly was creating, not backward, to how the Assembly itself had already been chosen.

> 🔑 **Concept — the sharpest possible statement of this chapter's central irony.** The Assembly could change the rules for everyone who came after it, but it could not un-elect itself under fairer rules. Recognising that a reforming body's own origin often cannot benefit from the very reform it enacts is a genuinely sophisticated point, and one worth making explicitly in a strong Mains answer.

</details>
