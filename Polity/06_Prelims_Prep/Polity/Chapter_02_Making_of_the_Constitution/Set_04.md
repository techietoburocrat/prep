# Chapter 2 · Practice Set 04 — From Draft to Text: the Three Readings

[← Set 03](Set_03.md) · [Chapter Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Set 05 →](Set_05.md)

**25 questions · 30 minutes · +2 / −0.66 · Level 🟡 Prelims Standard**
**Covers:** publication of the Draft Constitution, Feb 1948 · the three readings · public consultation · 4 November 1948

> **The idea behind this set.** Between the Drafting Committee's formation (29 Aug 1947, Set 3) and the Constitution's adoption (26 Nov 1949, Set 5) lie **fifteen months of public and Assembly scrutiny**, structured as three distinct "readings." Most candidates know the two endpoint dates and nothing in between — this set fills exactly that gap, which is where UPSC increasingly probes.

🔥 = high probability · 📰 = current-affairs linked · ⚠️ = classic trap

### 1. 🔥
The Draft Constitution, prepared by the Drafting Committee, was published for public discussion in

- (a) August 1947
- (b) February 1948
- (c) November 1948
- (d) November 1949

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **February 1948** — about six months after the Committee was constituted (29 August 1947, Set 3).

**Why not (a):** That is when the Committee itself was formed, not when it finished a publishable draft — the two events are six months apart.

> 🔑 **The enactment spine, part one:** Committee constituted **Aug 1947** → Draft published **Feb 1948** → public discussion period → first reading **Nov 1948**. Each step follows the last by months, not days — a different rhythm from the tight December 1946 sequence in Set 2.

</details>

### 2. 🔥
After the Draft Constitution was published, the general public was given how long to discuss it and propose amendments?

- (a) 2 months
- (b) 4 months
- (c) 8 months
- (d) 12 months

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Eight months** — from February 1948 until the Assembly took up the Draft for its first reading in November 1948.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Eight months of public discussion, on top of the Assembly's own extensive internal debate, is exactly the kind of fact that answers the "time-consuming" criticism from a position of strength rather than defensiveness — the time was not merely deliberation among 299 members, but a genuine national consultation window before formal readings even began.

</details>

### 3. 🔥
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar formally moved that the Assembly take the Draft Constitution into consideration — the **first reading** — on

- (a) 26 November 1949
- (b) 4 November 1948
- (c) 15 August 1947
- (d) 29 August 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **4 November 1948.** This is the date the chapter notes call "the first reading" — Ambedkar formally placing the completed Draft before the whole Assembly for general discussion.

**Why not (d):** 29 August 1947 is when the Drafting Committee itself was **constituted**, over a year earlier — a different event entirely, and a trap this set repeats deliberately because Set 3 already flagged it as a common confusion.

> 🔑 **Three Ambedkar dates, three different actions — do not let them blur into one:**
> **29 Aug 1947** — Drafting Committee **constituted** (he becomes chairman).
> **4 Nov 1948** — he **introduces** the completed Draft (first reading).
> **26 Nov 1949** — the finished Constitution is **adopted**.

</details>

### 4. 🔥
The **first reading** of the Draft Constitution consisted of

- (a) a clause-by-clause examination of every Article
- (b) a general discussion of the Draft Constitution as a whole
- (c) the final vote adopting the Constitution
- (d) a reading confined to the Preamble alone

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The first reading was a **general discussion** — debating the Draft's overall shape and philosophy, not yet working through it line by line.

**Why not (a):** Clause-by-clause examination is precisely what the **second** reading consisted of (Q5) — a different stage with a different kind of scrutiny.

> 🔑 **Concept:** "First reading = general, second reading = detailed, third reading = final" is a structure borrowed from ordinary parliamentary legislative procedure (you will meet the same three-reading pattern for ordinary Bills in the Parliament chapter) — the Assembly used the same discipline on itself that it was busy writing into the rules for every future Parliament.

</details>

### 5. 🔥
The **second reading** of the Draft Constitution consisted of

- (a) a clause-by-clause discussion of every Article, with amendments moved and considered
- (b) a purely ceremonial re-reading of the first reading's speeches
- (c) discussion confined only to Fundamental Rights
- (d) a reading conducted exclusively by the Drafting Committee, without the full Assembly

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The **second reading** was where the real detailed work happened — every Article examined clause by clause, with members moving and debating amendments, spanning roughly a year, from November 1948 through much of 1949.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This is where the bulk of the "165 sitting days" and "114 days debating the Draft" figures you will meet in Set 6 actually accumulate — the second reading, not the shorter first or third readings, is the real engine of the Assembly's working time.

</details>

### 6. 🔥
The **third reading** of the Draft Constitution — its final passing — concluded on

- (a) 4 November 1948
- (b) 17 October 1949
- (c) 26 November 1949
- (d) 26 January 1950

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **26 November 1949** — the same date on which the Constitution was formally **adopted**. The third reading and the act of adoption are, in effect, the same climactic event: a final check of the whole text, followed by its passing.

**Why not (d):** 26 January 1950 is when the finished, already-adopted Constitution **came into force** — a wholly separate later event, covered fully in Set 5.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Notice that "third reading" and "adoption" collapse into a single date, unlike the first and second readings, which are spread over many months. The Assembly checked the whole text once more, then passed it — there was no separate gap between finishing the third reading and formally adopting the document.

</details>

### 7.
Which one of the following correctly orders the three readings of the Draft Constitution?

- (a) Clause-by-clause discussion → general discussion → final passing
- (b) General discussion → clause-by-clause discussion → final passing
- (c) Final passing → general discussion → clause-by-clause discussion
- (d) General discussion → final passing → clause-by-clause discussion

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **General discussion first** (see the whole forest), **clause-by-clause second** (examine each tree), **final passing last** (accept the whole forest again, now improved). This order is not arbitrary — it is the only sequence in which detailed amendment can meaningfully follow an agreed sense of the whole, and a final check can meaningfully follow detailed correction.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** When a process has an inherent logic — you cannot amend details before agreeing on the whole, and you cannot finally pass something before amending its details — you can derive the correct order from that logic alone, even if you have momentarily forgotten which reading came first by name.

</details>

### 8. 🔥
Consider the following statements about the enactment process of the Constitution:

1. The Draft Constitution was published in February 1948.
2. The public was given eight months to discuss and propose amendments to the Draft.
3. The first reading took place before the Draft Constitution was published.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statements 1 and 2 are correct.

**Why not 3:** The first reading (**4 November 1948**) came **after** publication (**February 1948**) — publication necessarily precedes any reading of a document that did not yet exist to be read. Statement 3 reverses the sequence.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** A statement claiming an earlier event happened "before" a later one it actually followed is one of the simplest ways to construct a false statement — and one of the easiest to catch, provided you have the underlying sequence memorised rather than the dates in isolation.

</details>

### 9.
Which one of the following best explains why the Assembly built in an eight-month public discussion period between publishing the Draft and beginning its first reading?

- (a) The Assembly had no other work to occupy it during this period
- (b) It allowed the wider public, press and provincial legislatures to scrutinise the Draft and propose changes before the Assembly's own formal debate began
- (c) It was a requirement imposed by the departing British administration
- (d) It was needed to translate the Draft into regional languages before any debate could occur

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The gap gave the country outside the Assembly room — newspapers, provincial bodies, individual citizens — a genuine window to react and propose changes **before** the Assembly locked itself into formal, clause-by-clause debate.

**Why not (c):** By February 1948, India was already independent (August 1947) and the Assembly was fully sovereign — nothing in its procedure was dictated by Britain any longer.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This eight-month window is direct evidence against the "closed-door" version of the "not representative" criticism — whatever the limits of who *elected* the Assembly, the process of *writing* the Constitution deliberately opened itself to outside scrutiny before finalising anything.

</details>

### 10. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Draft Constitution's first reading, in November 1948, involved a clause-by-clause examination of every Article.

**Statement-II:** Clause-by-clause examination was the defining feature of the second reading, which followed the first.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** Statement-I is false — the first reading was a **general** discussion, not clause-by-clause. Statement-II is true and, in fact, is what makes Statement-I's claim wrong: clause-by-clause work belonged to the **second** reading, not the first.

> ⚠️ **A well-built trap:** Statement-II is entirely accurate on its own, and its truth might tempt you to assume it "confirms" Statement-I by association. Read Statement-I strictly on its own terms first — it attaches the second reading's defining feature to the wrong reading.

</details>

### 11.
Arrange the following in correct chronological order:

1. Public discussion period on the Draft Constitution begins
2. Drafting Committee constituted
3. First reading — general discussion
4. Second reading — clause-by-clause discussion begins

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 2 – 1 – 3 – 4
- (b) 1 – 2 – 3 – 4
- (c) 2 – 3 – 1 – 4
- (d) 2 – 1 – 4 – 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Committee constituted **Aug 1947** → Draft published, public discussion begins **Feb 1948** → first reading **Nov 1948** → second reading follows immediately after. Each event is a precondition for the next: you need a committee before a draft, a published draft before public discussion, and a completed public-discussion period before the Assembly's own formal first reading.

### 12.
Which one of the following best distinguishes the roles of the Drafting Committee and the full Constituent Assembly during the enactment process?

- (a) The Drafting Committee wrote the text; the full Assembly debated, amended and finally passed it
- (b) The full Assembly wrote the text; the Drafting Committee only read it aloud
- (c) Both bodies performed identical functions simultaneously
- (d) The Drafting Committee's role ended once the Draft was published in February 1948

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The Drafting Committee (seven members, Set 3) produced the working **text**; the full Assembly (up to 299 members) then subjected that text to three readings of scrutiny, amendment and final passage — a small drafting body and a large deliberative body, each doing what it was suited for.

**Why not (d):** The Drafting Committee's work continued well past February 1948 — it remained active advising on amendments and refining language through both the first and second readings, right up to the final text passed in November 1949.

> 🔑 **Concept:** A small body drafts precisely because precision and consistency are easier to achieve with few hands; a large body ratifies and amends precisely because legitimacy requires broad participation. The two-body structure is a deliberate division of labour, not a redundancy.

</details>

### 13.
Which one of the following statements about the second reading is correct?

- (a) It was completed within a single week
- (b) It extended over roughly a year, spanning multiple sessions of the Assembly
- (c) It took place entirely before the first reading
- (d) It was conducted only by the Drafting Committee, without full Assembly participation

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The clause-by-clause second reading was by far the longest stage, running across **roughly a year** and multiple Assembly sessions — consistent with it being where most of the Assembly's total sitting days (Set 6) were actually spent.

**Why not (a):** A single week could not accommodate genuine amendment-by-amendment debate on 395 Articles — the scale of the task alone rules this out, independent of any specific date you might recall.

</details>

### 16.
Which one of the following best explains why the first reading (November 1948) and the third reading (November 1949) are exactly one year apart?

- (a) The Assembly deliberately scheduled its readings on annual anniversaries
- (b) The intervening year was consumed almost entirely by the second reading's clause-by-clause work across 395 Articles
- (c) The Assembly suspended all work between the two readings
- (d) It is a coincidence with no procedural significance

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The gap is not decorative — it is the real time the second reading actually took, examining, amending and settling every Article one by one before the Assembly could responsibly proceed to a final check and passage.

**Why not (c):** Far from suspending work, this year contains the **bulk** of the Assembly's substantive activity — the majority of its sitting days (Set 6) fall inside exactly this window.

> 🔑 **Concept:** A striking date pattern (two events exactly a year apart) is often a clue to the process in between, not just a memorable coincidence. Whenever you notice such a pattern, ask what filled the gap — the answer is usually the most important part of the story.

</details>

### 17. 📰
Which one of the following statements about public participation in constitution-making, as practised in 1948, remains a live comparison point in constitutional debates today?

- (a) The eight-month public discussion window on the Draft Constitution is often cited as an early example of participatory constitution-making, compared against the speed of later major legislative reforms
- (b) The Assembly held no public consultation of any kind
- (c) Public comments were legally binding on the Assembly and had to be incorporated verbatim
- (d) Only Assembly members were permitted to view the Draft Constitution before its adoption

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The eight-month window between publication and the first reading is a genuine historical instance of pre-legislative public consultation — a comparison point still invoked when contemporary reforms (major codes, sweeping amendments, new laws) are criticised for being passed with little time for public comment.

**Why not (c):** Public comments informed the Assembly's debate but were never legally binding — the Assembly remained free to accept, modify or reject any suggestion; sovereignty over the final text stayed with the elected (if indirectly elected) body, not with unstructured public opinion.

> 📰 **Why this belongs in "current affairs."** Whenever a new law or major amendment is criticised for being rushed through with minimal public consultation — a recurring theme in Indian legislative debate — commentators frequently reach back to this eight-month 1948 window as the benchmark the Assembly itself set. Knowing the benchmark lets you recognise the comparison when you meet it in a newspaper editorial or a current-affairs brief.

</details>

### 20.
Which one of the following best explains why the first reading and the third reading are described in the chapter notes as distinct events, despite both nominally being "readings" of the same Draft?

- (a) They occurred in different Assembly buildings
- (b) The first reading opened debate on an unamended Draft; the third reading closed debate on a Draft already reshaped by a year of clause-by-clause amendment
- (c) The first reading was conducted by the public; the third by the Assembly alone
- (d) There is no meaningful distinction between them

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** By the time of the third reading, the text being examined was no longer the **original** Draft Ambedkar introduced in November 1948 — it had been reworked, clause by clause, through the second reading. The third reading checks and passes a materially different, refined document.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This is why "the Draft Constitution" and "the Constitution" are not simply two names for one static document — the text itself evolved substantially across the three readings, and the final Article count, wording and structure reflect that year of amendment, not merely Ambedkar's original November 1948 text.

</details>

### 22.
Which one of the following statements about the relationship between public discussion and the Assembly's formal readings is correct?

- (a) Public discussion replaced the need for a first reading entirely
- (b) Public discussion preceded the first reading and informed, but did not replace, the Assembly's own formal scrutiny across three readings
- (c) Public discussion occurred only after the Constitution was already adopted
- (d) Public discussion was limited strictly to members of the Drafting Committee

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

> 🔑 **Concept:** This distinction — public input informing a sovereign body's decision, without replacing that body's own responsibility to decide — is a recurring theme in how legislatures everywhere handle public consultation, not a quirk unique to 1948 India.

### 24.
Which one of the following statements is the most accurate description of the "first reading" in constitutional, rather than purely chronological, terms?

- (a) It was the moment the Assembly formally accepted the Draft Constitution as legally binding
- (b) It was the moment the Assembly opened the Draft Constitution to its own formal deliberative process, without yet committing to any of its specific provisions
- (c) It was the moment the Constitution came into force
- (d) It was a purely symbolic event with no procedural consequence

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The first reading opened formal deliberation — it did **not** bind the Assembly to the Draft's specific content, which remained fully open to amendment through the second reading that followed.

**Why not (a) and (c):** Legal acceptance is the **third** reading's job (adoption, 26 Nov 1949); coming into force is a separate, later event again (26 Jan 1950, Set 5) — three different levels of commitment, three different dates, none of them the first reading.

> 🔑 **Concept:** "Opened for debate," "adopted," and "came into force" are three distinct legal moments in this chapter, and confusing any pair of them is a guaranteed way to lose marks. This question exists to force you to state the first reading's actual legal weight precisely, rather than assuming "reading" implies some kind of formal commitment.

</details>

### 25. 🔥
Which one of the following best captures the purpose of structuring the Constitution's enactment into three distinct readings, rather than a single debate-and-vote?

- (a) It was purely a ceremonial tradition with no substantive purpose
- (b) It separated the questions of "should we proceed with this Draft," "is each specific clause correct," and "do we finally accept the whole, now-amended text" into three manageable, sequential stages
- (c) It was required because the Assembly could not agree on a single meeting date
- (d) It reflected disagreement between Nehru and Patel over procedure

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Three genuinely different questions — proceed in principle, correct in detail, accept the final whole — are far more manageable asked one at a time than bundled into a single up-or-down vote on 395 Articles at once.

> 🔑 **The big idea of this set.** The three-reading structure is not bureaucratic ritual — it is a sound way to manage an enormous, high-stakes drafting task without either rushing it or letting it collapse into permanent disagreement. It is also the direct institutional ancestor of how an ordinary Bill becomes an Act in the Parliament you will study later — the Assembly, while writing the rules for future legislation, was simultaneously demonstrating good legislative practice on itself.

</details>
