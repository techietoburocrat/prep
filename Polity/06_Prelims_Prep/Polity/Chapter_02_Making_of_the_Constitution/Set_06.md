# Chapter 2 · Practice Set 06 — The Numbers: Time, Sessions, Cost, Composition

**Covers:** 2 years 11 months 18 days · 11 sessions, 165 sitting days · 114 days on the Draft · ₹64 lakh · the 15 women members

> **The idea behind this set.** Bare numbers are the easiest facts to lose under exam pressure, because nothing about them is intuitive — you either know "165" or you don't. This set drills them the way you'd drill a phone number: by repetition, by cross-checking against each other, and by attaching each number to what it is actually counting.

### 1. 🔥
The Constituent Assembly took how long, from its first sitting to the Constitution's adoption, to complete its work?

- (a) 2 years, 6 months and 10 days
- (b) 2 years, 11 months and 18 days
- (c) 3 years exactly
- (d) 1 year, 11 months and 18 days

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **2 years, 11 months and 18 days** — measured from the first sitting (**9 December 1946**) to adoption (**26 November 1949**).

> 🔑 **Say it like a phone number: "2-11-18."** It is the single most quoted statistic about the Assembly's timeline, and it is measured from **first sitting to adoption** — not from the Drafting Committee's constitution (that figure, roughly 27 months, was covered separately in Set 4, Q19). Two similar "how long did it take" numbers, two different start points — keep them apart.

</details>

### 2. 🔥
The Constituent Assembly held how many sessions in the course of its work?

- (a) 8
- (b) 11
- (c) 15
- (d) 22

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **11 sessions** — the Assembly did not sit continuously for three years; it met in a series of distinct sessions, spread across that whole period.

**Why not (d):** **22** is the number of **Parts** in the original Constitution (Set 5) — a different unit of counting entirely, borrowed here to build a plausible-looking wrong answer.

</details>

### 3. 🔥
Across its 11 sessions, the Constituent Assembly actually sat for a total of

- (a) 114 days
- (b) 165 days
- (c) 299 days
- (d) 389 days

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **165 sitting days** — the total number of days the Assembly was actually in session, across all 11 sessions combined.

**Why not (c) and (d):** Both **299** and **389** are genuine numbers from this chapter — member-strength figures (Set 1) — reused here as unit-confusion traps. A "number of days" question should never be answered with a number you know to represent people.

> 🔑 **Concept — build the numeric hierarchy once:** **11 sessions** contain **165 sitting days**, of which **114 days** (Q4) were spent specifically debating the Draft Constitution. Each number nests inside the one before it — sessions, then days, then a subset of those days devoted to one particular task.

</details>

### 4. 🔥 ⚠️
Of the Assembly's total sitting days, how many were spent specifically debating the Draft Constitution?

- (a) 165 days
- (b) 114 days
- (c) 11 days
- (d) 284 days

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **114 days** — a large subset, but still a subset, of the 165 total sitting days. The remaining days were spent on the Assembly's other business (electing officers, the Objectives Resolution, functioning as the provisional legislature, and more — Set 7).

**Why not (a):** 165 is the **total** across all business, not the debate-on-the-Draft figure specifically — the two numbers are easily and commonly swapped.

> ⚠️ **The classic trap in this set: 165 vs 114, total sitting days vs days on the Draft specifically.** Anchor them with a sentence: *"Of 165 total sitting days, 114 went to the Draft — the rest went to everything else the Assembly also had to do."*

</details>

### 5. 🔥
The total expenditure incurred by the Constituent Assembly in preparing the Constitution is estimated at approximately

- (a) ₹6.4 lakh
- (b) ₹64 lakh
- (c) ₹6.4 crore
- (d) ₹64 crore

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Approximately **₹64 lakh** — a figure regularly cited as strikingly modest for a nearly three-year national undertaking producing the world's longest written constitution.

> 🔑 **Concept — a good comparative fact for a Mains introduction or conclusion.** Against the scale of the task — 299 members, 165 sitting days, 395 Articles — ₹64 lakh reads as remarkably economical, and is often invoked precisely to counter the "time-consuming and expensive" line of criticism developed fully in Set 10.

</details>

### 7. 🔥 📰
Which of the following were among the 15 women members of the Constituent Assembly?

1. Sarojini Naidu
2. Hansa Mehta
3. Sucheta Kripalani

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 and 2 only
- (b) 2 and 3 only
- (c) 1 and 3 only
- (d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** All three were genuine members. **Sarojini Naidu**, the poet-politician; **Hansa Mehta**, who later played a significant international role (see below); and **Sucheta Kripalani**, who went on to become India's first woman Chief Minister (of Uttar Pradesh, 1963).

> 📰 **Hansa Mehta's wider legacy is worth knowing beyond the Assembly itself.** As a member of the UN Commission on Human Rights that drafted the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)**, she is widely credited with successfully pushing to change the opening line from *"All men are created equal"* to **"All human beings are born free and equal"** — a change with lasting global impact on the language of rights. The same generation of Indians writing India's Constitution was, in several cases, simultaneously shaping international human-rights instruments.

</details>

### 8.
The other twelve women members of the Constituent Assembly, besides Naidu, Mehta and Kripalani, included

- (a) Ammu Swaminathan, Begum Aizaz Rasul and Durgabai Deshmukh
- (b) Indira Gandhi, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit and Annie Besant
- (c) Kamala Nehru, Kasturba Gandhi and Sarala Devi
- (d) Muthulakshmi Reddy, Rukmini Devi Arundale and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** **Ammu Swaminathan**, **Begum Aizaz Rasul** and **Durgabai Deshmukh** were all genuine Constituent Assembly members among the full list of fifteen.

**Why not (b):** Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit **was** an Assembly member — but Indira Gandhi and Annie Besant were **not** members of this Assembly at all, which is why this option, despite containing one correct name, is wrong as a whole.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** In a "which set of names is correct" question, a single genuinely correct name embedded among incorrect ones does not save the option — read every name in the set, not just the first or most familiar one.

</details>

### 10. 🔥
Arrange the following in order from **largest** to **smallest** number:

1. Total sitting days
2. Days spent debating the Draft Constitution
3. Sessions held
4. Signatories to the Constitution

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 4 – 1 – 2 – 3
- (b) 1 – 2 – 4 – 3
- (c) 1 – 4 – 2 – 3
- (d) 4 – 2 – 1 – 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Signatories **284** > total sitting days **165** > days on the Draft **114** > sessions **11**. Ordering four genuinely different kinds of number against each other forces you to hold all of them in mind simultaneously, which is exactly the kind of integrated recall UPSC's harder questions reward.

> 🔑 **Exam skill:** When a question mixes categories that are not directly comparable in the real world (people who signed, versus days, versus sessions), the only way through is to already have each individual number memorised — there is no shortcut logic to derive one from another here, unlike in some of the chronology questions elsewhere in this chapter.

</details>

### 11.
Which one of the following best explains why the Assembly needed 11 separate sessions rather than one continuous sitting?

- (a) British law required a mandatory recess between each session
- (b) A body of hundreds of members, most holding other public responsibilities, needed to break its nearly three-year task into manageable, spaced sessions rather than sitting continuously
- (c) The Assembly was legally barred from meeting more than once a year
- (d) Each session corresponded to a different Indian state hosting the Assembly in turn

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Many Assembly members were simultaneously serving in provincial governments, the interim national government, or other public roles — a continuous, uninterrupted multi-year sitting was neither practical nor necessary once the work was organised through committees (Set 3) that could continue preparatory work between sessions.

**Why not (a) and (c):** Nothing in the Cabinet Mission Plan or the Assembly's own procedure imposed either constraint — the sessional structure was a practical choice, not a legal requirement.

</details>

### 12.
Which one of the following statements about the women members of the Constituent Assembly is correct?

- (a) There were no women members at all
- (b) There were 15 women members, out of a post-Partition strength of 299
- (c) There were 15 women members, out of an original strength of 389
- (d) Women members formed the majority of the Drafting Committee

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **15 women**, set against the Assembly's **post-Partition** strength of **299** — the correct base figure to compare against, since these women served through the Assembly's main working life after Partition, not merely in its brief original pre-Partition form.

**Why not (d):** The seven-member Drafting Committee (Set 3) contained **no** women at all — a genuine gap in the Assembly's most consequential committee, worth remembering precisely because it complicates any simple "the Assembly was inclusive" narrative.

> 🔑 **Concept — hold two facts together, not just one.** Fifteen women shaped debates across the whole Assembly, and simultaneously, the single body that turned those debates into final legal text had none. Both facts are true, and a strong Mains answer on representativeness uses both rather than picking the one that is more flattering.

</details>

### 14. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Constituent Assembly's total expenditure, at roughly ₹64 lakh, is often cited as remarkably modest for the scale of its task.

**Statement-II:** The Assembly sat for a total of 165 days across 11 sessions spanning nearly three years, producing a Constitution of 395 Articles.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Both are correct, and II explains why I is a reasonable claim — a nearly three-year process producing 395 Articles across 165 sitting days is a genuinely large undertaking, which is exactly why ₹64 lakh strikes commentators as modest **relative to that scale**, rather than modest in some absolute, context-free sense.

> 🔑 **Concept:** "Modest" is always a relative judgement — Statement-II supplies the scale against which the cost figure in Statement-I is being judged modest. Whenever a statement calls a number "large," "small," "modest" or "excessive," check whether the explanation supplies the comparison that justifies the adjective.

</details>

### 16.
Consider the following statements:

1. The Assembly's 11 sessions were held continuously without any gap between them.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

- (a) 1 only
- (b) 2 only
- (c) Both 1 and 2
- (d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statement 2 is correct — the committee structure (Set 3) was precisely what allowed detailed preparatory work to continue **between** formal sessions, rather than every task waiting for the full Assembly to reconvene. Statement 1 is false: eleven separate sessions across nearly three years necessarily involved gaps, as established in Q11.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This links back directly to Set 3 — the committee structure is not merely an organisational detail, it is the reason the Assembly's discontinuous session pattern (Q11) did not simply stall its overall progress between sittings.

### 17.
Which one of the following best explains why "2 years, 11 months and 18 days" and "165 sitting days" are both true but very different-looking numbers for describing "how long the Assembly took"?

- (a) One of the two figures is simply incorrect
- (b) The first measures elapsed calendar time from first sitting to adoption; the second measures only the days the Assembly was actually in session during that period
- (c) They refer to two entirely different Assemblies
- (d) The Assembly worked exactly one day per week throughout its existence

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Calendar time (nearly 3 years) and actual working days (165) measure genuinely different things — much of the calendar span consisted of gaps between sessions (Q11), committee work outside formal sittings, and the public consultation window (Set 4).

> 🔑 **Concept:** This distinction — elapsed time versus actual sitting/working time — is a generally useful one for reading almost any institutional statistic, not just this chapter's. A legislature's "term" and its actual "sitting days" are always two different numbers, and confusing them is a common source of misleading claims well beyond this specific exam topic.

</details>

### 19. 📰
Which one of the following is the most defensible use of the "₹64 lakh" figure in a Mains answer?

- (a) As proof that the Assembly did not take its work seriously
- (b) As a comparative point showing the Constitution was produced economically relative to the scale and duration of the task, useful when countering the "time-consuming and expensive" criticism
- (c) As evidence that Assembly members were unpaid volunteers
- (d) As proof that the British government funded the entire process

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** This is the honest, defensible use of the figure — a comparative point about cost-efficiency **relative to scale**, not a claim about volunteerism, funding source, or seriousness, none of which the figure actually establishes on its own.

> 📰 **Why the framing matters.** A raw number like "₹64 lakh" means little without context — it becomes useful only when compared against something (the scale of the task, contemporary equivalents, or other constitution-making exercises). This is a transferable skill for handling any statistic that appears in a current-affairs brief or an editorial: ask what it is being compared against before deciding what it proves.

</details>

### 20.
Which one of the following statements correctly uses the figures in this set to respond to the criticism that the Assembly was "too slow"?

- (b) Of nearly three years of calendar time, only 165 days were actual sitting days — and 114 of those went into detailed, clause-by-clause scrutiny of 395 Articles for an exceptionally diverse country
- (c) The Assembly was slow because it lacked any committee structure to parallelise its work
- (d) The delay was caused entirely by British obstruction after independence

> 🔑 **Concept — this is Set 10 material previewed early.** The full "time-consuming" criticism and its standard counter-argument (the US Convention took four months for a far shorter document) belongs later in this chapter, but the raw numbers to support that counter-argument are exactly the ones this set has just drilled.

Consider the following statements about the Constituent Assembly's composition and duration:

### 25. 🔥
Which one of the following best captures the purpose of memorising this set's numbers as a connected system rather than as isolated facts?

- (a) Isolated numbers are just as easy to recall correctly under exam pressure as a connected system
- (b) A connected system — sessions containing days, days split between the Draft and other business, member counts split by gender and by committee — lets you check any single recalled number against the others for consistency, catching your own errors before you answer
- (c) UPSC never combines more than one number in a single question
- (d) The numbers in this chapter have no logical relationship to each other at all

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Every number in this set nests inside or relates to another — 11 sessions contain 165 days, of which 114 went to the Draft; 299 post-Partition members include 15 women, none of whom sat on the 7-member Drafting Committee; 389 shrinks to 299 shrinks to 284. Holding the **system** in mind lets you sanity-check any individual figure a question throws at you, rather than needing to have memorised it in perfect isolation.

> 🔑 **The big idea of this set.** Numbers that seem arbitrary in isolation become memorable once you see how they nest inside one another. That is also your best defence against the counting and pairing formats UPSC uses most often on exactly this kind of material — and it is why this set drilled every number at least twice, in two different relationships, rather than once each in a flat list.

</details>
