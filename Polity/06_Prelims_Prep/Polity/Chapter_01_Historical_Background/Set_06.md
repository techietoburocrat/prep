# Chapter 1 · Practice Set 06 — Government of India Act, 1935: The Blueprint

[← Set 05](Set_05.md) · [Chapter Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Set 07 →](Set_07.md)

**25 questions · 30 minutes · +2 / −0.66 · Level 🟡 Prelims Standard**
**Covers:** GoI Act 1935 — federation, provincial autonomy, three lists, Federal Court, and what our Constitution took from it

> **The idea behind this set.** Roughly **two-thirds of our Constitution comes from this Act.** When you later study the Governor, the Seventh Schedule, emergency provisions and the Public Service Commissions and think *"why is this so un-democratic?"* — the answer is that it was designed by a colonial government to control India, and we kept the machinery while changing its purpose.
>
> The other half of this set is a single recurring theme: **what the Act provided vs what actually operated.** Two of its biggest provisions **never came into being**. UPSC asks about that gap more than about anything else in the Act.

🔥 = high probability · 📰 = current-affairs linked · ⚠️ = classic trap

---

### 1. 🔥 ⚠️
Which of the following provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935 **never came into operation**?

1. The All-India Federation
2. Dyarchy at the Centre
3. Provincial autonomy

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 only
- (b) 1 and 2 only
- (c) 2 and 3 only
- (d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The **All-India Federation** never came into being because the **princely states refused to join**. **Dyarchy at the Centre**, which depended on that federation, therefore never operated either.

**Why not 3:** **Provincial autonomy DID operate** — from **1937**, when Congress formed ministries in most provinces, until **1939**, when those ministries **resigned** over India being taken into the Second World War without consultation.

> 🔑 **The most-asked fact in this Act. Learn it as one sentence:**
> ***"The provinces worked; the federation didn't."***
> **Operated:** provincial autonomy (1937–39).
> **Never operated:** All-India Federation, dyarchy at the Centre.

</details>

### 2. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 divided legislative powers between the Centre and the units into three lists. They were

- (a) Union, State and Concurrent
- (b) Central, Reserved and Transferred
- (c) Imperial, Provincial and Local
- (d) Federal, Provincial and Concurrent

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** **Federal List (59 items)**, **Provincial List (54 items)** and **Concurrent List (36 items)**.

**Why not (a):** "Union, State, Concurrent" is our **Seventh Schedule** — the descendant, not the ancestor. Reading the option too fast is exactly how this is lost.

> 🔑 **Concept — trace the descent:**
> **1919** two categories (central / provincial) → **1935** three lists (Federal / Provincial / Concurrent) → **1950** three lists (**Union / State / Concurrent**), Seventh Schedule.
> The **structure** was inherited whole; only the **names** changed. That is the clearest single illustration of "two-thirds borrowed."

</details>

### 3. 🔥 ⚠️
Under the Government of India Act, 1935, residuary powers were vested in

- (a) the Federal Legislature
- (b) the Provincial Legislatures
- (c) the Governor-General
- (d) the Federal Court

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** The **Governor-General** held the residuary power, and could decide **in his discretion** under which list any unlisted subject fell.

> ⚠️ **This was asked directly in Prelims 2018.** It is one of the most reliably examined single facts in the whole chapter.

> 🔑 **Concept — and here is what makes it interesting.** Compare where residuary power sits in different federations:
> | Country | Residuary power with |
> |---|---|
> | **USA, Australia, Switzerland** | The **States** |
> | **Canada** | The **Centre** |
> | **India (1935 Act)** | The **Governor-General** |
> | **India (Constitution, Art. 248 + Entry 97)** | The **Union Parliament** |
>
> India followed the **Canadian** model of a strong Centre — which is exactly what the 1935 Act had already accustomed the country to. Colonial centralisation made constitutional centralisation feel natural. Remember this table for **Chapter 13** and **Chapter 14**; it is asked from there too.

</details>

### 4. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 introduced provincial autonomy. This meant that

- (a) provinces became sovereign units free to secede
- (b) provinces were given residuary powers
- (c) dyarchy in the provinces was abolished and the Governor was to act on the advice of ministers responsible to the provincial legislature
- (d) provincial legislatures could amend the Act itself

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Provincial autonomy meant two things together — **dyarchy abolished** in the provinces, and **responsible government introduced** there, with the Governor acting on ministerial advice.

**Why not (b):** Residuary powers went to the **Governor-General**, as in Q3.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This is the **first time responsible government actually operated** anywhere in India. All of it, in the provinces; none of it, at the Centre. From here the constitutional argument narrows to a single question: **when does the Centre become responsible too?** The answer turns out to be **1947**.

</details>

### 5. 🔥
The Federal Court established under the Government of India Act, 1935 was set up in

- (a) 1935
- (b) 1937
- (c) 1939
- (d) 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The **Federal Court was set up in 1937**, with **Sir Maurice Gwyer** as its first Chief Justice. It functioned until the **Supreme Court** replaced it on **28 January 1950**.

> 🔑 **Concept — the crucial limitation.** The Federal Court was **not the final court of appeal**. Appeals from it lay to the **Privy Council in London**. That last colonial thread was cut in stages: the **Federal Court (Enlargement of Jurisdiction) Act, 1947**, then the **Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949**.
>
> **1937 → 1950**, and only then was India's highest court actually in India. Expect this in Chapter 26.

</details>

### 6. 🔥
Which of the following did the Government of India Act, 1935 abolish?

1. The Council of India
2. The office of Secretary of State for India
3. Dyarchy in the provinces

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 and 2 only
- (b) 1 and 3 only
- (c) 2 and 3 only
- (d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The 1935 Act abolished the **Council of India** (which had assisted the Secretary of State since **1858**) and **dyarchy in the provinces**.

**Why not 2:** The **Secretary of State for India survived** — with a team of advisers replacing the Council — and was abolished only by the **Indian Independence Act, 1947**.

> ⚠️ **The assistant died before the master.**
> **Council of India:** 1858 → **1935**.
> **Secretary of State:** 1858 → **1947**.
> Twelve years apart. This exact pairing is a favourite because it feels as if both should end together.

</details>

### 7. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 introduced bicameralism in how many of the eleven provinces?

- (a) Four
- (b) Five
- (c) Six
- (d) Eight

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Six of eleven** — **Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces** — each with a legislative council (upper house) and legislative assembly (lower house), though under many restrictions.

> 🔑 **Concept:** This is the ancestor of **Article 169** and today's **State Legislative Councils**. Note that bicameralism at the state level was **optional then and remains optional now** — currently only a handful of states have a Legislative Council, and Parliament can create or abolish one under **Article 169** on a resolution of the State Assembly. Andhra Pradesh has abolished and revived its Council; Jammu & Kashmir's ended with reorganisation in **2019**. Selective bicameralism is an inheritance, not an accident.

</details>

### 8. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 extended separate electorates to

- (a) Muslims and Sikhs
- (b) Anglo-Indians and Europeans
- (c) the depressed classes, women and labour
- (d) universities and chambers of commerce

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** The 1935 Act extended communal and special representation to the **depressed classes (Scheduled Castes), women and labour**.

**Why not the others:** Muslims — **1909**; Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans — **1919**; universities and chambers of commerce — **1909**.

> 🔑 **The complete chain, for the last time:**
> **1909** Muslims + special interests → **1919** Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans → **1932** Depressed Classes (Communal Award, modified by the Poona Pact) → **1935** depressed classes, women, labour.
> Four Acts, one direction. And then **Article 325** ends all of it in 1950.

</details>

### 9. 🔥
Under the Government of India Act, 1935, the franchise was extended to approximately

- (a) 3% of the population
- (b) 10% of the population
- (c) 25% of the population
- (d) 50% of the population

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** About **10%** of the total population — up from roughly **3%** under the 1919 Act.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Even the most liberal colonial franchise reached **one Indian in ten**. Then, in 1950, the Constitution gave the vote to **every adult** — in a country with mass poverty and roughly **16% literacy**. No other new nation had attempted it on that scale.
>
> This is a Mains point and, increasingly, a Prelims one: universal adult franchise was not the culmination of colonial reform but a **rejection** of its central assumption — that the vote must be earned through property or education.

</details>

### 10. 🔥
Consider the following statements about the All-India Federation proposed by the Government of India Act, 1935:

1. It was to consist of the British Indian provinces and the princely states.
2. Accession of the princely states to the federation was voluntary.
3. It came into existence in 1937 along with provincial autonomy.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. The federation was to comprise **provinces and princely states**, and accession by the states was **voluntary** — which is precisely why it failed.

**Why not 3:** **Provincial autonomy came into effect in 1937; the federation never did.** The princes, fearing loss of internal sovereignty, declined to accede in sufficient numbers.

> 🔑 **Concept — a design flaw with enormous consequences.** Making accession voluntary handed roughly 560 rulers a veto over India's constitutional future, and they used it.
>
> Note the historical irony: **voluntary accession** failed in 1935 and then **succeeded in 1947**, when Sardar Patel and V.P. Menon used the very same instrument — the **Instrument of Accession** — to integrate almost every state within about a year. Same mechanism, different political moment. That comparison is excellent Mains material and increasingly plausible as an analytical Prelims question.

</details>

### 11. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for the establishment of which of the following?

1. A Federal Public Service Commission
2. Provincial Public Service Commissions
3. A Joint Public Service Commission for two or more provinces

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 only
- (b) 1 and 2 only
- (c) 2 and 3 only
- (d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why all three:** The Act provided for a **Federal PSC**, **Provincial PSCs**, and a **Joint PSC** for two or more provinces.

> 🔑 **Concept — the structure survived intact.** Today: **UPSC** (Article 315(1)), **State PSCs** (Article 315(1)), and a **Joint State Public Service Commission** which Parliament may create on the request of two or more states (**Article 315(2)**). The three-tier design in front of you is 1935's, transplanted almost unchanged.
>
> ⚠️ Note the exam-relevant detail: **JPSC is created by an Act of Parliament** on a resolution of the state legislatures — not by the states acting alone.

</details>

### 12. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following statements about the Reserve Bank of India is correct in the context of the Government of India Act, 1935?

- (a) The 1935 Act contained provisions relating to the Reserve Bank, which had been constituted under the RBI Act, 1934 and began operations on 1 April 1935
- (b) The RBI was established by the Government of India Act, 1935 and began operations in 1937
- (c) The RBI was created by the Indian Independence Act, 1947
- (d) The RBI was established by the Charter Act of 1853

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** This is worth getting exactly right, because standard notes compress it. The **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** constituted the RBI, which **commenced operations on 1 April 1935**. The **Government of India Act, 1935** then dealt with the Reserve Bank in its financial provisions, as the central bank of the proposed federation — which is why Laxmikanth lists "provided for the establishment of a Reserve Bank of India" among its features.

**How to answer it in the exam:** if an option says the RBI's **constituting statute** was the **RBI Act, 1934**, that is correct. If the question simply asks which **Government of India Act** referred to establishing a central bank, the answer is **1935**. Read whether the question asks about the **Act that created it** or the **Act that mentions it**.

> 📰 **Current-affairs anchor:** the RBI was **nationalised on 1 January 1949** (it began as a shareholders' bank). It follows the **Hilton Young Commission (1926)** recommendation. First Governor: **Sir Osborne Smith**; first Indian Governor: **C.D. Deshmukh**.

</details>

### 13. 🔥
The Government of India Act, 1935 is often described as the blueprint of the Indian Constitution. Which of the following features did the Constitution borrow from it?

1. Federal scheme
2. Office of Governor
3. Emergency provisions
4. Public Service Commissions

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 and 2 only
- (b) 1, 2 and 3 only
- (c) 2, 3 and 4 only
- (d) 1, 2, 3 and 4

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why all four:** From the 1935 Act our Constitution took the **federal scheme**, the **office of Governor**, the **judiciary**, the **Public Service Commissions**, the **emergency provisions** and a mass of **administrative detail**.

> 🔑 **Concept — the borrowings table is a guaranteed question somewhere in your preparation.** Learn it as a whole:
> | Source | Borrowed |
> |---|---|
> | **GoI Act 1935** | Federal scheme · Governor · Judiciary · PSCs · Emergency provisions · Administrative details |
> | **Britain** | Parliamentary government · Rule of law · Single citizenship · Cabinet system · Writs |
> | **USA** | Fundamental Rights · Judicial review · Independent judiciary · Impeachment · VP's office |
> | **Ireland** | DPSP · Nomination to Rajya Sabha · Presidential election method |
> | **Canada** | Strong Centre · Residuary powers with Centre · Union–State terminology |
> | **Australia** | Concurrent List · Freedom of trade and commerce · Joint sitting |
> | **USSR** | Fundamental Duties · Justice (social, economic, political) in the Preamble |
> | **France** | Liberty, Equality, Fraternity · Republic |
> | **South Africa** | Amendment procedure · Rajya Sabha election |
> | **Japan** | Procedure established by law |
>
> This table is asked in **Chapter 3** but it starts **here**, and the 1935 row is the largest by far.

</details>

### 14. 🔥
Provincial autonomy under the Government of India Act, 1935 operated from

- (a) 1935 to 1937
- (b) 1937 to 1939
- (c) 1937 to 1947
- (d) 1939 to 1945

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Elections were held in **1936–37**; Congress ministries took office in **1937** and **resigned in 1939** in protest at India being declared a belligerent in the Second World War without any consultation with Indian opinion.

> 🔑 **Concept — what followed the resignations.** The Muslim League observed a **"Day of Deliverance"** in December 1939; and with Congress out of office, the constitutional initiative passed to negotiation — the **August Offer (1940)**, **Cripps Mission (1942)**, **Quit India (1942)**, **Wavell Plan (1945)**, **Cabinet Mission (1946)**.
>
> Two years of provincial self-government, then eight years of negotiation and confrontation. That is the last stretch of the chapter, and the subject of Set 07.

</details>

Arrange the following in correct chronological order:

1. Federal Court established
2. Government of India Act, 1935 enacted

4. Simon Commission report submitted

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 2 – 4 – 1 – 3
- (b) 4 – 2 – 1 – 3

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Simon Commission report **1930** → GoI Act **1935** → Federal Court **1937** → Congress ministries resign **1939**.

### 16. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Government of India Act, 1935 is described as providing a federal constitution, yet India did not become a federation under it.

**Statement-II:** The Government of India Act, 1935 divided legislative powers into Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **Both statements are true, but II does not explain I.**

The **actual** reason the federation never came into being is that it was to include the **princely states, whose accession was voluntary** — and a sufficient number of them **did not accede**.

Statement-II is perfectly true: the Act did create the **Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists**. But a division of legislative powers is part of the **federal design**; it is not the reason the design failed. If anything, it is evidence that the Act *was* federal in form.

> ⚠️ **Watch for a Statement-II that supports the first half of Statement-I while ignoring the second half.** Statement-I here has two parts — *"provided a federation"* and *"India did not become one"*. II explains only the first. That is not enough for option (a).

> 🔑 **This is also a Mains question you already have in your notes** — *"Did the Government of India Act, 1935 lay down a federal constitution? Discuss."* (**GS-II, 2016**).
>
> **The balanced answer:** federal in **form** — three lists, a Federal Court, a division of powers, autonomous provinces. Not federal in **substance** — residuary powers with the Governor-General, the Governor's overriding powers, no federation ever constituted, and units created and altered by the imperial power rather than being its constituents.
>
> The same tension reappears in our own Constitution, which Ambedkar called federal in normal times and unitary in emergencies. **Chapter 13** is a direct continuation of this argument.

</details>

### 17. ⚠️
Which one of the following statements about the Government of India Act, 1935 is **not** correct?

- (a) It was the longest Act enacted by the British Parliament at that time
- (b) It abolished dyarchy in the provinces
- (c) It made the Federal Court the final court of appeal for India
- (d) It provided for a Joint Public Service Commission

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c) is wrong (and therefore the answer):** Appeals from the Federal Court lay to the **Privy Council in London**. India had no final court of its own until the **Supreme Court in 1950**, after the Privy Council's jurisdiction was abolished in **1949**.

**Why the others are correct:** The Act ran to **321 sections and 10 schedules** — the longest of its time — and was later split into the Government of India Act, 1935 and the **Government of Burma Act, 1935**.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Test the word **"final"**. In colonial constitutional law, the final word is almost always in **London** — final court (Privy Council), final legislative authority (British Parliament), final executive authority (Secretary of State). Any option placing finality **in India** before 1947 deserves immediate suspicion.

</details>

### 18. 🔥
Consider the following pairs:

| Provision of the 1935 Act | Corresponding provision today |
|---|---|
| 1. Federal, Provincial and Concurrent Lists | Seventh Schedule |
| 2. Federal Public Service Commission | Union Public Service Commission |
| 3. Residuary powers with the Governor-General | Residuary powers with the Union Parliament |

How many of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why all three:** The three lists became the **Seventh Schedule**; the Federal PSC became the **UPSC**; and residuary powers passed from the **Governor-General** to the **Union Parliament** — **Article 248** read with **Entry 97 of the Union List**.

> ⚠️ **The most common misconception in Indian federalism.** Many candidates assume residuary powers go to the states "because that is what federations do." In India they do **not**. USA, Australia and Switzerland give them to the states; **Canada and India give them to the Centre**.
>
> Note also that we went **from Governor-General (1935) to Parliament (1950)** — the holder changed, the **centralisation did not**.

</details>

### 19.
The Government of India Act, 1935 created two new provinces. They were

- (a) Sindh and Orissa
- (b) Punjab and Assam
- (c) Bihar and Orissa
- (d) Burma and Aden

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** **Sindh** was separated from Bombay and **Orissa** from Bihar, both taking effect in **1936**. The Act also provided for the separation of **Burma** from India, effected in **1937** through a companion statute, the **Government of Burma Act, 1935**.

> 🔑 **Concept:** Note who is doing the creating. Provinces were made, merged and split by **imperial statute**, without reference to the people affected.
>
> Our Constitution kept that power — **Article 3** lets Parliament create, alter, rename or diminish any State by ordinary majority, with the State legislature entitled only to be **consulted**, not to consent. That is why India is called an **"indestructible Union of destructible States."** The lineage of that phrase runs straight through this provision — and you will meet it again in **Chapter 5**.

</details>

### 20. 🔥
Under the Government of India Act, 1935, dyarchy at the Centre would have divided federal subjects into

- (a) transferred and reserved subjects
- (b) federal and provincial subjects
- (c) concurrent and residuary subjects
- (d) imperial and dominion subjects

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The same **transferred/reserved** device used in the provinces after 1919 was to be applied to **federal** subjects — with defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs and tribal areas kept reserved under the Governor-General. It **never came into operation**.

> 🔑 **Concept — the mirror image, and the reason this Act confuses people:**
> **1919:** dyarchy **in the provinces** — it operated, and it failed.
> **1935:** dyarchy **abolished in the provinces**, **provided at the Centre** — and it never began.
> ***"Dyarchy moved upstairs and then died."*** If you remember only one sentence from Sets 05 and 06, make it that one.

</details>

### 21. 📰
Which one of the following correctly describes the position of the Governor under the Government of India Act, 1935?

- (a) He was bound by ministerial advice in all matters without exception
- (b) He acted on ministerial advice but retained special responsibilities and discretionary powers
- (c) He had no executive functions and was purely ceremonial
- (d) He was elected by the provincial legislature

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Governor was to act on ministerial advice **but retained "special responsibilities"** — for peace and tranquillity, minorities, the rights of civil servants and more — plus discretionary powers, in which he acted independently of his ministers.

> 📰 **Why this is one of the most live topics in Indian polity today.** Our **Article 163** reproduces the same architecture: the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers **"except in so far as he is required to exercise his functions in his discretion."** The colonial special-responsibility clause became the constitutional discretion clause.
>
> That inheritance is the root of every current Centre–State flashpoint you read about — **delay in granting assent to Bills**, **reservation of Bills for the President**, and the appointment of Chief Ministers in hung assemblies. The Supreme Court has been repeatedly drawn into it, including its rulings on gubernatorial delay in Punjab and Tamil Nadu.
>
> A Prelims question can come from **either end** — the 1935 provision or the Article 163 controversy. Know that they are the same provision, sixty years apart.

</details>

### 22. 🔥
Consider the following statements about the Government of India Act, 1935:

1. It provided for the establishment of an All-India Federation with residuary powers vested in the federal legislature.
2. It marked the end of the system of separate electorates.
3. It provided for a Federal Court with appellate jurisdiction over the High Courts.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

- (a) 3 only
- (b) 1 and 3 only
- (c) 2 and 3 only
- (d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Only statement 3 is correct — the Federal Court did have appellate jurisdiction over the High Courts (with a further appeal to the Privy Council).

**Why not 1:** The federation was provided for, but residuary powers went to the **Governor-General**, not the federal legislature.
**Why not 2:** Separate electorates were **extended** by this Act, not ended. They ended only with the **Constitution**.

> ⚠️ **Notice the construction of statement 1.** It is **half true** — the federation *was* provided for — and then attaches a **false tail**. This is the most effective false statement UPSC writes, because the true opening makes you nod before you reach the error.
>
> **Discipline for statement questions:** read to the **end of every statement** before judging it. Most wrong answers are lost in the last five words.

</details>

### 23. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why the Government of India Act, 1935 was rejected by both the Congress and the Muslim League, despite being the most far-reaching reform to date?

- (a) It abolished the provincial legislatures
- (b) It retained ultimate British control through the Governor-General's and Governors' overriding powers, and offered no dominion status
- (c) It withdrew the franchise from Indians
- (d) It merged the princely states into British India by force

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** However wide the provincial powers, the **Governor-General and Governors kept overriding and discretionary powers**, the **Secretary of State and British Parliament kept ultimate authority**, and **dominion status was not conceded**. Nehru famously described the Act as "a machine with strong brakes and no engine."

**Why not the others:** (a), (c) and (d) are all inversions of what the Act actually did.

> 🔑 **Concept — the recurring pattern of the whole chapter, now visible for the last time:**
> Every reform grants **more participation** and retains **final control**. 1909 permits election but keeps the official majority. 1919 gives ministers but keeps finance. 1935 gives provincial autonomy but keeps the Governor's override.
>
> The nationalist objection never changed either: **"the brakes are yours; give us the engine."** Only the **Indian Independence Act, 1947** hands over the engine.

</details>

### 24.
The Government of India Act, 1935 consisted of

- (a) 145 sections and 5 schedules
- (b) 321 sections and 10 schedules
- (c) 395 articles and 8 schedules
- (d) 250 sections and 12 schedules

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **321 sections and 10 schedules** — the **longest Act ever passed by the British Parliament** to that date.

**Why not (c):** **395 articles and 8 schedules** is the **Indian Constitution as originally adopted** — placed here deliberately, because the two numbers get stored in the same mental drawer.

> 🔑 **Keep the number pairs separate:**
> **GoI Act 1935:** 321 sections, 10 schedules.
> **Constitution (as adopted, 1949):** 395 articles, 22 parts, 8 schedules.
> **Constitution (currently):** ~470 articles, 25 parts, **12 schedules**.
> Note the Constitution's own numbers are asked constantly in **Chapter 3** — and note that articles are never renumbered, so counts are quoted as "approximately".

### 25. 🔥
Which one of the following best captures why the Government of India Act, 1935 is called the blueprint of the Indian Constitution but not its spirit?

- (a) It contained a preamble and a list of fundamental rights, but no directive principles
- (b) It applied only to the princely states and not to British India
- (c) It was drafted by Indians but enacted by the British Parliament
- (d) It supplied the structure of government while lacking popular sovereignty, fundamental rights and universal franchise

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** The Act gave us the **machinery** — three lists, federal court, Governor, PSCs, emergency provisions, administrative detail. What it entirely lacked was the **source of authority and purpose**: no preamble declaring the people as sovereign, **no fundamental rights**, **no universal adult franchise**, no popular mandate. It was written **for** Indians, not **by** them.

**Why not (a):** The 1935 Act had **neither** a preamble of the constitutional kind **nor** fundamental rights — the option's premise is false, which is what makes it tempting.

> 🔑 **The big idea of this set — and this is the sentence to carry into Chapter 3.**
>
> ***"We inherited the machinery and replaced the purpose."***
>
> The 1935 Act was designed for **control**; the Constitution deploys the same instruments for **self-government**. Same Governor, different master. Same three lists, different sovereign. Same emergency provisions, different accountability.
>
> This is why the modern Prelims paper can ask about 1935 without asking a single date. The question is never really *what did the Act say* — it is *what did India keep, what did India refuse, and why*. Sort every feature into those columns and you have finished this chapter.
