### 1. 🔥 ⚠️
Consider the following statements:

1. The Regulating Act of 1773 created the office of the Governor-General of India.
2. The Charter Act of 1833 ended the East India Company's commercial functions.
3. The Government of India Act of 1858 abolished the office of the Governor-General.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Only statement 2 is correct — the Charter Act of 1833 ended the Company's commercial functions.

**Why not 1:** The 1773 Act created the Governor-General of **Bengal**. The Governor-General of **India** dates from **1833**.

**Why not 3:** The 1858 Act abolished the **East India Company**, the **Board of Control** and the **Court of Directors** — ending double government. The **Governor-General survived**, and was additionally styled **Viceroy**. That office lasted until **1950**.

> 🔑 The Bengal/India swap again. It appears in every set of this chapter because it appears in every year of the exam. If you got this wrong at Set 08, write it on a card and carry it.

</details>

### 2.
Financial decentralisation in British India was initiated by

- (a) Lord Canning's resolution of 1861
- (b) Lord Ripon's resolution of 1882
- (c) Lord Mayo's resolution of 1870
- (d) Lord Curzon's resolution of 1904

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Lord Mayo's resolution of 1870** began financial decentralisation by giving provincial governments fixed grants for certain services, marking the start of provincial finance.

**Why not (b):** **Lord Ripon's resolution of 1882** dealt with **local self-government** — municipalities and local boards — earning him the title **"Father of Local Self-Government in India."**

> 🔑 **Fix the pair by function:** **Mayo 1870 = money to the provinces.** **Ripon 1882 = government to the localities.** Twelve years apart, constantly swapped.
>
> Ripon's 1882 resolution is also the starting point of **Chapter 37 (Panchayati Raj)** — so this one fact serves two chapters.

</details>

### 3. 🔥
Which one of the following Acts introduced dyarchy in the provinces and separated central and provincial subjects for the first time?

- (a) Indian Councils Act, 1909
- (b) Government of India Act, 1935
- (c) Government of India Act, 1919
- (d) Indian Independence Act, 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Both firsts belong to **1919** — dyarchy in the provinces, and the separation of central and provincial subjects.

> 🔑 The 1935 Act **abolished** provincial dyarchy and replaced two categories of subject with **three lists**. Both Acts touch both topics in opposite directions, which is precisely why they are confused.

</details>

### 4. ⚠️
The idea of a Constituent Assembly for India was first put forward by

- (a) Mahatma Gandhi in 1922
- (b) Jawaharlal Nehru in 1938
- (c) M.N. Roy in 1934
- (d) the Cripps Mission in 1942

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **M.N. Roy**, a pioneer of the communist movement in India, first proposed a **Constituent Assembly** in **1934**.

**The sequence that follows:**
- **1935** — the Indian National Congress officially demands a Constituent Assembly.
- **1938** — **Nehru** declares that the constitution of free India must be framed by a Constituent Assembly elected on adult franchise, without outside interference.
- **1940** — the demand is accepted in principle in the **August Offer**.
- **1946** — the Assembly is constituted under the **Cabinet Mission Plan**.

> 🔑 **This is Chapter 2 material appearing in Chapter 1 questions**, and it is asked often. Learn the four-step chain **Roy 1934 → Congress 1935 → Nehru 1938 → August Offer 1940 → Cabinet Mission 1946** once, and it serves both chapters.

</details>

### 5. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for a federation, but India did not become a federation under it.

**Statement-II:** The provinces were unwilling to surrender their autonomy to a federal centre.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Statement-I is correct. **Statement-II is false** — it was the **princely states**, not the provinces, that refused to accede. The provinces had no choice in the matter; their inclusion was automatic.

> ⚠️ **This is the hardest kind of Statement I/II question, and it is increasingly common.** Statement-II is not merely unrelated — it is a **plausible-sounding falsehood** placed where the true explanation should be. Because you *know* the federation failed, you are primed to accept any reason offered.
>
> **Discipline:** verify Statement-II as an independent fact **before** you consider whether it explains Statement-I. Never let a correct Statement-I vouch for the one below it.

</details>

### 6.
The Commonwealth of India Bill, 1925, an early Indian attempt at drafting a constitution, was associated with

- (a) Annie Besant
- (b) Motilal Nehru
- (c) Bal Gangadhar Tilak
- (d) Tej Bahadur Sapru

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The **Commonwealth of India Bill (1925)** was prepared under the aegis of **Annie Besant** and the Home Rule movement.

**The other Indian constitutional drafts, which get confused with it:**
- **Constitution of India Bill, 1895** ("Swaraj Bill") — often attributed to **Tilak's** circle.
- **Nehru Report, 1928** — chaired by **Motilal Nehru**, the first substantial Indian-drafted constitutional scheme, proposing **dominion status** and a list of **fundamental rights**.
- **Karachi Resolution, 1931** — on **Fundamental Rights and Economic Policy**, drafted largely by **Nehru** and endorsed by Congress.

> 🔑 **Concept — worth internalising for both Prelims and Mains.** Indians were drafting constitutions **decades** before 1946. The Constituent Assembly did not start from a blank page; it inherited a tradition. The **Nehru Report's fundamental rights** and the **Karachi Resolution's socio-economic rights** are visible ancestors of **Part III** and **Part IV** respectively.

</details>

### 8. 🔥
Which of the following features of the Indian Constitution are traceable to the Government of India Act, 1935?

1. Emergency provisions
2. Office of the Governor
3. Federal scheme
4. Fundamental Rights

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1, 2 and 3 only
- (b) 2, 3 and 4 only
- (c) 1 and 4 only
- (d) 1, 2, 3 and 4

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Emergency provisions, the Governor's office and the federal scheme all come from the 1935 Act.

**Why not 4:** **Fundamental Rights come from the United States Constitution** — and the 1935 Act contained **no bill of rights at all**, which was one of the strongest Indian objections to it.

> 🔑 **The cleanest way to remember this:** the 1935 Act gave us the **machinery of the State**; the USA gave us the **limits on the State**. Structure from London, rights from Washington. That contrast also answers "blueprint but not spirit" from Set 06.

</details>

### 9.
The Simon Commission was boycotted by Indians primarily because

- (a) it was appointed two years ahead of schedule
- (b) it proposed the abolition of the provincial legislatures
- (c) it recommended the partition of India
- (d) it consisted entirely of British members with no Indian representation

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** All **seven** members were British. A commission to determine India's constitutional future without a single Indian on it was an insult that united otherwise opposed political factions.

**Why not (a):** The early appointment was resented but was not the ground of the boycott.

> 🔑 **The consequence is more examinable than the boycott itself.** The Commission's own challenge — *"produce your own constitution if you can agree on one"* — provoked the **Nehru Report (1928)**, the first serious Indian constitutional draft. The insult produced the response, and the response produced Part III's ancestor.

</details>

### 10. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following statements about the Indian civil service is correct?

- (a) Open competition was introduced by the Charter Act of 1833 and implemented immediately
- (b) Open competition was provided by the Charter Act of 1853, and the Public Service Commission was established in 1926
- (c) The Public Service Commission was established by the Government of India Act, 1935
- (d) The Federal Public Service Commission was established in 1926

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** **1853** introduced open competition (on the **Macaulay Committee's** recommendation, 1854); the **1919 Act** provided for a Public Service Commission, which was **established in 1926**.

**Why not (c) and (d):** The **1935 Act** created the **Federal** PSC, Provincial PSCs and Joint PSCs — but the plain **Public Service Commission** of **1926** predates it.

> 🔑 **The full lineage, in five steps:**
> **1833** attempted, negated → **1853** open competition introduced → **1926** PSC established (Lee Commission) → **1935** Federal PSC → **1947** UPSC → **1950** constitutional body, **Articles 315–323**.

</details>

### 13. 🔥
Which one of the following statements about separate electorates is correct?

- (a) They were introduced in 1909 and abolished by the Government of India Act, 1935
- (b) They were introduced in 1909 and abolished by the Constitution of India
- (c) They were introduced in 1919 and abolished by the Poona Pact
- (d) They were introduced in 1892 and abolished in 1947

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Introduced in **1909**, progressively **extended** in 1919, 1932 and 1935, and abolished only by the **Constitution** — **Article 325** establishing a single general electoral roll.

**Why not (c):** The **Poona Pact (1932)** removed separate electorates only for the **Depressed Classes**, replacing them with **reserved seats in a joint electorate**. It left every other separate electorate intact.

> 🔑 **The trap in (c) is that it is half true.** A change limited to one group is not abolition. Read the **scope** of every claim, not just its direction.

</details>

### 14.
Under the Government of India Act, 1919, which of the following remained a reserved subject in the provinces?

- (a) Education
- (b) Public health
- (c) Finance
- (d) Agriculture

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Finance** was reserved — along with police, jails and land revenue. Education, health and agriculture were **transferred**.

> 🔑 **The rule again: they kept the money and the police; they gave away the schools and the hospitals.** And because finance was reserved, the ministers who ran the schools could not fund them — which is why dyarchy failed structurally rather than accidentally.

</details>

### 15. 🔥 📰
The provision of the Indian Councils Act, 1861 empowering the Viceroy to issue ordinances during an emergency finds its counterpart today in

- (a) Articles 123 and 213
- (b) Articles 352 and 356
- (c) Articles 200 and 201
- (d) Articles 245 and 246

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** **Article 123** (President's ordinance) and **Article 213** (Governor's ordinance).

**Why not the others:**
- (b) **National emergency (352)** and **President's rule (356)** trace to the **1935 Act's** emergency provisions, not to the 1861 ordinance power.
- (c) **Articles 200/201** — assent to Bills and reservation for the President, which trace to the **Governor's powers under the 1935 Act**.
- (d) **Articles 245/246** — distribution of legislative powers, tracing to the **three lists of 1935**.

> 🔑 **Notice that every option here has a genuine colonial ancestor.** That is the design. The question is not "which of these is colonial" — all are — but **which colonial provision matches which Article**. Building that mapping is the single highest-return thing you can do with this chapter.

> 📰 **Ordinance-making featured in Prelims 2025.** The **6 months (colonial) vs 6 weeks after reassembly (Article 123)** distinction is the likeliest trap.

</details>

### 16.
The Government of India Act, 1858 vested the government of India in

- (a) the Governor-General in Council
- (b) the Secretary of State for India, acting through the Council of India
- (c) the British Crown, exercised through the Secretary of State for India
- (d) the British Parliament directly

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Powers, territories and revenues passed to the **British Crown**, and Crown authority was exercised through the **Secretary of State for India**, a Cabinet Minister assisted by a 15-member Council.

**Why not (d):** Parliament legislated **for** India but did not govern it directly; government was executive, through the Crown's minister.

> 🔑 **Distinguish three things that all sound like "who ruled India":**
> **Sovereign** — the **Crown**.
> **Executive authority in London** — the **Secretary of State**.
> **Executive authority in India** — the **Viceroy/Governor-General**.
> Options in this chapter often offer all three; the answer depends on whether the question asks about **sovereignty**, **control** or **administration**.

</details>

### 17. 🔥
Consider the following statements about the Government of India Act, 1919:

1. It introduced universal adult franchise for the first time in India.
2. It provided that all six members of the Viceroy's executive council, other than the Commander-in-Chief, were to be Indian.
3. It made the Governor of a province a constitutional head bound by ministerial advice.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d) — all three are wrong:**
- **1** The 1919 Act introduced **direct elections**, not universal adult franchise. The franchise reached roughly **3%** of the population. Universal adult suffrage came only with **Article 326** in 1950.
- **2** It required **three of the six** members to be Indian, not all six.
- **3** The Governor was **never** a constitutional head under the 1919 Act — he administered the reserved subjects himself and retained overriding powers even over transferred ones. Governors became **constitutional heads only under the Indian Independence Act, 1947**.

> ⚠️ **"Constitutional head bound by ministerial advice" is a phrase with exactly one home: 1947.** Whenever you see it attached to 1919 or 1935, mark it false.
>
> The **1935 Act** came closest — the Governor acted on advice **but retained special responsibilities and discretion**. Close is not the same as bound.

</details>

### 18. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why the British introduced constitutional reforms at roughly twenty-year intervals between 1861 and 1935?

- (a) Each Act contained a clause requiring automatic review after twenty years
- (b) The Company's charter had to be renewed every twenty years
- (c) The British Parliament was constitutionally required to renew Indian legislation every twenty years
- (d) Reform followed periods of intensified nationalist pressure, and each Act conceded the minimum needed to contain it

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** Each Act follows a period of pressure and concedes the least that will restore calm — **1909** after the Swadeshi movement and revolutionary activity, **1919** after the Home Rule movement and the war, **1935** after the Civil Disobedience movement and the Round Table Conferences.

**Why not (b):** The twenty-year **charter renewal** cycle is real — but it belongs to the **Charter Acts up to 1853**, and ends there. The option is true of the wrong period, which makes it the strongest distractor.

> 🔑 **The big analytical idea of this chapter.** Constitutional reform in colonial India was **reactive, not benevolent**. Pressure → concession → disappointment → renewed pressure. That is why Non-Cooperation follows 1919, and why Civil Disobedience follows the Simon Commission.
>
> This framing answers a whole family of Mains questions and, increasingly, "which of the following best explains" Prelims questions — where no date will help you and only the pattern will.

</details>

### 19.
The Government of India Act, 1935 provided that the Governor-General would exercise residuary powers **in his discretion**. The significance of the words "in his discretion" is that

- (a) he was required to consult the Federal Court before deciding
- (b) he could only act with the concurrence of the Secretary of State
- (c) he acted without being bound by ministerial advice
- (d) the decision was subject to appeal to the Privy Council

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** "In his discretion" is a term of art meaning the officer acts **independently of ministerial advice**.

> 🔑 **Concept — the phrase survives into our Constitution, and it matters.** **Article 163(1)** says the Governor acts on the aid and advice of ministers *"except in so far as he is required... to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion."* And **Article 163(2)** makes the Governor the **final judge** of whether a matter falls within his discretion.
>
> Compare with the **President**, who under **Article 74** (after the **42nd and 44th Amendments**) **must** act on ministerial advice, though he may require reconsideration once.
>
> **So: the President has no discretion clause; the Governor does.** That asymmetry is a direct 1935 inheritance and it is the legal root of most Centre–State friction today. Expect it in Chapters 17 and 30 — and possibly in Prelims 2027 through a current-affairs framing.

</details>

### 21. ⚠️
Which one of the following is **correctly** matched?

- (a) Charter Act, 1813 — Ended the Company's monopoly of trade with China
- (b) Charter Act, 1833 — Introduced open competition for the civil services
- (c) Indian Councils Act, 1892 — Granted the power to vote on the budget
- (d) Government of India Act, 1919 — Established the office of High Commissioner for India

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** The **High Commissioner for India in London** was created by the **1919** Act.

**Why the others are wrong — each is off by exactly one Act:**
- (a) 1813 ended the **India** monopoly; the **China and tea** monopolies ended in **1833**.

> ⚠️ **The "off by one Act" distractor is this chapter's signature.** Every wrong option here is a **true statement about the neighbouring Act**. ### 22. 🔥
**Statement-I:** The Indian Independence Act, 1947 did not itself provide a constitution for independent India.

**Statement-II:** The Act provided that until new constitutions were framed, the two dominions would be governed under the Government of India Act, 1935.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Both correct, and II explains I. The 1947 Act was a **transfer-of-power** statute, not a constitution. Precisely **because** it left constitution-making to the sovereign Constituent Assemblies, it had to specify an interim arrangement — the **1935 Act, as adapted**.

> 🔑 **Concept:** The 1947 Act does three things and no more — it **partitions**, it **transfers sovereignty**, and it **provides an interim framework**. It creates no institutions of governance of its own. Knowing what a statute *does not* do is often the sharper exam skill.

</details>

### 23.
The Karachi Resolution of 1931 is significant in constitutional history because it

- (a) demanded a Constituent Assembly elected on adult franchise
- (b) set out fundamental rights and a programme of economic policy
- (c) accepted the principle of separate electorates
- (d) proposed dominion status as the goal of the national movement

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The **Karachi session (1931)** adopted a resolution on **Fundamental Rights and Economic Policy** — civil liberties, equality before law, universal adult franchise, free primary education, a living wage, protection for workers and peasants, and state ownership of key industries.

**Why not (d):** **Dominion status** as the goal belongs to the **Nehru Report (1928)**; by the **Lahore session (1929)** Congress had already moved to **Purna Swaraj**.

> 🔑 **Concept — the direct ancestry of Parts III and IV.** The Karachi Resolution's **civil liberties** become **Fundamental Rights**; its **socio-economic commitments** become the **Directive Principles**. When you reach Chapter 8 and wonder why the DPSPs read like a political manifesto, the answer is that they largely were one, in 1931.

</details>

### 24. 🔥
Which one of the following sequences correctly represents the growth of the elective principle in India?

- (a) Nomination → indirect election → direct election on restricted franchise → universal adult franchise
- (b) Direct election → indirect election → nomination → universal adult franchise
- (c) Indirect election → nomination → universal adult franchise → direct election
- (d) Nomination → universal adult franchise → direct election → indirect election

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

> **Franchise:** nomination → indirect → restricted direct → universal

### 25. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following statements best describes the relationship between the colonial constitutional Acts and the Constitution of India?

- (a) The Constitution rejected the colonial framework entirely and was drafted without reference to it
- (b) The Constitution retained much of the colonial administrative machinery while replacing its source of authority and its purpose
- (c) The Constitution is merely an amended version of the Government of India Act, 1935
- (d) The Constitution borrowed only from foreign constitutions and not from Indian colonial statutes

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The machinery was **retained** — the parliamentary form, the Governor's office, the three lists, emergency provisions, the ordinance power, the all-India services, the PSCs. What changed was the **source of authority** (from the British Crown to **"We, the People"**) and the **purpose** (from control to self-government).

**Why not the others:**
- (a) and (d) are contradicted by the borrowings themselves.
- (c) understates it decisively — the Constitution added **Fundamental Rights**, **universal adult franchise**, **an independent Supreme Court with judicial review**, **DPSPs**, and a **Preamble grounding authority in the people**. None of these existed in 1935. A machine with the same gears but a different engine and a different driver is not an amended version of the old machine.

> 🔑 **The big idea of this chapter — and the sentence to take into Chapter 3.**
>
> ***"We inherited the machinery and replaced the purpose."***
>
> **Kept:** parliamentary system · Governor · three lists · emergency provisions · ordinance power · all-India services · PSCs · ordinance-making · bicameralism · staggered upper-house terms.
> **Refused:** separate electorates · official majorities · an irremovable executive · a property franchise · finality of the Privy Council · government by discretion.
> **Added:** Fundamental Rights · universal adult franchise · judicial review · DPSPs · popular sovereignty.
>
> 📰 This is exactly the framing that dominated the public conversation around **26 November 2024**, the 75th anniversary of the Constitution's adoption — and it is the framing UPSC's analytical questions increasingly reward. **Three columns. Sort anything you meet into one of them.**

</details>
