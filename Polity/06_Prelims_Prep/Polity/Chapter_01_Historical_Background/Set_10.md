### 1. 🔥
Which one of the following statements best reflects why the colonial Acts from 1773 onwards are studied as "constitutional" development, though none of them was a constitution?

- (a) They were drafted by constitutional lawyers in Britain
- (b) They were approved by representative Indian assemblies
- (c) They progressively created offices, defined their powers, and made those powers subject to written rules
- (d) They guaranteed enforceable rights to Indian subjects

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Constitutional development means the **structuring and limiting of public power by written rule**. These Acts created offices (Governor-General, Secretary of State, Federal Court), prescribed their composition and competence, and made them operate through published law.

**Why not (d):** Enforceable **rights against the State** appear nowhere in colonial statutes. That is the sharpest single break between them and the Constitution.

> 🔑 **This is the same idea Prelims 2023 tested** — *"the chief purpose of a Constitution is that it defines and limits the powers of government."* Constitutionalism is about **rule-bound power**, which can exist without democracy. Democracy is about **who holds the power**, which is a separate question. Colonial India had a growing measure of the first and almost none of the second.

</details>

### 2. 🔥 ⚠️
Consider the following statements:

1. The Government of India Act, 1919 separated central and provincial subjects, and the Act of 1935 replaced this with three lists.
2. Under both Acts, residuary powers rested with the Governor-General.
3. The Constitution of India vests residuary powers in the State Legislatures.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Only statement 1 is correct — the two-category scheme of 1919 became the three lists of 1935.

**Why not 3:** **Article 248 read with Entry 97 of the Union List** vests residuary powers in **Parliament**, not in the States. This is the most commonly mistaken fact in Indian federalism: USA, Australia and Switzerland give residuary powers to the states; **Canada and India give them to the Centre**.

**Why not 2:** The **1919 Act made no provision for residuary powers at all** — the question did not arise, because the structure remained **unitary** and the Centre could legislate as it wished. Residuary power becomes meaningful only once a **genuine division** of powers exists, which is why it first appears in **1935**.

> ⚠️ **Statement 2 is subtle, and it is the hardest kind of false statement:** it applies a **later** Act's concept **backwards** to an earlier one. The 1935 answer (Governor-General) is correct; the claim that it also holds for 1919 is not.
>
> 🔑 **The underlying principle is worth more than the fact.** *Residuary power presupposes an exhaustive division of powers.* Where there is no true division, there is no residue. That idea explains why 1919 has no residuary clause and why every genuine federation must have one.

</details>

### 3. 🔥 📰
The Governor's discretionary power under **Article 163** of the Constitution is most directly traceable to

- (a) the Governor's overriding powers under the Government of India Act, 1919
- (b) the Governor's "special responsibilities" and discretion under the Government of India Act, 1935
- (c) the Viceroy's ordinance-making power under the Indian Councils Act, 1861
- (d) the Governor-General's residuary powers under the Government of India Act, 1935

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Under the 1935 Act the Governor acted on ministerial advice **except** where he had **special responsibilities** or was to act **in his discretion**. **Article 163(1)** reproduces that architecture almost exactly, and **Article 163(2)** makes the Governor the **final judge** of whether a matter falls within his discretion.

**Why not (a):** In 1919 the Governor was not bound by advice at all for reserved subjects — a **stronger** position, and structurally different from a discretion **carved out of** a general duty to follow advice.

> 📰 **The single most live colonial inheritance in Indian polity today.** Note the asymmetry that follows from it:
> **President — Article 74:** *shall* act on ministerial advice (after the **42nd Amendment**); may require reconsideration **once**, then is bound (**44th Amendment**). **No discretion clause.**
> **Governor — Article 163:** bound by advice **except in his discretion**, and he judges the exception himself.
>
> That asymmetry generates the recurring controversies you read about — **delay in assenting to State Bills (Article 200)**, **reservation of Bills for the President (Article 201)**, and government formation in hung assemblies. The Supreme Court has repeatedly been drawn in, including on gubernatorial delay in **Punjab** and **Tamil Nadu**.
>
> A Prelims question can enter from **1935** or from **today's headlines**. They are the same provision.

</details>

### 4. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why India adopted a strong Centre despite calling itself federal?

- (a) The Constituent Assembly copied the American model without modification
- (b) The Cabinet Mission Plan had recommended a strong centre
- (c) Colonial practice had centralised power for a century, and Partition and princely-state integration made fragmentation the immediate danger
- (d) The Government of India Act, 1935 vested residuary powers in the provinces

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** Two forces converged. Colonial administration had centralised steadily since **1833**, so a strong Centre was the **inherited default**. And in 1947–49 the Assembly faced Partition, mass migration, communal violence and 560 states to integrate — circumstances in which **fragmentation, not tyranny, was the visible danger**.

**Why not (b):** The **Cabinet Mission proposed the opposite** — a **weak** centre confined to defence, foreign affairs and communications, with residuary powers in the provinces. Its failure, and Partition, is precisely what **discredited** the weak-centre model in the Assembly's eyes.

> 🔑 **Concept — this is the best single answer to "why is Indian federalism quasi-federal", and it is a Mains-grade point:**
> **Inheritance** (centralised colonial machinery) + **circumstance** (Partition and integration) + **reaction** (the Cabinet Mission's weak centre had just failed) = a strong-centre federation.
>
> Hence: residuary powers with the Union, Article 3 allowing Parliament to reshape states, Article 356, all-India services, and a single integrated judiciary. **Chapters 13 and 14 are the elaboration of this answer.**

</details>

### 5. 🔥 ⚠️
Consider the following statements:

1. The Charter Act of 1833 declared that no Indian should be debarred from office by reason of religion, place of birth, descent or colour.
2. This declaration was given practical effect immediately through open competitive examinations.
3. The principle now appears in Article 29(2) of the Constitution.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** Only statement 1 is correct.

**Why not 3:** The principle appears in **Article 16(2)** — no discrimination in **employment or office under the State**. **Article 29(2)** concerns admission to **educational institutions**, a different subject entirely. When several Articles feel close, match the **subject matter**: the 1833 clause is about *holding office*, so it is **16(2)**.

**Why not 2:** The **open-competition provision of 1833 was negated** after the Court of Directors objected. Competition came only in **1853**, and even then the examination was held **in London alone** until **1922**, when it began to be held in India as well — a practical barrier that kept Indian entry minimal for decades.

> ⚠️ **The gap between declaration and effect is this chapter's most reliable source of false statements.** The British repeatedly announced a principle and withheld its machinery: the 1833 non-discrimination clause, the 1858 Queen's Proclamation promise of equal admission to office, the 1917 promise of responsible government.
>
> 🔑 **Rule for the exam:** when a colonial statute *declares* something admirable, ask whether it was ever *implemented*. Roughly half the time the answer is no, and that is where the question is.

</details>

### 6. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following pairs of dates is correctly matched with what happened on them?

- (a) 26 November 1949 — Constitution came into force
- (b) 26 January 1950 — Constitution adopted by the Constituent Assembly
- (c) 26 November 1949 — Constitution adopted; 26 January 1950 — Constitution came into force
- (d) 15 August 1947 — India became a sovereign democratic republic

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Adopted 26 November 1949; in force 26 January 1950.**

**Why not (d):** On 15 August 1947 India became **independent and sovereign** but was a **dominion**, not a republic. The republic dates from 26 January 1950.

> 📰 **Why 26 January?** It commemorates **26 January 1930**, the first **Purna Swaraj Day**, observed after the Congress's **Lahore session of December 1929** resolved on complete independence. The Assembly deliberately chose to fulfil the 1930 pledge on its anniversary.
>
> 🔑 **Note also:** some provisions — those concerning **citizenship, elections and provisional Parliament** — came into force on **26 November 1949** itself. So the Constitution was not wholly dormant between the two dates. This detail has been examined.
>
> **26 November** is **Samvidhan Divas**; **26 November 2024** marked **75 years of adoption**.

</details>

### 7. 🔥
Which one of the following is the **most accurate** statement about the relationship between the Poona Pact and the Constitution?

- (a) The Constitution adopted separate electorates as recommended by the Poona Pact
- (b) The Constitution adopted the Poona Pact's model of reserved seats within a joint electorate
- (c) The Constitution abolished all forms of reserved representation
- (d) The Constitution extended separate electorates to Scheduled Tribes

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Poona Pact's model — **reserved seats filled by candidates from the community but elected by all voters of the constituency** — is exactly what **Articles 330 and 332** provide for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

> 🔑 **The clean formulation to carry:** **Article 325** kills the separate electorate (one common roll). **Articles 330/332** deliver reserved seats within it. *One roll, some reserved seats.*
>
> 📰 The same architecture governs the **106th Amendment (2023)** reserving one-third of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats for **women** — again reserved seats, never a separate electorate. It takes effect after a **census and delimitation**. And the **104th Amendment (2019)** extended SC/ST reservation to **2030** while **ending the nomination of Anglo-Indians**.
>
> A 1932 compromise remains the operating principle of Indian representation in 2026.

</details>

### 8. ⚠️
Consider the following statements about the Federal Court established in 1937:

1. It had original jurisdiction in disputes between the federation and the provinces.
2. Its decisions were final and not subject to any further appeal.
3. It was replaced by the Supreme Court of India in 1950.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statements 1 and 3 are correct.

**Why not 2:** Appeals from the Federal Court lay to the **Privy Council in London**. Its jurisdiction was abolished only by the **Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949**.

> 🔑 **Test the word "final".** Before 1949, finality in Indian law was always in **London** — the Privy Council for courts, the Secretary of State for administration, the British Parliament for legislation. Any pre-1947 option asserting finality **in India** deserves immediate suspicion.

</details>

### 9. 🔥
Which one of the following features of the Indian Constitution has **no** colonial antecedent in the Acts studied in this chapter?

- (a) Emergency provisions
- (b) The office of Governor
- (c) Judicial review of legislation for violation of fundamental rights
- (d) The all-India services

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Judicial review for violation of fundamental rights** has no colonial ancestor, because there were **no fundamental rights to violate**. Colonial courts could test whether a law was *within the powers* conferred by the relevant Act — a limited form of review — but never whether it infringed a citizen's guaranteed rights.

**Why the others have clear ancestry:** emergency provisions and the Governor's office from the **1935 Act**; the all-India services from the **ICS**, retained under **Article 312**.

> 🔑 **This question defines the boundary of the chapter's central claim.** "Two-thirds borrowed" is true of the **machinery**. It is **not** true of:
> **Fundamental Rights** (Part III) · **judicial review for rights violations** · **universal adult franchise** · **DPSPs** · **popular sovereignty in the Preamble** · **a single integrated judiciary with a final Indian apex**.
>
> Those are the **additions**, and they are what convert an administrative apparatus into a constitution.

</details>

### 10. 🔥 📰
The colonial-era ordinance power and the constitutional ordinance power differ principally in that

- (a) the colonial ordinance lasted six months, while an ordinance under Article 123 ceases six weeks after Parliament reassembles
- (b) the colonial ordinance required legislative approval, while Article 123 does not
- (c) the colonial ordinance could only be issued in wartime
- (d) Article 123 ordinances cannot be challenged in court

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** **Colonial (1861): six months, on the Viceroy's own authority.** **Article 123: must be laid before both Houses and ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly** (or earlier if both Houses disapprove) — and is issued on the **advice of the Council of Ministers**, not personally.

**Why not (d):** Ordinances **are** justiciable. **D.C. Wadhwa (1987)** condemned serial re-promulgation, and **Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017)** held re-promulgation a **fraud on the Constitution** and made the satisfaction of the President/Governor reviewable.

> 📰 **Ordinance-making was a featured topic in Prelims 2025**, and the **six months vs six weeks** confusion is the designed trap. Also worth knowing: an ordinance has the **same force and effect as an Act**, cannot be issued when **both Houses are in session**, and cannot amend the Constitution.

</details>

### 11. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why the Government of India Act, 1935 continued to govern India after independence?

- (a) The Constituent Assembly lacked the authority to replace it
- (b) The Indian Independence Act, 1947 expressly provided for it as an interim arrangement pending new constitutions
- (c) The British Parliament retained legislative authority over India until 1950
- (d) The Act had been drafted with Indian participation and was acceptable to all parties

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The 1947 Act expressly continued the **1935 Act, as adapted**, as the working constitution of both dominions until each framed its own.

**Why not (a) and (c):** The Assembly was **fully sovereign** from 1947 and could repeal any British statute, and the British Parliament retained **no** authority over India. The continuation was a **practical necessity**, not a legal constraint — a country cannot govern itself for two years with no constitutional framework while a new one is written.

> 🔑 **Concept — the fact worth the mark:** independent India was governed by a **colonial statute for two years and five months**, from 15 August 1947 to 26 January 1950. Pakistan continued under it until **1956**.
>
> That single fact is the most concrete possible proof of the "blueprint" claim — and it is why our Constitution's administrative provisions read as they do.

</details>

### 12. ⚠️
Consider the following statements:

1. Provincial autonomy under the 1935 Act operated from 1937 to 1939.
2. It ceased because the Congress ministries resigned over India's entry into the Second World War.
3. Provincial autonomy was restored in 1942 after the Cripps Mission.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Statements 1 and 2 are correct.

**Why not 3:** The **Cripps Mission failed**, and provincial autonomy was **not restored** in 1942. In provinces where ministries had resigned, **Governor's rule under Section 93** of the 1935 Act continued. Congress governments returned only after the **1946 elections**.

> 🔑 **Section 93 of the 1935 Act is worth knowing by name.** It empowered a Governor to assume all powers of government where the constitutional machinery in a province failed — and it is the **direct ancestor of Article 356, President's Rule**.
>
> The colonial provision was used to govern provinces **without** their elected ministries during 1939–46. Its constitutional descendant became the most controversial article in Indian federalism, restrained only by **S.R. Bommai (1994)**. When you reach **Chapter 16**, remember that Article 356 has been in continuous use, under one name or another, since 1937.

</details>

### 13. 🔥
Which one of the following statements about the franchise is correct?

- (a) The 1919 Act extended the vote to about 10% of the population and the 1935 Act to about 25%
- (b) Universal adult franchise was introduced by the Government of India Act, 1935
- (c) Both Acts extended the vote to about 10%
- (d) The 1919 Act extended the vote to about 3% and the 1935 Act to about 10%

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** Roughly **3% in 1919**, roughly **10% in 1935**, and **100% of adults in 1950** under **Article 326** (voting age lowered from 21 to **18** by the **61st Amendment, 1988**).

> 🔑 **The scale of the 1950 jump deserves a moment.** India went from one adult in ten to every adult, at once, with mass poverty and roughly **16% literacy** — and did it *before* many older democracies had extended the vote fully. It is the boldest decision in the Constitution, and it is the strongest evidence that 1950 was a **break** with colonial constitutional logic, not a continuation of it.
>
> That logic held that the vote must be **earned** through property or education. The Constitution held that it belongs to you **because you are an adult citizen**. Everything in Chapter 3 follows from that inversion.

</details>

### 14. 🔥 📰
The all-India services retained under **Article 312** of the Constitution are best described as

- (a) an entirely new creation of the Constituent Assembly with no colonial precedent
- (b) services abolished in 1947 and revived in 1966
- (c) services recruited and controlled solely by the State governments
- (d) a colonial instrument of central control, deliberately retained as an instrument of national integration

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** The **Indian Civil Service** was designed to give London a cadre of officers loyal to the Centre and posted throughout India. The Constituent Assembly retained the design **knowingly**, converting it into the **IAS and IPS** — recruited centrally by the UPSC, but serving both the Union and the States.

**Why not (b):** The services continued without a break. The **Indian Forest Service** was created later, in **1966**, as the third all-India service — which is what the option distorts.

> 🔑 **Sardar Patel's defence in the Constituent Assembly gave it its name — the "steel frame"** holding a fragmenting country together. He argued that without an officer corps owing loyalty beyond the province, India would not hold.
>
> 📰 The dual-control structure is still contested: officers are recruited by the **Union** but serve in **State** cadres, and disputes over **deputation** and **cadre rules** recur regularly in Centre–State relations. Both **Chapter 14** and current affairs draw on this.
>
> **The irony is the examinable point:** an instrument built for imperial control was kept, deliberately, to hold a free nation together.

</details>

### 15. 🔥
Arrange the following in order of increasing devolution of power to Indians:

1. Indian Councils Act, 1892
2. Government of India Act, 1919
3. Indian Councils Act, 1909
4. Government of India Act, 1935

Select the correct answer using the code given below:

- (a) 1 – 3 – 4 – 2
- (b) 3 – 1 – 2 – 4
- (c) 1 – 2 – 3 – 4
- (d) 1 – 3 – 2 – 4

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** **1892** (discussion, indirect nomination) → **1909** (restricted direct election, executive council opened) → **1919** (dyarchy, partial responsibility, direct election) → **1935** (provincial autonomy, full responsible government in provinces).

> 🔑 **Note that here chronological order and order of devolution coincide** — which is itself a fact about this chapter: **every Act conceded more than its predecessor**. There is no reversal anywhere in the sequence.
>
> That monotonic quality is worth remembering, because it means that when you are unsure whether a power belonged to an earlier or a later Act, **the later Act is the safer guess**. It is not a certainty, but it is a good prior.

</details>

### 16. 🔥 ⚠️
**Statement-I:** Under the Government of India Act, 1935, India functioned as a federal state from 1937 onwards.

**Statement-II:** The federal provisions of the Act were never brought into operation, while the Governor-General retained residuary and overriding powers throughout.

Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above statements?

- (a) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (b) Both Statement-I and Statement-II are correct and Statement-II is not the correct explanation for Statement-I
- (c) Statement-I is correct but Statement-II is incorrect
- (d) Statement-I is incorrect but Statement-II is correct

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** **Statement-I is false; Statement-II is true**, and II is the reason I is false.

What began in **1937** was **provincial autonomy**, not federation. The **All-India Federation never came into being** because the princely states did not accede, and **dyarchy at the Centre** — which depended on it — never operated either. With the Governor-General retaining residuary and overriding powers, India remained **in practice a unitary state** until 1947.

> 🔑 **Concept — the distinction between a constitution's text and its operation.** The 1935 Act was federal **on paper** and unitary **in fact**. Prelims tests this repeatedly through the phrase *"came into operation"*.
>
> The same distinction matters for our own Constitution. Ambedkar described it as **federal in normal times and unitary in emergencies**, and during a **National Emergency under Article 352** Parliament may legislate on the **State List** and the Union may direct States on any matter. The text does not change; its operation does.

</details>

### 17.
The Instrument of Accession used in 1947 required the acceding princely states to cede which subjects to the Dominion?

- (a) Defence, external affairs and communications
- (b) Defence, finance and law and order
- (c) All subjects in the Federal List
- (d) Defence alone

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** **Defence, external affairs and communications** — the three subjects the **Cabinet Mission** had also proposed for its weak centre, and the same three the **1935 Act's** accession scheme had contemplated.

> 🔑 **Concept:** The limited cession is exactly why fuller integration required a **second stage** — merger agreements, the reorganisation of states, and eventually the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**. Accession was a legal minimum; integration was a political achievement.
>
> 📰 It is also why **Jammu & Kashmir's** case was distinct: it acceded on these three subjects in **October 1947** and did not sign a merger agreement, which is the origin of **Article 370**. The Supreme Court's **December 2023** judgment upholding the abrogation held that accession left J&K with **no residual internal sovereignty**.

</details>

### 18. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains why the Constituent Assembly rejected the Cabinet Mission's model of a weak centre?

- (a) The Cabinet Mission Plan had already been legally superseded
- (b) Partition removed the political reason for a weak centre, and made national cohesion the overriding priority
- (c) The princely states demanded a strong centre
- (d) The Government of India Act, 1935 required a strong centre

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The weak centre and provincial grouping existed **to accommodate the Muslim League within a united India**. Once **Partition** occurred, that purpose vanished — and the immediate lesson drawn from the preceding two years was that a weak centre invites fragmentation.

> 🔑 **Concept — one of the sharpest cause-and-effect chains in the syllabus.** Nehru's own account is direct: with Partition, the Assembly was free to build the strong, unitary-leaning federation it wanted.
>
> **So the shape of Indian federalism is a consequence of Partition.** Had the Cabinet Mission succeeded, we would plausibly have a genuinely weak centre with residuary powers in the states. This is excellent Mains material and increasingly plausible as an analytical Prelims question — and it is the answer to *"why is India quasi-federal"* that most candidates never reach.

</details>

### 19. ⚠️
Consider the following statements:

1. The Queen's Proclamation of 1858 announced the continuation of the policy of territorial annexation.
2. The Proclamation's promise on admission to public office was substantially fulfilled during the nineteenth century.
3. Indian nationalists rejected the Proclamation as irrelevant and made no use of it in their arguments.

How many of the statements given above are correct?

- (a) Only one
- (b) Only two
- (c) All three
- (d) None

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d) — all three are wrong:**
- **1** The Proclamation **renounced** further annexation, ending the Doctrine of Lapse. It is the opposite of what the statement says.
- **3** Nationalists did the reverse — they **repeatedly cited** the Proclamation to expose the gap between British promise and British practice.
- **2** The promise was **not** fulfilled. The civil service examination was held **only in London** until **1922**, the maximum age was repeatedly lowered, and Indians remained a small minority of the ICS well into the twentieth century. The **Aitchison Commission (1886)** and **Islington Commission (1912)** both examined the grievance without materially resolving it.

> 🔑 **Concept:** The gap between the 1858 promise and its performance is what made the Proclamation such an effective **nationalist weapon** — the demand was not for something new but for **the Crown's own word to be honoured**. Dadabhai Naoroji and the early Congress built the case for **simultaneous examinations in India** on precisely this footing.
>
> ⚠️ Statement 2 is the pattern from Q5 again: **declaration made, effect withheld.** In this chapter, that pattern is worth checking every single time.

</details>

### 20. 🔥
Which one of the following correctly describes what the Indian Independence Act, 1947 did **and** did not do?

- (a) It transferred power and framed a constitution for India
- (b) It transferred power but did not provide for partition
- (c) It framed a constitution and established the Constituent Assembly
- (d) It transferred power, partitioned India and provided an interim framework, but did not frame a constitution

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Why (d):** The Act does exactly three things — **partitions**, **transfers sovereignty**, and **continues the 1935 Act as an interim framework**. It creates no constitution, and it did not create the Constituent Assembly (that was the **Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946**; the 1947 Act made it **sovereign**).

> 🔑 **Exam skill worth generalising:** knowing what a statute **does not** do is often sharper than knowing what it does. Apply it across the chapter — the 1858 Act did **not** change the system of government in India; the 1909 Act did **not** create responsible government; the 1935 federation did **not** come into being; the 1947 Act did **not** frame a constitution.
>
> Four negatives, four frequently-examined facts.

</details>

### 21. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following statements about the replacement of colonial-era laws is correct?

- (a) The Indian Penal Code was drafted by the first Law Commission under the Charter Act of 1833 and was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita with effect from 1 July 2024
- (b) The Indian Penal Code was enacted in 1833 and amended in 2024
- (c) The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita amended the Indian Penal Code without replacing it
- (d) The Code of Criminal Procedure was retained unchanged in 2024

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (a)**

**Why (a):** The **first Law Commission (1834)**, chaired by **Macaulay** and provided for by the **Charter Act of 1833**, drafted the IPC, which was enacted in **1860** and came into force in **1862**. It was **replaced** — not amended — by the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**, effective **1 July 2024**.

**Why not (c) and (d):** All three criminal laws were **repealed and replaced**:
| Colonial law | Replaced by |
|---|---|
| **Indian Penal Code, 1860** | **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023** |
| **Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973** | **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023** |
| **Indian Evidence Act, 1872** | **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** |

> 📰 **A very high-probability current-affairs link, and note why it belongs in *this* chapter:** the IPC's authority traces to the Charter Act of **1833**, so a question can enter from either end. **Repeal-and-replace, not amendment** is the trap.

</details>

### 22. 🔥
Which one of the following features did the Indian Constitution **refuse** to inherit from the colonial Acts?

- (a) The parliamentary system of government
- (b) The office of Governor
- (c) Communal electorates and an executive not answerable to the legislature
- (d) The three-list division of legislative powers

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (c)**

**Why (c):** **Separate electorates** were abolished by **Article 325**, and an **unaccountable executive** was replaced by **collective responsibility to the legislature** under **Articles 75(3) and 164(2)**.

**Why the others were kept:** the parliamentary system (from Britain, reinforced by colonial practice), the Governor's office and the three lists (both from the **1935 Act**).

> 🔑 **The three-column framework, one last time — this is the deliverable of the entire chapter:**
>
> | **Kept** | **Refused** | **Added** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Parliamentary system | Separate electorates | Fundamental Rights |
> | Governor's office | Official majorities | Universal adult franchise |
> | Three lists / Seventh Schedule | Unaccountable executive | Judicial review for rights |
> | Emergency provisions | Property-based franchise | Directive Principles |
> | Ordinance power | Privy Council finality | Popular sovereignty |
> | All-India services, PSCs | Government by discretion | Single integrated judiciary |
> | Bicameralism, staggered terms | Paramountcy over princes | An amendable, sovereign text |
>
> **Sort anything you meet into one of these three columns.** That single skill answers the analytical questions this chapter now generates, and it is the bridge into **Chapter 3, Salient Features**.

</details>

### 23. 🔥
Which one of the following best explains the statement that the Indian Constitution derives its authority from the people, even though its immediate legal origin lies in a British statute?

- (a) The Constituent Assembly was elected by universal adult franchise
- (b) The Constitution was enacted in the name of the people, and the Assembly's power to repeal the Independence Act itself severed the colonial legal chain
- (c) The British Parliament formally renounced its authority in 1950
- (d) The Constitution was ratified by a national referendum

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** The Preamble declares that **"We, the People of India... adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution."** And because the 1947 Act empowered the Assembly to **repeal any British statute, including that Act itself**, the new Constitution rests on its **own** foundation rather than on a British grant.

**Why not (a):** ⚠️ **Important and frequently mistaken** — the Constituent Assembly was **not** elected by universal adult franchise. It was **indirectly elected** by the provincial legislative assemblies, which had themselves been elected on the **restricted ~10% franchise** of the 1935 Act. The Assembly **gave** universal franchise; it was not **produced** by it.

**Why not (d):** There was **no referendum**. The Constitution was adopted by the Assembly, not put to a popular vote.

> 🔑 **This is the most sophisticated question in the chapter, and it prepares you for two later ones.**
>
> The legitimacy of the Constitution does not rest on the manner of the Assembly's election. It rests on the **doctrine of popular sovereignty** declared in the Preamble — which is why the Preamble's opening words were the subject of such careful drafting, and why the Supreme Court in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** treated the Preamble as part of the Constitution and its basic features as unamendable.
>
> Expect the "was the Assembly representative?" criticism in **Chapter 2**, and the sovereignty question in **Chapter 4**. You now have the answer to both.

</details>

### 24. 🔥 📰
Which one of the following best captures why questions from this chapter increasingly test understanding rather than recall?

- (a) UPSC has removed all factual questions from the Prelims paper
- (b) Recent papers reward knowing what a constitutional term means and why a provision exists, since many questions offer no date to recall
- (c) The chapter has been removed from the syllabus
- (d) Only current-affairs questions are now asked in Polity

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** Recent Prelims papers have moved sharply towards **analytical** framing — long statement-based formats, "which one best describes" questions, and Statement-I/II reasoning. Polity remains one of the largest components of the paper, and in 2026 the overwhelming majority of Polity questions were long statement-based rather than one-line factual.

**Why not (a):** Factual questions certainly remain — dates, numbers and names are still asked. The shift is one of **balance**, not of elimination. Do not read this as permission to skip the facts.

> **Sets 08–10** built the **framework** — the ladders, the mappings, the three columns.
>
> On revision, the facts will fade first and the framework will survive. That is why **this set is your revision entry point**: it reconstructs the facts *through* the framework, which is the order in which they are actually retrievable under exam pressure.

### 25. 🔥
Which one of the following best states the central lesson of the historical background of the Indian Constitution?

- (a) The Constitution was a wholly original document owing nothing to what preceded it
- (b) The Constitution inherited a colonial administrative machinery and re-founded it on popular sovereignty, adding rights and franchise that the colonial order had denied
- (c) The Constitution merely renamed the Government of India Act, 1935
- (d) The Constitution abandoned all colonial institutions and built entirely new ones

<details><summary>Answer & Explanation</summary>

**Answer: (b)**

**Why (b):** It is the only option that holds both halves of the truth together — **continuity of machinery** and **discontinuity of authority and purpose**. Options (a) and (d) deny the continuity; option (c) denies the transformation.

> 🔑 **The chapter, finished.**
>
> ***"We inherited the machinery and replaced the purpose."***
>
> The parliamentary system, the Governor, the three lists, the emergency provisions, the ordinance power, the all-India services, the Public Service Commissions — all colonial in origin, all retained. And then: **a Preamble grounding authority in the people**, **Fundamental Rights enforceable against the State**, **universal adult franchise**, **judicial review**, **Directive Principles**, and a **final court sitting in India**.
>
> The same instruments, answerable now to Indians rather than to London. That is what independence meant constitutionally, and it is the answer to almost any question this chapter can ask you.
>
> **You are ready for [Chapter 2 — Making of the Constitution](../../../02_Laxmikanth/Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/02_Making_of_the_Constitution.md),** where the Constituent Assembly you have met four times in this set becomes the whole subject.

</details>
