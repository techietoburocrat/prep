# Chapter 29 — Public Interest Litigation

[← Chapter 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md)

**Read time ~8 min** · **The last chapter of Part III** · 🟠 Prelims: occasional · Mains: **2024** directly, and the theme in several others

> 📌 **PIL is the *doorway* that made [Ch 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md) possible.** Judicial activism needed cases to act in — and the traditional rule of *locus standi* meant the people whose rights were most violated were precisely the people who could never file. **PIL removed that door.** Read the two chapters as cause and effect.

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **PIL abolished the requirement that you must be the injured party to go to court.** Any public-spirited person may now approach the Supreme Court or a High Court **on behalf of** those who cannot — the poor, the illiterate, the imprisoned, the disabled. That single procedural change did more for access to justice in India than any statute, and it is also the source of every complaint about frivolous litigation clogging the courts.

---

## 📖 The story

The old rule was called ***locus standi*** — **standing**. Only the person whose own legal right had been violated could sue.

For most disputes that is sensible. It stops busybodies and keeps courts focused on real grievances.

**But it produces an absurdity at the bottom of society.** A bonded labourer does not know he has a right, cannot read the statute that protects him, cannot afford a lawyer, and cannot leave the quarry to reach a court. An undertrial has been in jail for nine years awaiting trial for an offence carrying a maximum of two — and cannot file anything from inside. **Under the old rule, the Constitution's rights simply did not reach them, because nobody with standing had any reason to litigate.**

> 💡 **The analogy:** a hospital with an excellent emergency department that admits patients **only if they walk in unaided**. The care is real; the entry condition guarantees that the sickest people never receive it. **PIL is the ambulance service** — it lets someone else bring the patient in.
>
> And the objection is the predictable one: **once anyone can call an ambulance for anyone, some calls will be frivolous, malicious, or made for publicity.** Both the achievement and the abuse follow from the same change.

**The turning point was 1979.** Advocate Kapila Hingorani filed a petition based on a **newspaper report** about undertrial prisoners in Bihar — people who had already spent longer in custody than the maximum sentence they faced. In ***Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar***, the Court entertained it, read a **right to speedy trial** into Article 21, and ordered releases that eventually ran to **over 40,000 undertrials**.

---

## PART A — Meaning and origin ⭐

### What it is

> **Litigation filed in a court of law for the protection of the public interest**, such as pollution, terrorism, road safety and constructional hazards. ⚠️ **It is not defined in any statute or Act** — its meaning has been developed by the judges.

**The other names it goes by:** ⭐ **Social Action Litigation (SAL)** · **Social Interest Litigation (SIL)** · **Class Action Litigation (CAL)** · **Public Interest Lawyering**.

### ⭐ The essential feature

> **The traditional rule of *locus standi* — that only the person whose own right is infringed may move the court — has been relaxed.** ⭐ **Any public-spirited person can file a petition on behalf of a person or class of persons who, by reason of POVERTY, DISABILITY or a SOCIALLY OR ECONOMICALLY DISADVANTAGED POSITION, cannot approach the court themselves.**

### Origin and evolution ⭐

| Stage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Origin** | The concept began in the **USA in the 1960s**, as a means of providing legal representation to **unrepresented groups and interests** |
| **India — the seed** | ⭐ Justice **V.R. Krishna Iyer**, in ***Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai* (1976)**, first indicated the relaxation of *locus standi* |
| ⭐ **The first PIL** | ***Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1979)** — on the **inhuman conditions of undertrial prisoners in Bihar**. **Right to speedy trial** read into Article 21; **over 40,000 undertrials released** |
| ⭐ **The formal doctrine** | ***S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981)** — Justice **P.N. Bhagwati** articulated the concept fully and **formally relaxed *locus standi***. *(The same case is the **First Judges Case** — see [Ch 26](26_Supreme_Court.md).)* |

> 🧠 **Two judges, two contributions:** ⭐ **Krishna Iyer opened the door (1976); Bhagwati built the doctrine (1981).** And **Bhagwati is generally regarded as the father of PIL in India.**

### ⭐ Epistolary jurisdiction

> The Court has treated **letters and even postcards** addressed to it — and **newspaper reports** — as **writ petitions**. This is called **epistolary jurisdiction**, and it is India's most distinctive procedural innovation: it removes the cost of a lawyer, court fees and formal drafting from the act of seeking a remedy.

---

## PART B — The features that make PIL different ⭐

| Feature | How it differs from ordinary litigation |
|---|---|
| ⭐ ***Locus standi* relaxed** | **Anyone** may file on behalf of the disadvantaged — not just the injured party |
| ⭐ **Not adversarial but collaborative** | The court, the petitioner and the State are treated as working **towards a common remedy**, not as opposing parties fighting to win |
| ⭐ **Investigative role** | The Court may **appoint commissions** or committees to **gather facts and report** — it does not rely solely on the parties' evidence |
| ⭐ **Continuing mandamus** | The case is **kept open** and the Court **supervises implementation** over months or years, issuing directions as needed |
| **Flexible procedure** | **Epistolary jurisdiction**; relaxed rules of pleading and evidence |
| **Remedial creativity** | Compensation, guidelines, monitoring committees — remedies not confined to what a statute prescribes |

### Where PIL may be filed and against whom

- ⭐ In the **Supreme Court under Article 32** *(for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights)* or in a **High Court under Article 226** *(for FRs **and any other purpose**)* — see [Ch 26](26_Supreme_Court.md).
- ⚠️ **PIL can be filed only against the State** — the Central or State government, municipal authorities, and **not against a private party**. *(A private party may, however, be **joined** as a respondent along with the State.)*

### ⭐ The subject matter the Court entertains

The Supreme Court's **guidelines** list the categories of letters and petitions to be treated as PIL, including:
**Bonded labour** · **neglected children** · **non-payment of minimum wages** and exploitation of casual labour · **petitions from jails** *(harassment, speedy trial, death in custody)* · petitions against **police** *(refusal to register a case, custodial violence)* · **atrocities on women** *(bride burning, rape, murder, kidnapping)* · petitions on **communal riots** · petitions against **atrocities on SCs and STs** · **environmental pollution**, ecological imbalance, drugs, food adulteration, **maintenance of heritage and culture**, antiques, forest and wildlife.

⚠️ **What is NOT entertained:** landlord–tenant matters, service matters and pensions, admission to medical and other educational institutions, and early hearing of cases pending in High Courts and subordinate courts.

## PART C — ⚠️ The abuse, and the response

### The problem

> PIL has been **misused for personal, political and commercial ends**, and the Court has coined labels for it:
> ⭐ **"Publicity Interest Litigation"** · **"Private Interest Litigation"** · **"Politics Interest Litigation"** · **"Paisa Income Litigation"**.

**The consequences:**
1. ⭐ **Docket congestion** — frivolous petitions consume court time that genuine cases need, worsening the very delay PIL was meant to attack
2. **Judicial overreach** — PIL is the **vehicle** through which the Court reaches into policy ([Ch 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md))
3. **Forum shopping** and settling scores with commercial rivals
4. **Stalling projects** — infrastructure and development held up by petitions with no merit
5. ⚠️ **Uneven implementation** — orders are passed and not enforced, which **damages the Court's own authority**

### ⭐ The Court's response

- **Exemplary costs** imposed on frivolous petitioners.
- ⭐ **Guidelines issued (1988, revised 2003 and notably restated in 2010)** on which letters and petitions will be entertained, and the categories listed above.
- The Court has repeatedly warned that PIL should be used for **"the poor and the downtrodden"**, and has emphasised that the petitioner must be **acting *bona fide***, not for personal gain, private profit or political motivation.

> ⭐ **The line to write in a Mains conclusion:** *"The remedy for the abuse of PIL is **stricter gatekeeping, not a narrower doorway**. Restoring the old rule of* locus standi *would exclude precisely the constituency PIL was created to serve, in order to solve a problem created by people who never needed it."*

---

## PART D — What PIL has actually achieved

| Area | Outcome |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Prisoners' rights** | ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** — right to **speedy trial**; **40,000+ undertrials released** |
| **Bonded labour** | ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984)** — enforcement of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 |
| **Environment** | The **Ganga pollution** cases; **Delhi CNG** transition; ⭐ **the "polluter pays" and "precautionary" principles** absorbed into Indian law; the **absolute liability** doctrine *(**Oleum gas leak**, 1986)* |
| ⭐ **Women at work** | ***Vishaka* (1997)** — guidelines against sexual harassment, in force **16 years** until the 2013 Act |
| **Right to food** | ***PUCL v. Union of India* (2001)** — mid-day meals and food-security directions |
| **Right to education** | ***Unni Krishnan* (1993)** — education read into Article 21, a precursor to the **86th Amendment** and Article 21A |
| **Electoral transparency** | ***ADR* (2002)** — candidates must **disclose criminal, financial and educational details**; **NOTA (2013)** |
| **Custodial safeguards** | ***D.K. Basu* (1997)** — binding guidelines on arrest and detention |

> ⭐ **This table is the answer to "has PIL genuinely helped?"** — [Mains 2024 Q14](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2024.md) asks exactly that. **Do not answer it abstractly; answer it with four or five of these.**

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The definition
> ⭐ **Litigation for the protection of the public interest.** ⚠️ **Not defined in any statute** — judge-developed. Also: **SAL · SIL · CAL · Public Interest Lawyering.**

### The three dates
> ⭐ **1976** *Mumbai Kamgar Sabha* — **Krishna Iyer** hints at relaxed *locus standi*
> ⭐ **1979** ***Hussainara Khatoon*** — the **FIRST PIL**; undertrials in **Bihar**; **speedy trial** under Article 21
> ⭐ **1981** ***S.P. Gupta*** — **Bhagwati** formally establishes the doctrine *(also the **First Judges Case**)*

### The essential change
> ⭐ ***Locus standi* relaxed** — anyone may file for those disabled by **poverty, disability, or a socially or economically disadvantaged position**.

### The four procedural signatures
> ⭐ **Epistolary jurisdiction** *(letters and postcards as petitions)* · **collaborative, not adversarial** · **commissions to find facts** · ⭐ **continuing mandamus**.

### The limits
> ⚠️ **Only against the State**, not a private party. ⚠️ **Not entertained:** landlord–tenant, service and pension matters, educational admissions, early hearing requests.

### The abuse
> ⭐ **"Publicity · Private · Politics · Paisa" Interest Litigation.** Remedy: **exemplary costs and guidelines** — not a narrower doorway.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Explain the reasons for the growth of public interest litigation in India. As a result of it, has the Indian Supreme Court emerged as the world's most powerful judiciary?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2024 Q14](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2024.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) **Reasons for growth** — the exclusionary effect of ***locus standi***; **mass poverty and illiteracy**; the **failure of the executive and legislature** ([Ch 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md)); the **expansive reading of Article 21**; **epistolary jurisdiction** removing cost barriers; **Articles 32 and 39A** *(free legal aid)*; and an activist bench from the mid-1970s. (ii) **The case that it is the world's most powerful** — ⭐ **basic structure** *(it can strike down constitutional amendments — almost no other court can)*; ⭐ **it appoints its own judges** *(the collegium, upheld in NJAC)*; **Article 142's** complete-justice power; **Article 136's** near-unlimited appellate reach; PIL and *suo motu* jurisdiction; the sheer breadth of subject matter it has entered. (iii) **The case against** — ⭐ **no enforcement machinery**, so many orders go unimplemented; **massive pendency**; SLPs crowding out constitutional adjudication; power over **process** is not power over **outcomes**. (iv) Conclude: **the widest jurisdiction in the democratic world, coupled with the weakest capacity to enforce it** — powerful in authority, constrained in effect.

**2. "PIL has become 'publicity interest litigation'. Critically examine, and suggest safeguards." (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* Concede the abuse with the Court's own four labels and its consequences *(congestion, stalled projects, forum shopping, overreach)*. Then set against it the achievements table in Part D. **Safeguards:** verification of the petitioner's *bona fides* and credentials; **exemplary costs**; a dedicated **PIL cell** for screening; **prioritisation** of cases affecting the disadvantaged; **time-bound disposal**; and **monitoring mechanisms** so that orders passed are actually implemented. Conclude with the gatekeeping-not-doorway line.

**3. "PIL has diluted the distinction between judicial adjudication and administration." Discuss. (250 words)**
> *Skeleton:* Explain how the **procedural features** do it — commissions to gather facts *(an investigative, not adjudicative, function)*, **continuing mandamus** *(supervision, an executive function)*, and **guidelines** *(a legislative function)*. Illustrate with environmental and prison cases. Then balance: the Court entered these spaces because they were **vacant**; and its interventions are usually **framed as interim** and **subject to legislation**. Conclude on the boundary from [Ch 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md): **enforcing rights and existing law is adjudication; making resource trade-offs is administration.**

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- ⭐ **PIL = litigation for the protection of the public interest.** ⚠️ **Not defined in any statute.** Also called **SAL, SIL, CAL, Public Interest Lawyering**.
- ⭐ **The essence: *locus standi* relaxed** — any public-spirited person may file on behalf of those who cannot approach the court by reason of **poverty, disability, or a socially or economically disadvantaged position**.
- **Origin: USA, 1960s.** India — ⭐ **Krishna Iyer, *Mumbai Kamgar Sabha* (1976)** hints at it; ⭐ **first PIL: *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)**, Bihar undertrials, **speedy trial under Article 21**, **40,000+ released**; ⭐ **formalised by Bhagwati in *S.P. Gupta* (1981)**.
- ⭐ **Epistolary jurisdiction** — **letters, postcards and newspaper reports** treated as writ petitions.
- **Features:** ***locus standi* relaxed · collaborative not adversarial · commissions to find facts · ⭐ continuing mandamus · flexible procedure · creative remedies.**
- **Filed under Article 32 (SC) or 226 (HC).** ⚠️ **Only against the State**, not private parties.
- ⚠️ **Not entertained:** landlord–tenant, service and pension matters, educational admissions, requests for early hearing.
- ⚠️ **Abuse:** ⭐ **"Publicity / Private / Politics / Paisa" Interest Litigation** — causing congestion, stalled projects, forum shopping and overreach. **Response: exemplary costs and the Court's guidelines** on admissible categories.
- **Achievements:** *Hussainara Khatoon* *(undertrials)* · *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* *(bonded labour)* · **Ganga and Delhi CNG** · **polluter pays, precautionary principle, absolute liability** · ⭐ ***Vishaka* (1997)** · ***PUCL* (2001)** mid-day meals · ***Unni Krishnan* (1993)** education · ***ADR* (2002)** candidate disclosure · ***D.K. Basu* (1997)** arrest guidelines.
