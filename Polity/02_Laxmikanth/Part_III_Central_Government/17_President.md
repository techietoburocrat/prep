# Chapter 17 — President

[← Chapter 16](../Part_II_System_of_Government/16_Emergency_Provisions.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 18 →](18_Vice_President.md)

**Read time ~16 min** · **Articles 52–62, 72, 74, 111, 123** · 🔴 Prelims: 1–2 most years · Mains: **2001, 2003, 2007, 2011, 2022, 2025**

> 📌 **[Chapter 12](../Part_II_System_of_Government/12_Parliamentary_System.md) told you the President is the *nominal* executive. This chapter asks the harder question: if he must act on advice, what is left that is genuinely his?** The answer is small but real — and it is almost the entire Prelims and Mains yield of the chapter.

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **The President has enormous powers on paper and almost none in his own right.** Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in him; Article 74 requires him to exercise it on the Council of Ministers' advice. What survives is a **narrow band of situational discretion** — who to invite as Prime Minister, when to send advice back, and how long to sit on a bill — and every examinable controversy lives in that band.

---

## 📖 The story

Read Article 53 alone and you would think India has a presidential system: *"The executive power of the Union shall be vested in the President."*

Read Article 74 and the whole thing inverts: he **"shall act in accordance with"** the advice of the Council of Ministers.

So which is it? The framers' answer is that **the President is where authority formally resides, and the Council of Ministers is where it is actually exercised.** Every act of the Union government is done in his name; almost none is decided by him.

> 💡 **The analogy that fits:** the President is like the **official signatory on a bank account** that someone else operates. Every cheque must carry his signature, and nothing leaves the account without it — which sounds like control. But he does not decide what the cheques are for. His real power is narrow and procedural: he can **ask once why a particular cheque is being written**, he can **delay signing**, and if the account holders fall out among themselves he decides **whom to recognise as the operator**. That is small — until the day the account holders cannot agree, and then it is decisive.

**And that day has come repeatedly.** In 1979, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 1999 no party had a clear majority, and the President's judgement about *whom to invite* effectively determined who governed India. **The powers that look ceremonial in a majority Parliament become the deciding powers in a hung one** — which is precisely why the chapter is worth learning properly.

## PART A — Election, qualification and term

### The electoral college ⭐

The President is elected **indirectly**, by an electoral college consisting of:

1. The **elected** members of both Houses of Parliament
2. The **elected** members of the legislative assemblies of the **states**
3. The **elected** members of the legislative assemblies of the **Union Territories of Delhi and Puducherry** *(added by the 70th Amendment, 1992)*

> ⚠️ **Four exclusions that are asked constantly:**
> - **Nominated members** of either House of Parliament — excluded
> - **Nominated members** of the state legislative assemblies — excluded
> - Members of the **legislative councils** (the upper house of a state) — excluded, whether elected or nominated
> - Members of the legislative assemblies of **other UTs** — excluded
>
> **Consequence worth stating:** because Legislative Council members are excluded, a state's weight in the electoral college does not change according to whether it is bicameral.

### ⭐ The value of the votes — the formula

The election is by **proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote**, and the voting is by **secret ballot**.

| | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Value of an MLA's vote** | *Total population of the state ÷ Total number of elected members of the assembly*, then **÷ 1000** |
| **Value of an MP's vote** | *Total value of votes of all MLAs of all states ÷ Total number of elected members of Parliament* |

⚠️ **"Population" here means the population as ascertained at the 1971 Census** — frozen by the 42nd Amendment and extended by the 84th Amendment **until the figures of the first census after 2026** are published. *(This is why the electoral college's arithmetic has not shifted with actual population growth — tested in [Mains 2012 Q4(c)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2012.md).)*

**Two principles the Constitution requires:**
1. **Uniformity** in the scale of representation of different states
2. **Parity** between the states as a whole and the Union

### Qualifications

| Must | Detail |
|---|---|
| Be a **citizen of India** | — |
| Have completed **35 years** of age | — |
| Be **qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha** | Not the Rajya Sabha — the Lok Sabha |
| **Not hold any office of profit** under the Union, a state, or any local or other authority | ⚠️ **Exception:** the sitting **President, Vice-President, a Governor, or a Minister** of the Union or a state is **not** disqualified |

**Also required:** the nomination must be subscribed by at least **50 electors as proposers** and **50 as seconders**, with a security deposit of **₹15,000**.

### Term, re-election, oath, vacancy

- **Term: five years** from the date he enters office. He may **resign to the Vice-President**.
- ⭐ **Eligible for re-election any number of times** — unlike the USA, where a two-term limit applies. *(Only **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** has been elected twice.)*
- He **continues to hold office beyond his term until his successor enters office** — so the office is **never vacant**.
- The **oath is administered by the Chief Justice of India**, and in his absence, the **seniormost judge of the Supreme Court available**.
- ⚠️ **An election to fill a vacancy caused by expiry of term must be completed before the expiry.** A vacancy by death, resignation or removal must be filled **within six months**, and the person elected holds a **fresh full five-year term**.

### ⭐ Impeachment (Article 61) — the only way to remove him

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Ground** | **"Violation of the Constitution"** — ⚠️ the Constitution **does not define** what this means |
| **Where it starts** | **Either House** of Parliament |
| **Notice** | Signed by **at least one-fourth of the total members** of that House, with **14 days' notice** |
| **Passing** | By a **special majority — two-thirds of the *total membership*** of that House |
| **The other House** | **Investigates** the charge; the President has the **right to appear and be represented** |
| **Final** | If the second House also passes it by **two-thirds of its total membership**, the President is removed **from the date the resolution is passed** |

> ⭐ **Two facts UPSC loves here:**
> 1. **Nominated members of both Houses can participate in impeachment**, although they did not vote in the election. The reverse is true of the electoral college, where elected MLAs vote but nominated members do not.
> 2. ⚠️ **The elected members of the state legislative assemblies do NOT participate in impeachment**, although they *do* participate in the election. **Election ≠ removal — different bodies.**
>
> **No President has ever been impeached.**

## PART B — The powers ⭐

Group them into six. Almost every question is one of these six.

### ① Executive powers

- All **executive action of the Government of India is formally taken in his name**.
- He may make rules for the more convenient transaction of business of the Union government and for allocating business among ministers.
- **Appoints** the Prime Minister and other ministers, who hold office during his pleasure; the **Attorney General**; the **CAG**, **Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners**, **Chairman and members of the UPSC**, **Governors**, **Chairman and members of the Finance Commission**, and others.
- May seek **any information** from the Prime Minister relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union.
- May appoint a commission to investigate the **conditions of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other backward classes**.
- May declare any area a **scheduled area**, and has powers over the administration of **scheduled areas and tribal areas**.

### ② Legislative powers

- **Summons and prorogues** Parliament, and can **dissolve the Lok Sabha**.
- Can **summon a joint sitting** of both Houses, presided over by the **Speaker**, to resolve a deadlock over an ordinary bill.
- Addresses Parliament at the **first session after each general election and the first session of each year**.
- **Nominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha** *(persons with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service)*.
- ⚠️ **The power to nominate two Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha was abolished by the 104th Amendment Act, 2019.** This is a very common stale-fact trap.
- His **prior recommendation or permission** is needed to introduce certain bills — a **Money Bill**, a bill altering state boundaries or names, a bill affecting taxation in which states are interested, and others.
- Decides questions of **disqualification of MPs in consultation with the Election Commission**.

### ③ ⭐ Veto powers — the highest-yield sub-topic

When a bill is presented to him under **Article 111**, the President may:

| Option | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Give assent** | The bill becomes an Act |
| **Withhold assent** | The bill ends |
| **Return the bill for reconsideration** *(ordinary bills only)* | ⚠️ **If Parliament passes it again, with or without amendments, the President MUST give assent** |

**The three vetoes:**

| Veto | Meaning | Available in India? |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute** | Withholding assent altogether | ✅ Yes |
| **Suspensive** | Returning for reconsideration, overridable by the legislature | ✅ Yes — but overridden by an **ordinary majority**, not a special one |
| **Pocket** | Neither assenting, nor withholding, nor returning — simply **not acting** | ✅ **Yes** — because ⭐ **the Constitution prescribes NO time limit** within which the President must act |
| **Qualified** | Overridable only by a **special** majority | ❌ **Not available to the Indian President** *(the American President has it)* |

> ⭐ **The pocket veto in practice — the one real incident to cite:** in **1986, President Zail Singh** neither signed nor returned the **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill**, which would have allowed the government to intercept post. He simply **did nothing**, and the bill lapsed with the government. It remains the classic illustration.

**Three restrictions to remember:**
1. ⚠️ **The President cannot return a Money Bill** for reconsideration — he may assent or withhold, nothing else. *(In practice he assents, since it is introduced with his prior recommendation.)*
2. ⭐ **Assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill is OBLIGATORY** — he can neither withhold nor return it. *(Made explicit by the **24th Amendment, 1971** — see [Ch 11](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md).)*
3. Over **state bills reserved by the Governor** under Article 201, he has an **absolute veto** and may withhold assent even after the state legislature re-passes the bill.

**The veto over state bills:**

| Situation | President's options |
|---|---|
| **A state bill reserved by the Governor** | Assent · withhold assent · **return it for reconsideration** by the state legislature |
| **If the state legislature re-passes it** | ⚠️ **The President is still NOT bound to give assent** — this is the key contrast with a Union bill |

### ④ ⭐ Ordinance-making power (Article 123)

> The President may promulgate an ordinance when **both Houses are not in session** — or when **either House is not in session** — and he is **satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary to take immediate action**.

| Feature | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Force** | ⭐ Has **the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament** |
| **Limits** | It is **subject to the same constitutional limitations** as an Act — so it **cannot abridge a Fundamental Right** *(Article 13 applies)* |
| **Scope** | Only on subjects on which **Parliament can legislate** |
| **Duration** | Must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble. It **ceases to operate six weeks from the reassembly** of Parliament — or earlier if both Houses pass resolutions disapproving it |
| **Retrospectivity** | ⭐ **May be made retrospective** — it can come into effect from a **back date** |
| **Withdrawal** | May be withdrawn by the President at any time |
| **Advice** | Promulgated **on the advice of the Council of Ministers** — not in his discretion |

> ⭐ **The maximum life of an ordinance is 6 months and 6 weeks**, since the maximum gap between two parliamentary sessions is six months.
>
> ⚠️ **This exact combination was tested in [Prelims 2025 Q1](../../03_PYQ_Prelims/2025.md):** an ordinance **can** amend a Central Act ✔, **can** operate retrospectively ✔, but **cannot** abridge a Fundamental Right ✘.

**The judicial checks:**
- ***D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1987)** — the Court struck down the practice of **re-promulgating ordinances** without placing them before the legislature, calling it a **"fraud on the Constitution"** and a **subversion of the democratic process**. Bihar had re-promulgated ordinances for years on end.
- ***Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar* (2017)** — a seven-judge bench held that **re-promulgation is unconstitutional**, and that **laying the ordinance before the legislature is mandatory**.

### ⑤ ⭐ Pardoning power (Article 72)

The President may grant **pardon, reprieve, respite or remission of punishment**, or **suspend, remit or commute** the sentence of any person convicted of any offence:

1. In all cases where the punishment is by a **court martial**
2. In all cases where the punishment is for an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the **executive power of the Union** extends
3. ⭐ In all cases of a **sentence of death**

**The five terms — a certain Prelims item:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pardon** | Removes **both the sentence and the conviction**, and frees the convict completely — as if never convicted |
| **Commutation** | **Substitutes one form of punishment for a lighter one** — e.g. death → rigorous imprisonment |
| **Remission** | **Reduces the period** of the sentence **without changing its character** — e.g. two years' rigorous imprisonment → one year's rigorous imprisonment |
| **Respite** | Awards a **lesser sentence in place of that originally awarded**, because of some **special fact** — e.g. the physical disability of a convict or the pregnancy of a woman offender |
| **Reprieve** | A **stay of the execution** of a sentence, especially of death, **for a temporary period** — to allow the convict time to seek pardon or commutation |

> ⭐ **The two exclusive powers of the President that the Governor does NOT have:**
> 1. **Court martial** cases — the Governor has no power over them
> 2. ⭐ **Death sentences** — the Governor can **suspend, remit or commute** a death sentence, but **cannot pardon** one. Only the President can.
>
> This comparison is one of the most reliable Prelims questions in the whole book, and it was the subject of [Mains 2025 Q3](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2025.md), which asked candidates to compare the Indian and US pardon powers.

**The judicial position:**
- The power is **exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers**, not at the President's personal discretion.
- ***Maru Ram* (1980)** and ***Kehar Singh* (1989)** — the President may **examine the evidence afresh** and take a view different from the court's; he is not sitting in appeal but is exercising an executive power.
- ⭐ ***Epuru Sudhakar* (2006)** — clemency is subject to **limited judicial review**: it can be challenged if the order was passed **without application of mind**, is **mala fide**, is based on **wholly irrelevant considerations**, if **relevant material was kept out**, or if it suffers from **arbitrariness**.
- ***Shatrughan Chauhan* (2014)** — **inordinate and unexplained delay** in deciding a mercy petition is itself a ground for commuting a death sentence.

> **The formulation to use in Mains** — from [Mains 2011 Q1(b)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2011.md): *"The exercise of executive clemency is not a privilege but is based on several principles."* It is a **constitutional duty exercised on advice, reviewable for process**, not an act of royal grace.

### ⑥ Emergency, military, diplomatic and judicial powers

- **Emergency powers** — Articles **352, 356, 360**. See [Chapter 16](../Part_II_System_of_Government/16_Emergency_Provisions.md).
- **Military** — he is the **supreme commander of the defence forces**; appoints the chiefs of the Army, Navy and Air Force; **can declare war or conclude peace, subject to parliamentary approval**.
- **Diplomatic** — negotiates and concludes **international treaties and agreements**, **subject to ratification by Parliament**; represents India in international forums; sends and receives diplomats.
- **Judicial** — appoints the **Chief Justice and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts**; may seek the **Supreme Court's advice on any question of law or fact** of public importance under **Article 143** — ⚠️ **the advice is not binding on him**, and **the Court may refuse to give it**.

## PART C — ⭐⭐ The discretionary powers

**This is the heart of the chapter, and it answers [Mains 2007 Q8(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2007.md) directly.**

Article 74 (as amended by the 42nd and 44th Amendments) makes the Council's advice **binding**, with the President entitled to **one reconsideration**. So where does genuine discretion survive?

### ① Situational discretion — where the advice itself is uncertain

| Situation | The discretion |
|---|---|
| **Appointment of the Prime Minister** | When **no party has a clear majority**, or when a sitting PM dies in office with no obvious successor, the President decides **whom to invite** |
| **Dismissal of the Council of Ministers** | When it **cannot prove its majority** in the Lok Sabha but refuses to resign |
| **Dissolution of the Lok Sabha** | When the Council of Ministers **has lost its majority** — the President need not accept a defeated PM's advice to dissolve |

> ⭐ **These are real, not theoretical.** The hung Parliaments of **1979, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 1999** each turned on the President's judgement. The convention that has emerged — invite the leader of the **single largest party or pre-poll alliance** first, and require a **floor test within a fixed time** — comes from practice and from ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)**, which held that majority must be tested **on the floor of the House**.

### ② The powers that survive by textual design

| Power | Why it survives |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Suspensive veto — one reconsideration** | Article 74 proviso: he may **require the Council to reconsider**; only after reconsideration is he bound |
| ⭐ **Pocket veto** | Because **no time limit is prescribed** for action on a bill. *(Zail Singh, 1986)* |
| **Absolute veto over reserved state bills** | Under Article 201 he may withhold assent **even after** the state legislature re-passes the bill |
| **Seeking information from the PM** | Article 78 obliges the PM to furnish information the President calls for |
| **Returning advice / seeking clarification** | An established convention of a "President with a nudge" |

### ⚠️ Where there is NO discretion

- **Assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill** — obligatory *(24th Amendment)*.
- **Promulgating an ordinance** — done **on advice**, not personally.
- **Pardoning** — exercised **on advice**.
- **Proclaiming an Emergency** — needs the **Cabinet's written recommendation** *(44th Amendment)*.

> **The conclusion to write:** the President's discretion is **residual, situational and political — not legal**. It expands exactly in proportion to the **fragmentation of the Lok Sabha**, and shrinks to almost nothing under a majority government. **That is the answer to any question about whether the office is "merely ceremonial."**

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The article block
> **52** the office · **53** executive power vested in him · **54–55** election and the value of votes · **56** term · **58** qualifications · **61** impeachment · **72** pardon · **74** aid and advice · **78** duties of the PM towards him · **111** assent to bills · **123** ordinance · **143** advisory jurisdiction.

### The electoral college — who is IN and OUT
> **IN:** elected MPs · elected MLAs of states · elected MLAs of **Delhi and Puducherry**.
> **OUT:** all **nominated** members · **all** Legislative Council members · MLAs of other UTs.
> ⭐ **Reverse it for impeachment:** nominated MPs **can** take part; **state MLAs cannot.**

### The vetoes
> **Absolute ✔ · Suspensive ✔ · Pocket ✔ · Qualified ✘.** No **Money Bill** may be returned. Assent to a **Constitutional Amendment Bill is obligatory**. **Zail Singh, 1986, Indian Post Office Bill** = the pocket veto.

### The ordinance
> Same force as an Act · **cannot abridge an FR** · **may be retrospective** · ceases **six weeks** after reassembly · **maximum life 6 months + 6 weeks** · *Wadhwa* (1987) "fraud on the Constitution" · *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) re-promulgation unconstitutional.

### Pardon — the five words
> **Pardon** wipes conviction and sentence · **Commutation** substitutes a lighter *kind* · **Remission** shortens the *period* · **Respite** lesser sentence for a **special fact** · **Reprieve** a **temporary stay**.
> ⭐ **President alone: court martial + death sentence.**

### The discretion, in one line
> **Whom to invite · when to send it back · how long to sit on it.** Everything else is on advice.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "What are the exceptions when the President of India is not bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2007 Q8(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2007.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Start from Article 74 as amended by the **42nd and 44th** — advice binding, subject to **one reconsideration**. Then the exceptions: **appointing a PM** in a hung House; **dismissing** a Council that has lost majority but won't resign; **dissolving** the Lok Sabha on a defeated PM's advice; the **suspensive veto**; the **pocket veto** (no time limit — Zail Singh 1986); the **absolute veto over reserved state bills**; seeking information under **Article 78**. Conclude: the discretion is **situational and political, not legal**, and varies inversely with the government's majority.

**2. "Compare and contrast the President's power to pardon in India and in the USA. Are there any limits on this power?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2025 Q3](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2025.md).*
> *Skeleton:* **Similarities** — both cover federal offences; both extend to pardon, commutation, remission, reprieve. **Differences** — the US President exercises it **personally and at his sole discretion**, and it is **not judicially reviewable in the same way**; the Indian President exercises it **on the advice of the Council of Ministers**, and it **is** subject to limited review. The US power does not extend to **impeachment**; the Indian power uniquely covers **court martial** cases and is the **only** route to pardon a death sentence. **Limits in India** — *Maru Ram*, *Kehar Singh*, ⭐ *Epuru Sudhakar* (mala fide, irrelevant considerations, non-application of mind, arbitrariness), *Shatrughan Chauhan* (inordinate delay). Conclude with the [2011 Q1(b)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2011.md) formulation: **clemency is not a privilege but a principled constitutional duty.**

**3. "Resorting to ordinances has always raised concern on violation of the spirit of separation of powers doctrine. While noting the rationales justifying the power to promulgate ordinances, analyse whether the decisions of the Supreme Court on the issue have further facilitated resorting to this power." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2015 Q5](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2015.md); see also [2001 Q8(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2001.md).*
> *Skeleton:* **Rationale** — genuine urgency when Parliament is not in session; the executive cannot be paralysed for up to six months. **The concern** — legislation by the executive; six weeks of unexamined law; **re-promulgation** turning a stopgap into permanent government by decree. **The Court's role** — ***Wadhwa* (1987)** called re-promulgation a **"fraud on the Constitution"**, and ***Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017)** held it unconstitutional and made laying mandatory — so the decisions have **restricted** rather than facilitated the power. Conclude on the residual problem: the **satisfaction of the President is subjective**, and the number of ordinances remains a function of political convenience, not urgency.

**4. "Discuss the question of death sentence and Presidential clemency." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2003 Q6(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2003.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Article 72 and the President's exclusive power over death sentences; the *rarest of rare* doctrine from *Bachan Singh* (1980); the fact that clemency is exercised **on advice**, making it a government decision in substance; the review standards from *Epuru Sudhakar*; **delay as a ground for commutation** (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014). Conclude on the tension between **executive mercy and judicial finality**, and on the case for a **time limit** on deciding mercy petitions.

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **Article 53:** executive power **vested in** the President. **Article 74:** he **acts on advice** — binding since the **42nd**, with **one reconsideration** since the **44th**.
- **Electoral college:** elected MPs + elected MLAs of states + elected MLAs of **Delhi and Puducherry** (70th Amendment). ⚠️ **Nominated members and all Legislative Council members excluded.** **Proportional representation, single transferable vote, secret ballot.** Population = **1971 Census**, frozen till the first census after **2026**.
- **Qualifications:** citizen · **35** years · qualified for the **Lok Sabha** · no office of profit *(but a sitting President, VP, Governor or Minister is exempt)*. **50 proposers + 50 seconders**, deposit **₹15,000**.
- **Term five years**, **re-eligible any number of times** *(only Rajendra Prasad twice)*; continues **until his successor takes office**; oath by the **CJI**; resignation to the **Vice-President**.
- ⭐ **Impeachment (Art 61):** ground = **"violation of the Constitution"** (undefined) · **either House** · **¼ of members** sign, **14 days' notice** · **two-thirds of TOTAL membership** in both Houses · **nominated MPs participate**, ⚠️ **state MLAs do not**. **Never used.**
- **Vetoes:** absolute ✔ suspensive ✔ **pocket ✔** qualified ✘. **Money Bill cannot be returned.** **Constitutional Amendment Bill — assent obligatory (24th Amd).** Reserved **state bills — absolute veto even after re-passage.** **Zail Singh, 1986, Indian Post Office Bill.**
- **Ordinance (Art 123):** when a House is not in session · **same force as an Act** · **cannot abridge an FR** · **can be retrospective** · lapses **six weeks** after reassembly · **max life 6 months + 6 weeks** · ***Wadhwa* 1987**, ***Krishna Kumar Singh* 2017**.
- **Pardon (Art 72):** **pardon · commutation · remission · respite · reprieve.** ⭐ **Only the President** can act in **court martial** cases and can **pardon a death sentence**. Exercised **on advice**; reviewable per ***Epuru Sudhakar* (2006)**; **delay** is a ground for commutation (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014).
- **Legislative:** summons, prorogues, dissolves; **joint sitting** presided by the **Speaker**; nominates **12 to the Rajya Sabha**; ⚠️ **Anglo-Indian nomination to the Lok Sabha abolished — 104th Amendment, 2019**.
- **Article 143:** may seek the Supreme Court's advice — **not binding**, and the **Court may decline**.
- ⭐ **Discretion = whom to invite as PM · dismissal · dissolution · suspensive veto · pocket veto · absolute veto over state bills.** It grows when the Lok Sabha is **hung** and shrinks to nothing under a majority government.
