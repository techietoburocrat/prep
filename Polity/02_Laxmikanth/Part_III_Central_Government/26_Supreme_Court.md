Read them in one arc.

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **India has a single, integrated judiciary with the Supreme Court at its apex — and its most consequential feature is not any listed power but the fact that it appoints itself.** The collegium, invented in 1993 and defended in 2015 as *basic structure*, makes the Indian Supreme Court the most institutionally autonomous apex court in the democratic world — which is simultaneously its greatest strength and the sharpest criticism of it.

---

## 📖 The story

The framers made a choice most federations do not.

The **USA** has a **double system** — federal courts applying federal law, state courts applying state law, two hierarchies running in parallel. India has **one integrated hierarchy**: the Supreme Court at the top, High Courts below, subordinate courts below them, **all of them enforcing both central and state laws**.

> 💡 **The analogy:** the American system is like two separate ladders leaning against the same wall — you must know at the outset which ladder your case belongs on. India has **one ladder**, and any case can climb it. That removes an entire category of jurisdictional dispute, and it is a deliberate unitary feature ([Ch 13](../Part_II_System_of_Government/13_Federal_System.md)).

Then comes the part the framers **did not** design.

**Article 124 says the President appoints judges "after consultation with" the Chief Justice.** For forty years that meant what it says: the executive decided, having consulted. Then in **1993** the Supreme Court held that "consultation" actually means **"concurrence"** — and that the Chief Justice's view, formed with his seniormost colleagues, would **bind** the government.

**The judiciary had taken over its own appointments, by interpretation, from a text that says something else.** When Parliament tried to reverse this in **2014** with the NJAC — passed by both Houses and ratified by **sixteen state legislatures** — the Court **struck it down**, holding that the independence of the judiciary is part of the **basic structure**.

> **You cannot answer a question on this chapter well without taking a position on that sequence.** It is either the strongest defence of judicial independence anywhere, or a court insulating itself from every form of accountability — and the honest answer is that it is **both**.

## PART A — Composition and appointment

### Composition

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | **28 January 1950**, succeeding the **Federal Court** established under the Government of India Act, 1935 *(which functioned 1937–1950)* |
| **Original strength** | ⭐ **8 — the Chief Justice + 7 judges** |
| ⭐ **Present strength** | **34 — the Chief Justice + 33 judges**, fixed by the **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019** |
| **Who fixes it** | ⭐ The Constitution **authorises Parliament** to increase the number — it is **not fixed by the Constitution** |
| **Seat** | ⭐ **Delhi**; but the **Chief Justice may appoint other places** as the seat, **with the approval of the President**. *(This has never been done — a standing demand for regional benches.)* |

### ⭐⭐ Appointment — and the collegium story

**What Article 124 actually says:** judges are appointed by the **President** — the **Chief Justice** after consultation with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President deems necessary; **other judges** after consultation with the Chief Justice and such other judges as he deems necessary.

**What it has come to mean:**

| Stage | Case | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **First Judges Case** | ***S.P. Gupta*** (**1981**) | ⭐ **"Consultation" does NOT mean "concurrence."** The **President** was not bound by the CJI's advice — **executive primacy** |
| ⭐ **Second Judges Case** | **(1993)** | ⭐ **Reversed.** "Consultation" **means "concurrence."** The **CJI's advice is binding** — but the CJI must form his opinion after consulting **two seniormost judges**. ⭐ **The collegium is born** |
| **Third Judges Case** | **(1998)** — a **Presidential Reference under Article 143** | ⭐ The collegium is the **CJI + the FOUR seniormost judges** *(a total of five)*. ⚠️ **The CJI's sole individual opinion does not constitute "consultation"** |
| ⭐ **Fourth Judges Case** | ***NJAC*, 2015** | ⭐ The **99th Amendment Act, 2014** and the **NJAC Act, 2014** were **struck down** — the **independence of the judiciary is part of the BASIC STRUCTURE**, and the collegium was **revived** |

### ⭐ The NJAC — what was struck down

The **National Judicial Appointments Commission** would have had **six members**:
1. The **Chief Justice of India** *(Chairperson)*
2. **Two seniormost Supreme Court judges**
3. The **Union Minister of Law and Justice**
4. **Two eminent persons**, nominated by a committee of the **Prime Minister, the CJI and the Leader of the Opposition** in the Lok Sabha *(one of whom must be from the SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women)*

⚠️ **The fatal clause:** the NJAC **could not recommend a person if ANY TWO members disagreed.** That gave the **two eminent persons a veto** over the three judges — and the Court held this could allow **non-judicial members to override the judiciary**, destroying its primacy.

> ⭐ **The case both ways — this is what [Mains 2017 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2017.md) asked for:**
>
> **For the judgment:** independence of the judiciary is basic structure and cannot be amended away; the executive is the **largest litigant** before the courts, so giving it a decisive role in choosing judges is a conflict of interest; the "eminent persons" veto was genuinely capable of blocking meritorious candidates for political reasons.
>
> **Against it:** the amendment was passed by **both Houses unanimously** and ratified by **16 states** — the Court struck down the most broadly supported constitutional amendment in decades; the collegium it restored has **no constitutional text behind it**, **no secretariat, no fixed criteria, no published reasons, and no appeal**; the Court itself, in the same judgment, **admitted the collegium needed reform** and called for an improved **Memorandum of Procedure** — which remains **unsettled to this day**, with recurring stand-offs over the government sitting on recommendations.

### Qualifications ⭐

A person must be a **citizen of India**, and:
1. A **judge of a High Court** *(or of two or more such courts in succession)* for **five years**; **or**
2. An **advocate of a High Court** *(or of two or more such courts in succession)* for **ten years**; **or**
3. ⭐ A **distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President**

> ⚠️ **Two traps:** ⭐ **The Constitution prescribes NO minimum age** for a Supreme Court judge, and **no fixed minimum period of experience as a judge of the Supreme Court** for becoming Chief Justice. And ⭐ **no "distinguished jurist" has ever been appointed** under the third route.

**Convention on the Chief Justice:** the **seniormost judge** of the Supreme Court is appointed. ⚠️ **The convention was violated twice** — in **1973** *(A.N. Ray superseding three seniors, immediately after **Kesavananda** — see [Ch 11](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md))* and in **1977** *(M.H. Beg superseding H.R. Khanna, whose dissent in **ADM Jabalpur** had displeased the government — see [Ch 16](../Part_II_System_of_Government/16_Emergency_Provisions.md))*.

## PART B — Tenure and removal

### Tenure

- Holds office until the age of ⭐ **65 years**. *(High Court judges retire at **62** — the difference is asked.)*
- May **resign** by writing to the **President**.
- May be **removed by the President on the recommendation of Parliament**.
- Any question as to his **age** is decided by the **President** after consultation with the **Chief Justice**, and the President's decision is **final**.

### ⭐⭐ Removal — Article 124(4) and the Judges Enquiry Act, 1968

| Step | Requirement |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Grounds** | **"Proved misbehaviour or incapacity"** — the **only** two grounds |
| **Motion signed by** | ⭐ **100 members of the Lok Sabha**, or **50 members of the Rajya Sabha** |
| **Admission** | The **Speaker/Chairman may admit or refuse to admit** the motion |
| ⭐ **Enquiry committee** | If admitted, a **three-member committee**: a **Supreme Court judge**, the **Chief Justice of a High Court**, and a **distinguished jurist** |
| **If found guilty** | The House may **take up the motion** |
| ⭐ **Majority** | A **special majority in EACH House — a majority of the total membership AND two-thirds of members present and voting — in the SAME session** |
| **Final** | The **President** issues the order of removal |

> ⭐ **No judge of the Supreme Court has ever been removed.** Two attempts:
> - **Justice V. Ramaswami (1993)** — the enquiry committee found him **guilty**, but the motion **failed in the Lok Sabha** because the **ruling party abstained** and the special majority was not reached.
> - **Justice Soumitra Sen (2011)** — the **Rajya Sabha passed** the motion, but he **resigned before the Lok Sabha** took it up.
>
> ⚠️ **The lesson to state in an answer:** the removal procedure is so demanding, and so **dependent on the political arithmetic of the day**, that it has **never once worked**. That is the strongest argument for an alternative accountability mechanism — and the strongest counter to the claim that judges are accountable through impeachment.

### Acting, ad hoc and retired judges

| Provision | Who |
|---|---|
| **Acting Chief Justice** *(Art 126)* | Appointed by the **President** when the office is vacant, or the CJI is absent or unable to perform his duties |
| ⭐ **Ad hoc judges** *(Art 127)* | When there is **no quorum** of permanent judges, the **CJI may appoint a HIGH COURT judge** as an ad hoc judge — with the **President's prior consent** and after **consulting the Chief Justice of that High Court**. The judge must be **qualified for appointment as a Supreme Court judge** |
| ⭐ **Retired judges** *(Art 128)* | The **CJI may request a RETIRED judge of the Supreme Court, or a retired judge of a High Court** *(duly qualified)*, to act as a judge — with the **President's prior consent** and **the retired judge's consent**. ⚠️ He gets the **allowances the President determines** and **all the jurisdiction, powers and privileges** of a judge, ⭐ **but is not deemed to be a judge of the Supreme Court** |

## PART C — ⭐ Independence of the judiciary

**Ten safeguards. Learn all ten — this is a standard 150-word question and a component of many 250-word ones.**

| # | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **Mode of appointment** — judges appointed by the President in consultation with judicial authorities, not by political process alone *(and since 1993, effectively by the collegium)* |
| 2 | **Security of tenure** — removable only in the manner and on the grounds in the Constitution |
| 3 | ⭐ **Fixed service conditions** — salaries, allowances, privileges, leave and pension are determined by Parliament, and ⚠️ **cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment**, **except during a Financial Emergency** ([Ch 16](../Part_II_System_of_Government/16_Emergency_Provisions.md)) |
| 4 | ⭐ **Expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India** — **non-votable** by Parliament ([Ch 22](22_Parliament.md)) |
| 5 | ⭐ **Conduct of judges cannot be discussed** in Parliament or a state legislature, **except on a motion for removal** |
| 6 | ⭐ **Ban on practice after retirement** — a retired judge **cannot plead or act before any court or authority in India** |
| 7 | **Power to punish for its contempt** — Article 129 |
| 8 | **Freedom to appoint its own staff** — Article 146 |
| 9 | ⭐ **Its jurisdiction cannot be curtailed** — ⚠️ **Parliament may EXTEND but not CURTAIL** the Supreme Court's jurisdiction and powers |
| 10 | ⭐ **Separation from the executive** — the executive cannot possess judicial powers *(Article 50, a DPSP — see [Ch 8](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/08_DPSP.md))* |

> ⚠️ **The criticism to pair with safeguard 6:** the ban on practice does **not** bar **post-retirement appointments** — to tribunals, commissions, governorships or the Rajya Sabha. Critics argue the prospect of such appointments creates a **pre-retirement incentive** that no formal safeguard addresses. This is a strong, current point for any answer on judicial independence.

## PART D — ⭐⭐ Jurisdiction and powers

**Seven heads. This is the core of the chapter.**

### ① Original jurisdiction — Article 131 *(federal disputes)*

⭐ **Exclusive** original jurisdiction over disputes:
- Between the **Centre and one or more states**
- Between the **Centre and any state(s) on one side and one or more other states on the other**
- Between **two or more states**

⚠️ **The dispute must involve a question (of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a LEGAL RIGHT depends.** A merely political dispute does not qualify.

**⭐ Five exclusions from Article 131 — asked directly:**
1. A dispute arising out of any **pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, sanad** or similar instrument
2. ⭐ A dispute arising out of any **treaty or agreement which expressly provides** that the jurisdiction shall not extend to it
3. ⭐ **Inter-state water disputes** *(Article 262 — see [Ch 15](../Part_II_System_of_Government/15_Inter_State_Relations.md))*
4. Matters referred to the **Finance Commission**
5. **Adjustment of certain expenses** between the Centre and the states
6. ⚠️ Ordinary **commercial disputes** between the Centre and states, and **recovery of damages**

### ② Writ jurisdiction — Article 32

- ⭐ The Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction under Article 32 is **original but NOT exclusive** — **High Courts also have it under Article 226**.
- ⭐⭐ **The crucial difference:** the **Supreme Court can issue writs ONLY for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights**. A **High Court** can issue them **for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights AND "for any other purpose"** — i.e. for the enforcement of an **ordinary legal right**.
- ⭐ **Therefore the High Court's writ jurisdiction is WIDER than the Supreme Court's.**

> ⭐ *Directly asked in [Mains 2006 Q8(c)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2006.md): "Is the High Courts' power to issue 'writs' wider than that of the Supreme Court of India?"* **Answer: yes**, on the "any other purpose" ground.
>
> ⚠️ **But note the counter-balance:** the Supreme Court **cannot refuse** to exercise its Article 32 jurisdiction — Article 32 is **itself a Fundamental Right**, which Ambedkar called *"the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it."* A High Court's Article 226 power is **discretionary**. So the SC's jurisdiction is **narrower in scope but stronger in obligation.** Saying both halves is what separates a good answer.

### ③ Appellate jurisdiction — four routes

| Route | Article | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Constitutional** | **132** | An appeal from a High Court **if it certifies** that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution** |
| **Civil** | **133** | If the High Court certifies that the case involves a **substantial question of law of general importance** which **needs to be decided by the Supreme Court** |
| **Criminal** | **134** | ⭐ **Without a certificate** if the High Court **(a) reversed an acquittal and sentenced the accused to death**, or **(b) withdrew a case from a subordinate court, convicted and sentenced to death**. **With a certificate** if the High Court certifies the case is **fit for appeal** |
| ⭐ **Special Leave** | **136** | ⭐ **Discretionary** — the SC may grant special leave to appeal from **any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order** of **any court or tribunal** in India. ⚠️ **Except a military tribunal / court martial** |

> ⭐ **Article 136 is the widest jurisdiction of any apex court in the world.** It is **extraordinary and discretionary**, may be exercised against **any order of any court or tribunal**, at **any stage**, and the Court has said it will be used **sparingly** — though in practice **SLPs dominate its docket**, which is central to the argument that the Court is overloaded and has drifted from constitutional adjudication to error-correction.

### ④ Advisory jurisdiction — Article 143 ⭐

The President may seek the Supreme Court's opinion in **two categories**:

| Category | Rule |
|---|---|
| **(a)** A question of **law or fact of public importance** which **has arisen or is likely to arise** | ⭐ The Court **MAY** tender its opinion — **it may also refuse** |
| **(b)** A dispute arising out of a **pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, sanad** etc. | ⭐ The Court **MUST** tender its opinion |

⭐ **In both cases the opinion is ADVISORY — NOT binding on the President.** It is also **not binding on courts** as a precedent under Article 141, though it carries **high persuasive value**.

**The references worth naming:** Delhi Laws Act (1951) · Kerala Education Bill (1958) · **Berubari Union (1960)** · Sea Customs Act (1963) · Keshav Singh (1964) · Presidential Election (1974) · Special Courts Bill (1978) · Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1992) · ⭐ **Ayodhya (1993) — the Court REFUSED to answer** · Gujarat Assembly Election (2002) · Punjab Termination of Agreements Act (2004) · 2G Spectrum (2012).

### ⑤ A court of record — Article 129

Two implications:
1. ⭐ Its **judgments, proceedings and acts are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony**, have **evidentiary value**, and **cannot be questioned** when produced before any court. They are recognised as **legal precedents and legal references**.
2. ⭐ It has the **power to punish for contempt of court** — with simple imprisonment up to **six months**, or a fine up to **₹2,000**, or both. The **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** defines **civil contempt** *(wilful disobedience of a judgment, order, writ or undertaking)* and **criminal contempt** *(scandalising the court, prejudicing judicial proceedings, or interfering with the administration of justice)*.

> ⚠️ **Note:** the Supreme Court can punish for contempt **of itself and of subordinate courts**. Fair criticism, and *innocent* publication or distribution of matter, are **not** contempt.

### ⑥ Power of judicial review

See [Ch 27](27_Judicial_Review.md) in full.

### ⑦ Other powers ⭐

- ⭐ **Disputes regarding the election of the President and Vice-President** — its **original and exclusive** jurisdiction
- Enquires into the conduct and behaviour of the **Chairman and members of the UPSC** on a reference by the President
- ⭐ **Power to review its own judgment or order** — **Article 137**. ⚠️ **The Supreme Court is NOT bound by its own previous decisions** — a point that is asked
- ⭐ **Power to withdraw cases pending before High Courts** and dispose of them itself — **Article 139A**
- ⭐ **Article 141 — the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on ALL COURTS within India.** ⚠️ *Binding on all courts, but **not on the Supreme Court itself***
- ⭐ **Article 142 — the Supreme Court may pass any decree or order necessary for doing COMPLETE JUSTICE** in any cause or matter pending before it. This is the most expansive and most controversial provision in the chapter — it has been used to dissolve marriages, transfer investigations, and settle disputes no statute covered
- **Article 144** — all authorities, civil and judicial, in the territory of India shall **act in aid of the Supreme Court**

> ⚠️ ⭐ **Article 142 is where "complete justice" meets "separation of powers."** Critics say it lets the Court legislate in the guise of doing justice; defenders say it is the safety valve that lets the Constitution reach situations its drafters did not foresee. **This is the bridge to [Ch 28](28_Judicial_Activism.md)** and to [Mains 2020 Q12](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md) on judicial legislation and separation of powers.

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The numbers
> ⭐ **34 judges** *(CJI + 33)*, since **2019**; originally **8** *(CJI + 7)*. **Retirement 65** *(High Court: **62**)*. **Removal motion: 100 LS or 50 RS.** **Enquiry committee of 3.** **Contempt: 6 months or ₹2,000.**

### The four Judges Cases
> ⭐ **1981** *(S.P. Gupta)* — consultation ≠ concurrence, **executive primacy**.
> ⭐ **1993** — consultation **=** concurrence; **collegium born** *(CJI + 2)*.
> ⭐ **1998** *(Presidential Reference)* — collegium = **CJI + 4 seniormost**.
> ⭐ **2015** *(NJAC)* — 99th Amendment **struck down**; judicial independence is **basic structure**.

### Qualifications — the three routes and the two traps
> **5 years a HC judge · 10 years a HC advocate · a distinguished jurist.** ⚠️ **No minimum age.** ⚠️ **No distinguished jurist has ever been appointed.**

### Article 32 vs Article 226
> ⭐ **SC: Fundamental Rights ONLY. HC: Fundamental Rights AND "any other purpose."** ⇒ **the HC's writ jurisdiction is WIDER.** But **Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right**, so the SC **cannot refuse**; the HC's power is **discretionary**.

### The jurisdictions
> **131** original *(federal, exclusive)* · **132/133/134** appellate *(constitutional / civil / criminal)* · ⭐ **136** special leave *(widest, discretionary, not against court martial)* · **143** advisory *(**MAY** for (a), **MUST** for (b); ⭐ **never binding**)*.

### The article block at the end
> ⭐ **129** court of record · **137** review its own judgment *(and is **not bound** by its own decisions)* · **139A** withdraw cases from HCs · **141** law declared binding on **all courts** · ⭐ **142 complete justice** · **144** all authorities act in aid.

### The two supersessions
> **1973 — A.N. Ray**, after *Kesavananda*. **1977 — M.H. Beg**, over **H.R. Khanna** after his *ADM Jabalpur* dissent.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Discuss the evolution of the collegium system in India. Critically examine the advantages and disadvantages of the system." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2025 Q13](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2025.md); the NJAC angle in [2017 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2017.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) **Evolution** — the text of Article 124; **1981** executive primacy; **1993** consultation = concurrence, collegium of CJI + 2; **1998** expanded to CJI + 4; **2015** NJAC struck down, independence held to be basic structure. (ii) **Advantages** — insulates appointments from the **largest litigant**; preserves independence as basic structure; judicial peers are best placed to assess judicial merit. (iii) **Disadvantages** — ⭐ **no constitutional text**; **no secretariat, criteria, published reasons or appeal**; opacity and allegations of nepotism *("uncle judges")*; **poor social diversity** *(see [Mains 2021 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2021.md) on women in the higher judiciary)*; government delays in acting on recommendations, with no remedy; the **Memorandum of Procedure still unsettled a decade on**. (iv) **Reform** — a permanent secretariat, published criteria and reasons, fixed timelines binding on both sides, and a broader consultation base — **without** returning a veto to the executive.

**2. "What is the position of the Supreme Court under the Constitution of India?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2002 Q7(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2002.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Apex of an **integrated** judiciary *(contrast the US double system)*; **federal court** under Article 131; ⭐ **guarantor of Fundamental Rights** under Article 32, itself a Fundamental Right; **court of record**; **final interpreter** of the Constitution with **Article 141** binding all courts; **judicial review** and the **basic structure** limit on Parliament; **advisory** role under 143; and **Article 142's** complete-justice power. Conclude with the tension: an institution that is simultaneously **the last court of appeal and the constitutional check on the other two organs**.

**3. "Critically examine the Supreme Court's judgement on the 'National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014' with reference to appointment of judges of higher judiciary in India." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2017 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2017.md).*
> *Skeleton:* NJAC's **six-member composition** and the ⭐ **two-member veto** that proved fatal; the Court's reasoning *(independence = basic structure; executive is the largest litigant)*; the counter-case *(unanimous passage, **16 state ratifications**, the collegium's own opacity, the Court judging a case about its own power)*; and the unfinished business — the **Memorandum of Procedure**. Take a position: the **judgment protected independence but left accountability unaddressed**, and reform must now come from **within** the collegium.

**4. "The judicial system in India and UK seem to be converging as well as diverging in recent times. Highlight the key points of convergence and divergence between the two nations in terms of their judicial practices." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2020 Q4](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md).*
> *Skeleton:* **Convergence** — the UK created a **Supreme Court in 2009**, separating it from the House of Lords; both have moved toward **rights adjudication** *(the UK's Human Rights Act, 1998)*; both use judicial review of administrative action; both have a **Judicial Appointments Commission**-type debate. **Divergence** — ⭐ **the UK has no power to strike down primary legislation** *(only a declaration of incompatibility)*, India does; **parliamentary sovereignty vs constitutional supremacy** ([Ch 22](22_Parliament.md)); the UK appoints through an **independent JAC with lay members**, India through the **collegium**; **PIL and Article 142** have no British equivalent. Conclude on the **basic structure doctrine** as the deepest divergence.

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **Articles 124–147.** Established **28 January 1950**, succeeding the **Federal Court (1937)**. ⭐ **34 judges (CJI + 33)** since **2019**; originally **8**. **Parliament** fixes the number. Seat: **Delhi**, but the **CJI with the President's approval** may fix another.
- ⭐ **The four Judges Cases: 1981** executive primacy · **1993** consultation = concurrence, **collegium born** · **1998** collegium = **CJI + 4 seniormost** · **2015 NJAC struck down**, independence = **basic structure**. NJAC's fatal flaw: **any two members could veto**.
- **Qualifications:** **5 yrs HC judge** / **10 yrs HC advocate** / **distinguished jurist**. ⚠️ **No minimum age**; ⚠️ **no distinguished jurist ever appointed**. **Seniormost becomes CJI** — violated in **1973 (A.N. Ray)** and **1977 (M.H. Beg over H.R. Khanna)**.
- **Retirement 65** *(HC 62)*. ⭐ **Removal: "proved misbehaviour or incapacity"; 100 LS / 50 RS signatures; 3-member enquiry committee; special majority in BOTH Houses in the SAME session.** ⚠️ **Never used** — *Ramaswami (1993)* failed in the LS; *Soumitra Sen (2011)* resigned first.
- **Ad hoc judges (127)** — a **HC judge**, by the CJI with the President's consent. **Retired judges (128)** — with their own consent; ⭐ **not deemed to be a judge of the SC**.
- ⭐ **Ten independence safeguards:** appointment · tenure · **service conditions not variable to disadvantage** *(except Financial Emergency)* · **expenses charged on the CFI** · **conduct not discussable except on a removal motion** · ⭐ **ban on practice after retirement** · contempt power · own staff · ⭐ **jurisdiction may be extended but not curtailed** · separation from the executive.
- **Jurisdiction:** ⭐ **131** original and **exclusive** *(federal disputes; ⚠️ excludes pre-Constitution treaties, **inter-state water disputes**, Finance Commission references, expense adjustments, ordinary commercial disputes)* · **32** writs — ⭐ **FRs only**, so ⭐ **the HC's 226 power is wider**, but **Article 32 is itself an FR** so the SC **cannot refuse** · **132/133/134** appellate · ⭐ **136 SLP — widest in the world, discretionary, not against court martial** · **143** advisory — ⭐ **MAY** for (a), **MUST** for (b), **never binding**; ⭐ **refused to answer in Ayodhya (1993)**.
- **Court of record (129):** judgments **cannot be questioned**; **contempt — 6 months or ₹2,000**; **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** *(civil and criminal)*.
- **Other powers:** election disputes of the **President and Vice-President** *(exclusive)* · UPSC conduct enquiry · ⭐ **137 review its own judgment** *(and is **not bound** by its own decisions)* · **139A withdraw cases** · ⭐ **141 binding on all courts** · ⭐ **142 complete justice** · **144 all authorities act in aid**.
