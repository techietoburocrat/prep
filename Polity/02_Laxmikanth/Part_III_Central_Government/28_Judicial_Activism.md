# Chapter 28 — Judicial Activism

[← Chapter 27](27_Judicial_Review.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 29 →](29_Public_Interest_Litigation.md)

**Read time ~8 min** · 🟠 Prelims: occasional · Mains: **2005, 2008, 2014, 2020** — and the theme runs through many more

> 📌 **[Ch 27](27_Judicial_Review.md) was the *power*. This chapter is the *attitude* with which that power is used.** The distinction between the two is itself an exam question ([Mains 2005 Q6](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2005.md)), so get it right at the outset.

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **Judicial activism is the judiciary playing an assertive role in forcing the other two organs to discharge their constitutional obligations.** It is not a power — it is a **posture** adopted in exercising the powers of review and Article 142. Its legitimacy rests on a single premise: that **the executive and legislature have failed**, and someone must act.

---

## 📖 The story

The classical theory of the judge is passive. He waits for a case, applies the law as it is, and grants the remedy the law provides. He does not go looking for problems, and he does not tell the government what to do.

**That model assumes the other two organs are working.**

In India, from the 1970s, judges increasingly concluded that they were not. Undertrials were rotting in jail for longer than the maximum sentence for their alleged offence. Bonded labour persisted three decades after it was outlawed. Environmental statutes existed and were not enforced. There was **no law at all** against sexual harassment at the workplace.

Faced with that, the Court had two options: **apply the law as it stood and dismiss the case, or fill the gap.**

> 💡 **The analogy:** a referee normally enforces the rules and nothing more. But suppose the groundskeeper has stopped maintaining the pitch and it is now dangerous. Strictly, that is not the referee's job. **Judicial activism is the referee stopping play and ordering the ground fixed** — on the reasoning that the game cannot be played at all otherwise.
>
> **And the objection writes itself:** the referee has no expertise in groundskeeping, no budget, and nobody elected him to run the stadium. **Both propositions are true at once**, which is why this chapter has no tidy answer, and why examiners like it.

**The origin of the term:** coined by the American historian **Arthur Schlesinger Jr. in 1947**. **In India its architects were Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer, Justice P.N. Bhagwati, Justice O. Chinnappa Reddy and Justice D.A. Desai**, from the mid-1970s — and its vehicle was **Public Interest Litigation** ([Ch 29](29_Public_Interest_Litigation.md)).

## PART A — Meaning and distinctions ⭐

### The definition

> **Judicial activism denotes the proactive role played by the judiciary in the protection of the rights of citizens and in the promotion of justice in society** — forcing the other two organs to **discharge their constitutional obligations**.

It is also called **"judicial dynamism"**. Its opposite is **judicial restraint** — the view that judges should decide narrowly, defer to the elected branches, and avoid making policy.

### ⭐⭐ The three-way distinction — the highest-value content here

| | **Judicial Review** | **Judicial Activism** | **Judicial Overreach** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | ⭐ A **constitutional POWER** | ⭐ A **judicial ATTITUDE** in exercising that power | ⭐ **Activism taken beyond legitimate limits** |
| **Function** | Tests the **validity** of a law or executive action against the Constitution | **Expands rights, fills gaps, issues directions** to the other organs | **Substitutes** the Court for the legislature or executive |
| **Nature** | **Reactive** — needs a case | **Proactive** — often *suo motu*, and remedial | **Usurpation** |
| **Legitimacy** | ⭐ **Explicitly conferred** *(Art 13, 32, 226 …)* and **basic structure** | ⭐ **Contested but defensible** where the other organs have failed | ⭐ **Generally regarded as illegitimate** |
| **Example** | *Minerva Mills*; NJAC | ***Vishaka* guidelines**; continuing mandamus in environmental cases | Directions on matters of pure policy or administration |

> ⭐ **The one-line answer to "distinguish review from activism":** **review is a power; activism is how energetically it is used.** They are not opposites — activism is normally **exercised through** review and through **Article 142**.

## PART B — Why it arose ⭐

**Seven causes. A good answer names four or five with an example each.**

1. ⭐ **Near collapse of responsible government** — failure of the legislative and executive wings to discharge their constitutional obligations
2. ⭐ **A vacuum in leadership**, and the resulting expectation that the judiciary would step in
3. **Legislature failing in its duties** — laws not made, or made and not enforced
4. **The people losing faith** in the executive and legislature, and turning to the courts
5. ⭐ **The judiciary's own *suo motu* power**, and the relaxation of ***locus standi*** through **PIL** ([Ch 29](29_Public_Interest_Litigation.md))
6. ⭐ **Legislative vacuum** — the absence of any law on a subject requiring regulation
7. **The Constitution's own text** — the expansive reading of **Article 21**, and **Article 142's "complete justice"** power

### ⭐ The landmark illustrations

| Case / area | What the Court did |
|---|---|
| ⭐ ***Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997)** | ⭐ **Framed binding guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace** where **no law existed** — and they held the field for **16 years**, until the **Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013**. **The single best example of activism filling a legislative vacuum** |
| **Environmental cases** | **Continuing mandamus** — the Court retains the case and supervises implementation over years: the **Ganga pollution** cases, **CNG for Delhi public transport**, forest and mining matters |
| ⭐ ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** | Right to a **speedy trial** read into Article 21; **over 40,000 undertrials released** |
| ***Lily Thomas* (2013)** | A convicted legislator is **disqualified immediately** on conviction — struck down the protection that allowed continuation pending appeal |
| **2G spectrum (2012), coal block allocation (2014)** | **Cancelled licences and allocations** made by the executive |
| ***K.S. Puttaswamy* (2017)** | **Right to privacy** read into Article 21 by a nine-judge bench |
| ***Common Cause* (2018)** | **Passive euthanasia and living wills** permitted |
| **NOTA (2013)** | Directed the Election Commission to provide a **"None of the Above"** option |

### ⚠️ And the counter-examples — where it drew criticism

- The **National Anthem in cinemas** order (2016), **substantially modified in 2018** after criticism that the Court was legislating on patriotism.
- Detailed directions on matters like **highway liquor bans** and administrative arrangements for religious institutions, where the Court effectively took over **executive** functions.
- **Judicial appointments** — the collegium ([Ch 26](26_Supreme_Court.md)) is the sharpest instance of the Court expanding its own power by interpretation.

## PART C — The case for and against ⭐

### For

| Argument | Substance |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Constitutional duty** | Articles **32 and 226** make the Court the **guarantor** of Fundamental Rights; a right without a remedy is not a right |
| ⭐ **Filling genuine vacuums** | *Vishaka* is unanswerable — the alternative was **no protection at all** for 16 years |
| **Access to justice** | PIL brought the courts within reach of people who could never have approached them |
| **Enforcing existing law** | Much activism is not new law but **making the executive obey laws it already ignored** |
| **Article 142** | The Constitution **itself** confers a power to do "complete justice" — activism is partly a textual mandate |

### Against

| Argument | Substance |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Separation of powers** | The Court legislates and administers, violating a scheme the Constitution assumes *(see [Mains 2020 Q12](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md) and [2019 Q1](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2019.md))* |
| ⭐ **No democratic mandate** | Judges are **unelected and unaccountable**; policy choices involve **trade-offs** properly made by those who face voters |
| ⭐ **No institutional competence** | Courts lack the **expertise, data and budget** to design policy; they hear two parties, not all stakeholders |
| ⚠️ **Implementation** | The Court has **no machinery** to enforce its own directions — orders on pollution and prison reform have gone unimplemented for decades |
| **Selectivity and unpredictability** | Which causes get taken up depends on which bench and which petitioner |
| ⚠️ **Judicial populism** | Activism can shade into **"judicial adventurism"** — headline-driven intervention without a remedy behind it |
| ⭐ **It masks the real failure** | Every judicial rescue **reduces the pressure** on the legislature and executive to do their own jobs |

> ⭐ **The formulation that works in a Mains conclusion:** *"Judicial activism is a **symptom, not a disease**. The remedy is not a restrained judiciary but a functioning legislature and executive — because a court that stops intervening in a vacuum does not fill the vacuum; it merely leaves it."* Then add the boundary: **activism is legitimate when it enforces rights and existing law, and becomes overreach when it makes policy choices involving resource trade-offs.**

---

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The three-way distinction
> ⭐ **Review = POWER · Activism = ATTITUDE · Overreach = ATTITUDE gone too far.**

### Origin
> Term coined by ⭐ **Arthur Schlesinger Jr., 1947 (USA)**. India's architects: ⭐ **Krishna Iyer · Bhagwati · Chinnappa Reddy · D.A. Desai**, from the **mid-1970s**, through **PIL**.

### The one case that answers everything
> ⭐ ***Vishaka* (1997)** — binding guidelines where **no law existed**, in force for **16 years** until the **2013 Act**. **Use it for the defence; use the National Anthem order (2016, modified 2018) for the criticism.**

### The mechanism
> ⭐ **Continuing mandamus** — the Court keeps the case open and **supervises implementation** over years. This is the procedural signature of activism.

### The conclusion to write
> ⭐ **"A symptom, not a disease."** Legitimate when **enforcing rights and existing law**; overreach when **making policy with resource trade-offs**.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Judicial legislation is antithetical to the doctrine of separation of powers as envisaged in the Indian Constitution. In this context justify the filing of large number of public interest petitions praying for issuing guidelines to executive authorities." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2020 Q12](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) **Concede the premise partly** — India follows a **functional, not strict, separation** *(see [Mains 2019 Q1](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2019.md))*; judicial legislation does strain it. (ii) **But justify the petitions** — Articles **32 and 226** make the Court the guarantor of rights; **legislative vacuums** exist *(Vishaka)*; laws exist but go **unenforced**; PIL is often the **only route** for those without means. (iii) **Draw the line** — guidelines are defensible where they are **interim, gap-filling and expressly subject to legislation** *(as Vishaka was)*; they become overreach when **permanent, policy-laden or budget-allocating**. (iv) Conclude: the answer is **not fewer petitions but faster legislatures**, plus judicial self-discipline — **sunset clauses on guidelines** and deference on polycentric issues.

**2. "What is meant by 'Judicial Activism'? Evaluate its role in the context of the functioning of Indian polity." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2008 Q5(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2008.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Definition and the review/activism/overreach distinction; the seven causes; illustrations in three buckets — **rights expansion** *(Article 21: speedy trial, privacy, euthanasia)*, **enforcement of existing law** *(environment, prisons)*, **gap-filling** *(Vishaka, NOTA)*. Then the critique: mandate, competence, implementation, and the moral hazard of relieving pressure on the other organs. Conclude on the **boundary test**.

**3. "Is it possible to distinguish between judicial review and judicial activism in India?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2005 Q6](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2005.md) — see [Ch 27](27_Judicial_Review.md) for the fuller skeleton.*

**4. "Starting from inventing the 'basic structure' doctrine, the judiciary has played a highly proactive role in ensuring that India develops into a thriving democracy. In light of the statement, evaluate the role played by judicial activism in achieving the ideals of democracy." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2014 Q1](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2014.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Trace from *Kesavananda* → *Minerva Mills* → NJAC as **structural protection of democracy**; then the **rights** contribution *(Article 21's expansion)*; then **access** *(PIL)*; then **accountability** *(2G, coal, Lily Thomas)*. Balance with the democratic-deficit critique and the implementation gap. Conclude: the judiciary has **protected the conditions of democracy** while **occasionally displacing its processes** — and the second is the price of the first.

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- ⭐ **Judicial activism = the proactive role of the judiciary in protecting rights and forcing the other organs to discharge their constitutional obligations.** Also called **judicial dynamism**; opposite of **judicial restraint**.
- ⭐ **Review = power · Activism = attitude · Overreach = attitude beyond legitimate limits.**
- **Term coined by Arthur Schlesinger Jr. (1947, USA).** India: **Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Chinnappa Reddy, D.A. Desai**, from the mid-1970s, via **PIL**.
- **Causes:** collapse of responsible government · leadership vacuum · legislature failing · loss of public faith · ⭐ ***suo motu* power and relaxed *locus standi*** · **legislative vacuum** · Article 21's expansion and **Article 142**.
- **Illustrations:** ⭐ ***Vishaka* (1997)** — guidelines where **no law existed**, until the **2013 Act** · **continuing mandamus** in environmental cases *(Ganga, Delhi CNG)* · ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** speedy trial, **40,000+ undertrials released** · **2G (2012)** and **coal blocks (2014)** cancelled · ***Lily Thomas* (2013)** · ***Puttaswamy* (2017)** privacy · ***Common Cause* (2018)** passive euthanasia · **NOTA (2013)**.
- **For:** constitutional duty under 32 and 226 · genuine vacuums · access to justice · enforcing laws already on the books · **Article 142**.
- **Against:** ⭐ **separation of powers** · **no democratic mandate** · **no institutional competence or data** · ⚠️ **no enforcement machinery** · selectivity · **judicial adventurism** · ⭐ **it relieves pressure on the organs that actually failed**.
- ⭐ **The conclusion:** *activism is a **symptom, not a disease***. Legitimate when it **enforces rights and existing law**; **overreach** when it **makes policy involving resource trade-offs**.
