# Chapter 34 — High Court

[← Chapter 33](33_State_Legislature.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 35 →](35_Subordinate_Courts.md)

**Read time ~11 min** · **Articles 214–231** · 🔴 Prelims: **2013, 2019, 2021** · Mains: **2006, 2021, 2025**

> 📌 **Read beside [Ch 26 Supreme Court](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md).** The institutional machinery is near-identical; ⭐ **the one substantive difference — the High Court's WIDER writ jurisdiction under Article 226 — is worth more marks than everything else in the chapter combined.**

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **The High Court is subordinate to the Supreme Court in the hierarchy but broader than it in one crucial respect:** the Supreme Court can issue writs **only to enforce Fundamental Rights**; a High Court can issue them **for Fundamental Rights *and for any other purpose*** — i.e. for an ordinary legal right. ⭐ **The High Court's writ jurisdiction is therefore wider than the Supreme Court's**, and that single sentence has been asked directly in Mains.

---

## 📖 The story

India's judiciary is a **single integrated pyramid** ([Ch 26](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md)) — the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts below, subordinate courts below them, **all applying both central and state law**.

But the High Courts are **older than the Supreme Court and older than the Constitution**. The first three — **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras** — were established in **1862**, under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861. They have been running for over a century and a half, and much of their jurisdiction was **inherited, not created**.

> 💡 **The analogy:** the Supreme Court is a **specialist appellate and constitutional court** that people reach at the end of a long road. The High Court is the **general hospital of the legal system** — it takes everything: constitutional challenges, ordinary civil appeals, criminal appeals, service disputes, and writ petitions against every arm of the state within its territory.
>
> **And that breadth is precisely why Article 226 is wider.** A court that handles only constitutional questions needs only constitutional remedies; a court that is the state's principal supervisory court needs the power to correct **any** illegality, not just a rights violation.

## PART A — Composition and appointment

### The basics

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Article 214** | **There shall be a High Court for each State** |
| ⭐ **Article 231** | ⭐ Parliament may establish a **common High Court for two or more states**, or for **states and Union Territories** |
| ⭐ **The oldest three** | **Calcutta, Bombay and Madras — established 1862** |
| **Composition** | ⭐ A **Chief Justice and such other judges as the PRESIDENT may from time to time deem necessary** — ⚠️ **the Constitution does NOT fix the strength**, unlike the Supreme Court where Parliament fixes it |

### ⭐ Appointment — the differences from the Supreme Court

| | **Supreme Court judge** *(Ch 26)* | **High Court judge** |
|---|---|---|
| **Appointed by** | The **President** | The **President** *(same)* |
| ⭐ **Consultation** | CJI and such other judges as he deems necessary | ⭐ The **CJI**, the **GOVERNOR of the state**, and — for a judge other than the Chief Justice — the ⭐ **CHIEF JUSTICE OF THAT HIGH COURT** |
| ⭐ **Collegium** | **CJI + 4 seniormost** SC judges | ⭐ **CJI + 2 seniormost** SC judges *(plus consultation with the HC collegium)* |

> ⚠️ ⭐ **The trap that recurs:** the **Governor is only CONSULTED** — ⭐ **he does NOT appoint High Court judges.** The **President** appoints them. *(Tested in [Prelims 2013 Q9](../../03_PYQ_Prelims/2013.md).)*

### Qualifications ⭐

A person must be a **citizen of India**, and:
1. Have held a **judicial office** in the territory of India for **ten years**; **or**
2. Have been an **advocate of a High Court** *(or of two or more such courts in succession)* for **ten years**

> ⚠️ ⭐ **Two contrasts with the Supreme Court:**
> 1. ⭐ **There is NO "distinguished jurist" route for a High Court judge.** That third qualification exists **only for the Supreme Court**.
> 2. **No minimum age is prescribed** for either.

### ⭐ Tenure and removal

| | **Supreme Court** | **High Court** |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Retirement age** | **65** | ⭐ **62** *(raised from 60 by the **15th Amendment Act, 1963**)* |
| **Resignation to** | The **President** | The **President** |
| ⭐ **Removal** | Same procedure | ⭐ **Identical** — by the **President on an address by both Houses**, on the ground of **"proved misbehaviour or incapacity"**, by a **special majority in each House in the same session**, under the **Judges Enquiry Act, 1968** |
| **Transfer** | ⚠️ Not applicable | ⭐ **The President may TRANSFER a judge from one High Court to another**, after consulting the **CJI** |
| **Question of age** | Decided by the President after consulting the CJI | ⭐ Decided by the **President after consulting the CJI**, and his decision is **final** |

> ⭐ **No High Court judge has ever been removed**, though several have resigned during proceedings — the same practical result as at the Supreme Court ([Ch 26](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md)).

### ⭐ Additional, acting and retired judges

| Type | Article | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Additional judge** | **224(1)** | Appointed by the **President** for a period **not exceeding two years**, when there is a **temporary increase in business** or **arrears of work** |
| **Acting judge** | **224(2)** | Appointed when a permanent judge is **temporarily absent** or is **acting as Chief Justice** |
| ⭐ **Retired judge** | **224A** | ⭐ The **Chief Justice of a High Court, with the President's previous consent**, may request a **retired judge** of that or any other High Court to sit and act as a judge. ⚠️ He gets such allowances as the **President determines**, and **all the jurisdiction, powers and privileges** of a judge — ⭐ **but is not deemed to be a judge of that High Court** |

> ⚠️ **Additional and acting judges hold office only up to the age of 62**, as permanent judges do.

### ⭐ Independence — the same ten safeguards, with two differences

The ten safeguards from [Ch 26](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md) apply, with these variations:

1. ⭐ **Expenses are charged on the CONSOLIDATED FUND OF THE STATE** — ⚠️ **not the Consolidated Fund of India**. *(But the **pensions** of High Court judges are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of INDIA** — see [Ch 22](../Part_III_Central_Government/22_Parliament.md).)*
2. ⭐ **The ban on practice after retirement is PARTIAL** — a retired High Court judge **may plead and act before the Supreme Court and other High Courts**, ⚠️ **but not before the court in which he served, or any court subordinate to it**. *(A retired **Supreme Court** judge is barred from pleading before **any** court in India.)*
3. Conduct **cannot be discussed** in Parliament or a state legislature except on a **motion for removal**.
4. **Salaries and allowances cannot be varied to their disadvantage** after appointment, **except during a Financial Emergency**.

> ⭐ **Those first two differences are both examinable.** Charged on the **state** fund; **partial** practice ban.

## PART B — ⭐⭐ Jurisdiction and powers

### ⭐⭐ ① Writ jurisdiction — Article 226, the heart of the chapter

> A High Court may issue **directions, orders or writs** — including **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari** — ⭐ **"for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III AND FOR ANY OTHER PURPOSE."**

| | **Supreme Court — Article 32** | **High Court — Article 226** |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **For what** | ⭐ **Fundamental Rights ONLY** | ⭐ **Fundamental Rights AND "any other purpose"** — i.e. an **ordinary legal right** |
| ⭐ **Scope** | **Narrower** | ⭐ **WIDER** |
| ⭐ **Discretion** | ⚠️ **Cannot refuse** — Article 32 is **itself a Fundamental Right** *(Ambedkar: "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it")* | ⭐ **Discretionary** — the High Court **may decline** relief |
| **Territorial reach** | The **whole of India** | ⭐ **Its own territorial jurisdiction — or outside it, if the CAUSE OF ACTION arises within its territory** *(added by the **15th Amendment Act, 1963**)* |

> ⭐⭐ **[Mains 2006 Q8(c)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2006.md) asked this directly:** *"Is the High Courts' power to issue 'writs' wider than that of the Supreme Court of India?"*
>
> ⭐ **The complete answer is BOTH halves:** **wider in scope** *(any other purpose)*, but **weaker in obligation** *(discretionary, whereas Article 32 cannot be refused)*. An answer giving only the first half is incomplete.

### ② Original jurisdiction

- ⭐ **Disputes relating to the election of members of Parliament and of the state legislature.**
- **Revenue matters** and acts done in the collection of revenue.
- **Enforcement of Fundamental Rights** *(Article 226)*.
- ⭐ **Cases transferred from a subordinate court** involving the interpretation of the Constitution.
- ⚠️ ⭐ **The four "presidency" High Courts — Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi — additionally have ORIGINAL CIVIL jurisdiction** in cases of higher value. *(A distinctive, examinable feature.)*

### ③ Appellate jurisdiction

- **Civil** — appeals from district and subordinate courts; **letters patent appeals** from a single judge to a division bench where permitted.
- **Criminal** — appeals from sessions and additional sessions courts *(where the sentence exceeds a specified period)*, and ⭐ **a death sentence awarded by a sessions court must be CONFIRMED by the High Court before it can be executed**, whether or not the convict appeals.

### ④ ⭐ Supervisory jurisdiction — Article 227

> ⭐ **A High Court has superintendence over ALL courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction** *(except military courts or tribunals)*.

It may **call for returns**, **make and issue general rules and prescribe forms** regulating their practice and proceedings, and **settle tables of fees**.

> ⭐ **This power is very wide** — it extends to **administrative as well as judicial** superintendence, is **revisional** in nature, and can be exercised **suo motu**. ⚠️ **But the courts have held it must be used sparingly**, only to keep subordinate courts within their authority — not to correct every error.

### ⑤ Control over subordinate courts — Article 235

⭐ A High Court has **control over district courts and courts subordinate to them**, including **posting, promotion and grant of leave** to persons belonging to the state judicial service.
- It is **consulted** by the Governor on the appointment, posting and promotion of **district judges** *(Article 233)*.
- It is **consulted** by the Governor, along with the **State Public Service Commission**, on appointments to the **judicial service other than district judges** *(Article 234)*.
- ⭐ It may **withdraw a case** pending in a subordinate court involving a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**, and either **dispose of the case itself** or determine the question and return it *(Article 228)*.

### ⑥ A court of record — Article 215

Like the Supreme Court, a High Court is a **court of record**: its judgments have **evidentiary value and cannot be questioned**, and it has the ⭐ **power to punish for contempt of itself** — ⚠️ **and, unlike the Supreme Court's position, its contempt power extends to contempt of courts subordinate to it as well.**

### ⑦ Power of judicial review

⭐ A High Court can declare a law **unconstitutional** on the same three grounds as the Supreme Court ([Ch 27](../Part_III_Central_Government/27_Judicial_Review.md)) — infringement of Fundamental Rights, want of legislative competence, or repugnancy to the Constitution.

> ⚠️ **The 42nd Amendment (1976) curtailed** the High Courts' power of judicial review; the ⭐ **43rd Amendment (1977) restored** it.

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The article block
> **214** a HC for each state · **215** court of record · ⭐ **226** writs *(**wider**)* · ⭐ **227** superintendence · **228** transfer of constitutional cases · **231** common HC for two or more states · **233/234** district judges and judicial service · **235** control over subordinate courts · **224** additional and acting judges · ⭐ **224A** retired judges.

### The five differences from the Supreme Court
> ⭐ **Retirement 62** *(not 65)* · ⭐ **no "distinguished jurist" route** · ⭐ **judges are TRANSFERABLE** · ⭐ **expenses charged on the STATE fund** · ⭐ **PARTIAL practice ban after retirement** *(may appear before the SC and other HCs, not his own or its subordinates)*.

### ⭐ The writ comparison — say both halves
> **Article 226 is WIDER in scope** *(FRs + any other purpose)* **but DISCRETIONARY**; **Article 32 is NARROWER but CANNOT BE REFUSED**, being itself a Fundamental Right.

### The appointment trap
> ⚠️ ⭐ **The PRESIDENT appoints High Court judges; the GOVERNOR is only consulted.** Collegium for a HC judge = ⭐ **CJI + 2 seniormost** *(SC judge = CJI + 4)*.

### The oldest and the special
> ⭐ **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras — 1862.** ⭐ **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi have ORIGINAL CIVIL jurisdiction.**

### The confirmation rule
> ⭐ **A death sentence passed by a sessions court must be CONFIRMED by the High Court** before execution — even if the convict does not appeal.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Is the High Courts' power to issue 'writs' wider than that of the Supreme Court of India?" (150 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2006 Q8(c)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2006.md).*
> *Skeleton:* ⭐ **Yes in scope** — Article 226 covers Fundamental Rights **and "any other purpose"**, so a High Court can enforce an **ordinary legal right**; Article 32 is confined to Fundamental Rights. Add the **territorial extension** from the **15th Amendment (1963)** — a High Court may act where the **cause of action** arises within its territory even if the authority sits outside it. ⭐ **But qualify:** Article 32 is **itself a Fundamental Right**, so the Supreme Court **cannot refuse** to entertain a petition, whereas Article 226 is **discretionary**. Conclude: **wider in reach, weaker in obligation** — and note ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997)**, which made the review power under **both** Articles part of the **basic structure**.

**2. "Discuss the desirability of greater representation to women in the higher judiciary to ensure diversity, equity and inclusiveness." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2021 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2021.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) **The position** — women remain a small minority of High Court and Supreme Court judges, and **no woman has been Chief Justice of India**. (ii) **Why it matters** — representativeness legitimises adjudication; **lived experience** affects reasoning in cases on sexual violence, maintenance, workplace harassment and reproductive rights; it signals access to the profession. (iii) **The causes** — the **collegium's opacity** and its narrow consultation base ([Ch 26](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md)); the seniority pipeline from the bar; the absence of reservation or targets in the higher judiciary; conditions at the district level from which elevation occurs. (iv) **The remedies** — published **criteria and reasons** for recommendations, a **permanent secretariat** for the collegium, diversity as a stated consideration, and strengthening the pipeline via the **All-India Judicial Service** *(recommended by Sarkaria — see [Ch 14](../Part_II_System_of_Government/14_Centre_State_Relations.md))*. (v) Conclude: diversity is **not in tension with merit** — a selection process that overlooks a majority of the population is not selecting on merit alone.

**3. "Discuss the evolution of the collegium system in India." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2025 Q13](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2025.md) — the fuller skeleton is in [Ch 26](../Part_III_Central_Government/26_Supreme_Court.md).* ⭐ **For High Court appointments, remember the collegium is CJI + 2**, and the **High Court collegium** initiates.

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **Articles 214–231.** ⭐ **A High Court for each state; Article 231 allows a COMMON High Court** for two or more states/UTs. ⭐ **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras — 1862.** ⚠️ **The Constitution does not fix the strength** — the **President** determines it.
- **Appointment:** by the **PRESIDENT**, after consulting the **CJI**, the ⭐ **GOVERNOR**, and *(for other judges)* the **Chief Justice of that High Court**. ⚠️ ⭐ **The Governor does NOT appoint.** Collegium = ⭐ **CJI + 2 seniormost**.
- **Qualifications:** citizen + **10 years' judicial office** OR **10 years as a High Court advocate**. ⚠️ ⭐ **No "distinguished jurist" route** *(that is Supreme Court only)*. **No minimum age.**
- ⭐ **Retirement 62** *(raised from 60 by the **15th Amendment, 1963**)*. **Removal identical to a Supreme Court judge** — "proved misbehaviour or incapacity", special majority in both Houses, **Judges Enquiry Act, 1968**. ⭐ **Judges are TRANSFERABLE** by the President after consulting the CJI. ⭐ **None has ever been removed.**
- **Additional/acting judges (224)** — up to **two years**, and only to age 62. ⭐ **Retired judges (224A)** — by the **HC Chief Justice with the President's previous consent**; **not deemed a judge** of that court.
- **Independence:** ⭐ **expenses charged on the CONSOLIDATED FUND OF THE STATE** *(but **pensions** on the CFI)*; ⭐ **PARTIAL practice ban** — may appear before the **Supreme Court and other High Courts**, ⚠️ **not his own court or its subordinates**.
- ⭐⭐ **Article 226:** writs for **Fundamental Rights AND "any other purpose"** ⇒ ⭐ **wider than Article 32** — but ⭐ **discretionary**, whereas **Article 32 cannot be refused**. **15th Amendment (1963)** added the **cause-of-action** territorial extension.
- **Original jurisdiction:** election disputes of **MPs and MLAs** · revenue matters · FR enforcement · transferred constitutional cases · ⭐ **original CIVIL jurisdiction in Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Delhi**.
- **Appellate:** civil and criminal; ⭐ **a death sentence by a sessions court must be CONFIRMED by the High Court**.
- ⭐ **Article 227 superintendence** over all courts and tribunals in its territory *(except military)* — **administrative and judicial**, exercisable **suo motu**, but to be used **sparingly**. ⭐ **Article 235** — control over subordinate courts *(posting, promotion, leave)*. **Article 228** — withdraw constitutional cases.
- **Article 215 — court of record**, with contempt power ⭐ **extending to subordinate courts**. **Judicial review** on the same three grounds; ⚠️ **curtailed by the 42nd Amendment, restored by the 43rd**.
