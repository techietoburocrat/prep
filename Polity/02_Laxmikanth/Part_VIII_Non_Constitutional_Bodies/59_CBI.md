# Chapter 59 — Central Bureau of Investigation

[← Chapter 58](58_Central_Vigilance_Commission.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 60 →](60_Lokpal.md)

**Read time ~9 min** · **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946** · 🟠 Prelims: constitutional-vs-statutory format · ⚠️ **Mains: 2021 — a full 250-mark-standard question on state consent**

> 📌 ⭐ **The CBI is the most famous investigating agency in India and it has no law of its own.** ⚠️ **It was created by a Home Ministry RESOLUTION in 1963 and borrows its police powers from a 1946 Act about the Delhi police.** ⭐ **Every problem in this chapter — state consent, federal friction, the "caged parrot" jibe — traces back to that founding weakness.**

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> ⭐⭐ **"Police" is entry 2 of the STATE LIST.** ⚠️ **So a central investigating agency is a constitutional anomaly, and the Constitution permits it only because the states consent to it.** ⭐ **The CBI's entire legal existence in any state rests on Section 6 of the DSPE Act — the consent provision — and that is why [Mains 2021 Q11](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2021.md) asked about it.**

---

## PART A — ⚠️⚠️ The legal foundation, and its defect

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Origin** | The **Special Police Establishment**, created in **1941** to investigate bribery in war-time purchases, put on a statutory footing by the ⭐ **Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946** |
| ⭐⭐ **Created as the "CBI"** | ⚠️ ⭐ **By a RESOLUTION of the MINISTRY OF HOME AFFAIRS in 1963**, on the recommendation of the ⭐ **SANTHANAM Committee** — *the same committee that produced the CVC* |
| ⚠️ ⭐⭐ **Statutory basis** | ⚠️ ⭐ **There is NO "CBI Act."** Its **police powers come entirely from the DSPE Act, 1946** — ⭐ **the CBI is, in law, the Delhi Special Police Establishment** |
| ⭐ **Administrative control** | ⚠️ ⭐ **The DEPARTMENT OF PERSONNEL AND TRAINING (Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions)** — ⚠️ **not the Home Ministry** |
| ⭐ **Supervision** | ⭐ **The CVC supervises its investigations under the PREVENTION OF CORRUPTION ACT, 1988** *(and only those)* |
| ⭐ **Also** | ⭐ **The National Central Bureau of INTERPOL for India** |

> ⚠️ ⭐ **A fact worth flagging:** in ***Navendra Kumar v Union of India* (2013)** the **Gauhati High Court** held that the CBI, having been constituted by an **executive resolution rather than a statute**, was not a validly constituted police force. ⭐ **The Supreme Court stayed that judgment**, and the matter has remained in that state — ⚠️ **so the CBI's foundational legality is a question that has been raised and never conclusively settled.** ⭐ **The **2nd ARC** recommended a dedicated CBI Act.**

### ⭐ Its three divisions

> ⭐ **Anti-Corruption Division** *(PC Act cases against central public servants)* · ⭐ **Economic Offences Division** *(bank frauds, financial crime, counterfeiting)* · ⭐ **Special Crimes Division** *(serious conventional crime — murder, kidnapping, terrorism — taken up on a state's request or a court's order)*.

---

## PART B — ⭐⭐ The Director, and the tenure protection

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Appointment** | ⭐ **By the Central Government on the recommendation of a THREE-member committee: the ⭐ PRIME MINISTER *(chair)*, the ⭐ LEADER OF THE OPPOSITION in the LOK SABHA** *(or the leader of the single largest opposition party, where there is no recognised LoP)*, ⭐ **and the CHIEF JUSTICE OF INDIA or a Judge of the Supreme Court nominated by him** |
| **Introduced by** | ⭐ **The LOKPAL AND LOKAYUKTAS ACT, 2013**, which amended the DSPE Act to create this committee |
| ⭐⭐ **Tenure** | ⭐ **A minimum of TWO years — a protection laid down by the Supreme Court in *VINEET NARAIN* (1997)** and written into the DSPE Act |
| ⭐ **Extension** | ⭐ **The DSPE (Amendment) Act, 2021 permits annual extensions beyond two years, up to a TOTAL of FIVE years** |
| ⭐ **Other officers** | ⭐ **A committee headed by the CENTRAL VIGILANCE COMMISSIONER recommends appointments of officers of the rank of SUPERINTENDENT OF POLICE AND ABOVE** |

> ⚠️ ⭐ **The 2021 amendment is contested, and it is a good "both sides" point.** ⭐ **For:** continuity in long, complex investigations. ⚠️ **Against:** a Director whose extension is decided **annually by the government** has an incentive to please it — ⭐ **which is exactly the defect the two-year minimum was created to cure.** ⭐ **The same criticism applies to the identical amendment for the Enforcement Directorate.**

---

## PART C — ⚠️⚠️ State consent — the heart of the chapter

### ⭐ Section 6 of the DSPE Act

> ⭐⭐ **The powers and jurisdiction of the Delhi Special Police Establishment shall not be extended to any area in a state WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE GOVERNMENT OF THAT STATE.**

| Type of consent | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **GENERAL consent** | ⭐ **A standing, blanket permission** allowing the CBI to register and investigate cases in that state without asking each time. ⭐ **Most states historically gave it** |
| ⭐ **SPECIFIC (case-by-case) consent** | ⭐ **Permission for a named case.** ⚠️ **Where general consent is withdrawn, the CBI must obtain this for every case** |

⚠️ ⭐ **Since 2018 several states have WITHDRAWN general consent** — a genuinely new development in Indian federalism, and the trigger for the 2021 Mains question.

### ⭐⭐ But the consent requirement is NOT absolute — the four qualifications

| # | Qualification | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **1** | ⭐ **The SUPREME COURT and the HIGH COURTS may direct a CBI investigation ANYWHERE IN INDIA WITHOUT the state's consent** | ⭐ ***State of West Bengal v Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights* (2010)** — a **five-judge bench**. ⭐ **It held this power flows from Articles 32 and 226, is part of the courts' duty to enforce Fundamental Rights, and does NOT violate federalism** |
| ⭐ **2** | ⭐ **Withdrawal of general consent operates PROSPECTIVELY** — it does not affect cases already registered, or investigations already under way | Settled by the Supreme Court |
| ⭐ **3** | ⭐ **Consent is not needed for offences committed in a UNION TERRITORY**, or by **central government employees in relation to central subjects**, where the offence is complete outside the state's area | The scheme of the DSPE Act |
| ⭐ **4** | ⭐ **An offence with effects across states** may be registered where the CBI has jurisdiction and investigated onwards | Practice, upheld by courts |

> ⭐⭐ **The formulation for [Mains 2021 Q11](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2021.md):** ⚠️ **"The power of a state to withhold consent is real but not absolute. It binds the EXECUTIVE, not the JUDICIARY."** ⭐ **A state can shut the door on the Union government; it cannot shut it on the Supreme Court or its own High Court.** ⭐ **That is precisely the balance a federal constitution with a unified judiciary produces — and it is worth saying that the unified judiciary is what makes the arrangement workable at all.**

### ⭐ The prior-approval saga — a clean two-step story

| Step | What happened |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Section 6A of the DSPE Act** *(the "single directive")* | Required the **CBI to obtain the Central Government's prior approval** before investigating an officer of the rank of **Joint Secretary and above** |
| ⭐⭐ ***Subramanian Swamy v Director, CBI* (2014)*** | ⭐ **A Constitution Bench STRUCK IT DOWN as violating ARTICLE 14** — corruption does not become a different offence because the accused is senior; the provision **destroyed the object of the PC Act** and impeded the tracking of the corrupt |
| ⚠️ ⭐ **Section 17A, Prevention of Corruption Act** *(inserted by the 2018 amendment)* | ⚠️ ⭐ **Reintroduced a prior-approval requirement by statute** — **no police officer may conduct any enquiry or investigation into an offence alleged against a public servant, where the alleged offence relates to a recommendation made or decision taken in the discharge of official functions, without the previous approval of the appropriate authority** |

> ⚠️ ⭐ **Present this as the pattern it is:** ⭐ **the Court removed an executive shield in 2014; Parliament re-enacted a version of it in 2018.** ⭐ **You have seen the identical pattern in [Ch 39](../Part_VI_Union_Territories_and_Special_Areas/39_Union_Territories.md) (Delhi services) and [Ch 41](../Part_VII_Constitutional_Bodies/41_Election_Commission.md) (ECI appointments).** ⚠️ **A judgment about a statute can always be answered by a new statute — and pointing that out is what turns a descriptive answer into an analytical one.**

### ⚠️ The other criticisms

1. ⚠️ ⭐ **"A caged parrot speaking in its master's voice"** — the Supreme Court's remark in the **coal-blocks** matter *(2013)*, after finding that a draft status report had been shared with the political executive;
2. ⚠️ **No dedicated statute**, leaving jurisdiction, powers and accountability spread across the DSPE Act, the PC Act, a 1963 resolution and court directions;
3. ⚠️ **Administrative control by the DoPT**, and dependence on the government for **staff, sanctions and prosecution**;
4. ⚠️ ⭐ **Sanction for prosecution** under **Section 19 of the PC Act** and **Section 197 CrPC/BNSS** remains with the government, with **no enforceable time-limit in practice**;
5. ⚠️ **Vacancies, overload and low conviction rates** in complex cases;
6. ⚠️ **Perception of selective action**, which is corrosive whether or not it is accurate.

⭐ **The reform list:** ⭐ **a dedicated CBI Act** *(2nd ARC)*; ⭐ **a fixed, non-extendable tenure for the Director**; ⭐ **an independent prosecution directorate**; ⭐ **statutory timelines for sanction**; ⭐ **financial autonomy**; and ⭐ **a transparent case-allocation policy**.

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The legal skeleton
> ⚠️ ⭐⭐ **NO CBI Act.** ⭐ **DSPE Act, 1946 + a Home Ministry RESOLUTION of 1963 (Santhanam Committee).** ⭐ **Administered by the DoPT.** ⭐ **CVC supervises only its PC Act work.**

### The Director
> ⭐ **Committee = PM + Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha + CJI or his nominee** *(created by the **Lokpal Act, 2013**)*. ⭐ **Minimum TWO years (*Vineet Narain*, 1997), extendable annually to FIVE (2021 amendment).**

### Consent
> ⭐⭐ **Section 6 DSPE — no jurisdiction in a state without that state's CONSENT.** ⭐ **General vs case-specific.** ⚠️ ⭐ **BUT the SUPREME COURT and HIGH COURTS can order a CBI probe without consent — *CPDR* (2010), five judges, Articles 32 and 226.** ⭐ **Withdrawal is PROSPECTIVE.**

### The approval saga
> ⭐ **Section 6A struck down in *Subramanian Swamy* (2014) under Article 14** → ⚠️ ⭐ **Section 17A of the PC Act (2018) brought prior approval back.**

### The phrase
> ⚠️ ⭐ **"A caged parrot speaking in its master's voice"** *(Supreme Court, coal-blocks case, 2013)*.

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "The jurisdiction of the CBI regarding lodging an FIR and conducting a probe within a particular state is being questioned by various States. However, the power of States to withhold consent to the CBI is not absolute. Explain with special reference to the federal character of India." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2021 Q11](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2021.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) ⭐ **Why consent exists at all** — **"police" and "public order" are State List entries 2 and 1**; the CBI is the **DSPE**, a Delhi police force, and ⭐ **Section 6 of the DSPE Act requires state consent** to extend it into a state. ⭐ **The requirement is therefore a federal safeguard, not a technicality.** (ii) **The dispute** — several states have **withdrawn general consent** since 2018, alleging selective use of the agency; the CBI must then seek **case-by-case consent**. (iii) ⭐⭐ **Why the power is not absolute** — ⭐ ***State of West Bengal v Committee for Protection of Democratic Rights* (2010)**, five judges: ⭐ **the Supreme Court under Article 32 and a High Court under Article 226 may direct a CBI investigation without the state's consent**, because the duty to enforce Fundamental Rights cannot be defeated by executive refusal, and **this does not violate federalism**; ⭐ **withdrawal is prospective only**; consent is unnecessary for **Union Territories** and for offences complete outside the state. (iv) ⭐ **The federal reading** — India's federalism has a **unified judiciary**, so the courts function as a **constitutional bypass** around executive deadlock. (v) ⚠️ **The legitimate concern on both sides** — states fear the agency's political use; the Union fears local shielding of the powerful. ⭐ **The remedy is institutional, not litigative:** a **dedicated CBI Act** with objective criteria for taking over a case, a fixed-tenure Director, an independent prosecution wing, and a **standing Centre–state consultative mechanism**, plausibly under the **Inter-State Council**.

**2. "The Central Bureau of Investigation lacks the one thing every police force needs — its own law." Discuss. (150 words)**
> *Skeleton:* ⭐ **The CBI exists by a 1963 resolution and borrows powers from the DSPE Act, 1946**; ⚠️ **the Gauhati High Court's 2013 judgment (stayed) questioned that foundation.** **Consequences:** jurisdiction dependent on **state consent**; **administrative control with the DoPT**; ⚠️ **no statutory guarantee of operational autonomy, financial powers or prosecution independence**; accountability rules scattered across a resolution, two Acts and judicial directions. ⭐ **Contrast the NIA**, which has its **own Act (2008)** and ⭐ **needs no state consent**. ⭐ **Conclude with the 2nd ARC's recommendation of a dedicated Act**, and note the counter-point: **a statute alone will not confer independence unless it fixes appointment, tenure and prosecution.**

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- ⚠️ ⭐⭐ **No CBI Act.** ⭐ **Set up by a MINISTRY OF HOME AFFAIRS RESOLUTION, 1963** *(SANTHANAM Committee)*; ⭐ **police powers from the DELHI SPECIAL POLICE ESTABLISHMENT ACT, 1946**; ⭐ **administered by the DoPT**; ⭐ **India's INTERPOL National Central Bureau**.
- ⭐ **Three divisions: Anti-Corruption · Economic Offences · Special Crimes.** ⭐ **The CVC supervises only its PREVENTION OF CORRUPTION ACT investigations.**
- ⭐⭐ **Director appointed by the Central Government on a committee of the PM (chair), the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and the CJI or his nominee** *(inserted by the **Lokpal Act, 2013**)*. ⭐ **Minimum tenure 2 years (*Vineet Narain*, 1997)**; ⭐ **extendable annually up to 5 years (2021 amendment)**. ⭐ **Officers of SP rank and above: a CVC-headed committee recommends.**
- ⭐⭐ **Section 6, DSPE Act — no jurisdiction in a state without that state's CONSENT** *(general or case-specific)*. ⚠️ **Several states withdrew general consent from 2018.**
- ⭐⭐ **But: the SUPREME COURT and HIGH COURTS may order a CBI probe WITHOUT consent — *State of WB v CPDR* (2010), five judges, Articles 32 and 226; and this does NOT violate federalism.** ⭐ **Withdrawal of consent is PROSPECTIVE.**
- ⭐ **Section 6A ("single directive") struck down in *Subramanian Swamy v CBI* (2014) under Article 14** ⚠️ ⭐ **→ Section 17A of the PC Act (2018) restored prior approval by statute.**
- ⚠️ **Criticisms:** the "caged parrot" remark *(2013)* · no dedicated statute · DoPT control · **sanction for prosecution** with the government · vacancies and low conviction rates · perceived selectivity. ⭐ **2nd ARC: enact a CBI Act.**
