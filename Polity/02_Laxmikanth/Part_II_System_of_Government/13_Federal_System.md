# Chapter 13 — Federal System

[← Chapter 12](12_Parliamentary_System.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 14 →](14_Centre_State_Relations.md)

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **The word "federation" appears nowhere in the Constitution.** Article 1 says India is a **"Union of States"** — and Ambedkar chose that word deliberately, to signal two things: the Indian federation was **not created by an agreement among states**, and **no state has the right to leave it**. India is federal in structure and unitary in spirit — a design, not an accident.

---

## 📖 The story

Every federation in history has been built one of two ways.

**By coming together.** Independent states, already sovereign, negotiate and surrender some powers to a new common centre. The **USA** (13 colonies), Switzerland and Australia were made this way. Because the states existed *first*, they keep real bargaining power: equal representation in the Senate, an indestructible existence, their own constitutions.

**By holding together.** A single large country, already unified, devolves powers downward to prevent it from breaking apart. **India, Canada, Spain and Belgium** were made this way. The units were *created by* the centre, so the centre keeps the upper hand.

India is emphatically the second kind, and the framers had a specific reason. They had just watched **Partition**. They were integrating **565 princely states**. They faced linguistic movements, communal violence and a war in Kashmir in the very first year. A federation of the American type — where states could bargain hard and possibly leave — was not a risk they were prepared to run.

> 💡 **The analogy that actually fits:** the USA is a **partnership firm** — independent partners pooled their assets by agreement, and the agreement is the source of the firm's authority. India is a **holding company that created its own subsidiaries** — it drew their boundaries, it can redraw them, and no subsidiary can walk out. Both are "federal" in that power is genuinely divided; but who derives authority from whom is reversed.
>
> *(A note on analogies: the corporate framing is used here only because the direction of authority is the crux. Everywhere else in these notes the analogy is drawn from ordinary life.)*

**Ambedkar's own explanation of the word "Union" is the quotable core:**

> **"The Committee has used the term 'Union' because (a) the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement by the units, and (b) the component units have no freedom to secede from it. The federation is a Union because it is indestructible."**

And then the twist that makes this chapter interesting: **the Supreme Court later held federalism to be part of the basic structure** (*S.R. Bommai*, 1994) — so a Constitution that avoids the word "federal" now protects federalism as unamendable. See [Chapter 11](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md).

## PART A — What makes a federal government

Six standard tests. India passes all six on paper.

| # | Feature | How India satisfies it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dual polity** | The **Union** at the centre and the **states** at the periphery, each with sovereign powers in its own field |
| 2 | **Written Constitution** | A single **written** document specifying the structure, organisation, powers and functions of both levels |
| 3 | ⭐ **Division of powers** | The **Seventh Schedule** — **Union List (100 subjects)**, **State List (61)**, **Concurrent List (52)** |
| 4 | **Supremacy of the Constitution** | It is the **supreme law**; laws made by either level must conform to it, or be declared invalid by the courts |
| 5 | **Rigid Constitution** | Provisions concerning the federal structure can be amended **only with the concurrence of half the states** (Article 368 proviso) |
| 6 | **Independent judiciary** | The Supreme Court settles **Centre–state disputes** and enforces the Constitution's supremacy, protected by security of tenure and service conditions |
| 7 | **Bicameralism** | The **Rajya Sabha** represents the states; the **Lok Sabha** represents the people |

> ⚠️ **Get the Seventh Schedule numbers right — they are directly asked.** Originally **97 / 66 / 47**; now **100 / 61 / 52**. The State List shrank because the **42nd Amendment (1976)** moved five subjects to the Concurrent List — **education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, and administration of justice**. Detail in [Chapter 14](14_Centre_State_Relations.md).

## PART B — The unitary features ⭐

This is the list that wins marks, because most candidates can name three or four. There are **fourteen**.

| # | Unitary feature | The point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Strong Centre** | The **Union List has the most and most important subjects**; the Centre prevails on the Concurrent List; **residuary powers** are with the Centre — the opposite of the US |
| 2 | ⭐ **States not indestructible** | **Article 3** — Parliament can **form a new state, alter any state's area, boundaries or name**, by a **simple majority** and **without the state's consent**. *"India is an indestructible Union of destructible states"* |
| 3 | **Single Constitution** | States have **no right to frame their own constitutions** (unlike US states) |
| 4 | **Flexibility of the Constitution** | The **bulk** of the Constitution can be amended **unilaterally by Parliament**; even where states are needed, **only half** must ratify, and there is **no time limit** |
| 5 | **No equality of state representation** | Rajya Sabha seats are allotted **by population**, not equally — the US Senate gives every state **two** seats regardless of size |
| 6 | ⭐ **Emergency provisions** | During an emergency the Centre becomes **all-powerful** and states go into **total control of the Centre** — *"the federal structure is transformed into a unitary one without a formal amendment"* |
| 7 | **Single citizenship** | Only **Indian citizenship** — no separate state citizenship (contrast the US) |
| 8 | **Integrated judiciary** | A **single system** enforcing both central and state laws, headed by the Supreme Court, with **High Court judges appointed and removable by the President/central process** |
| 9 | **All-India Services** | **IAS, IPS, IFoS** — recruited and trained centrally, serving the states but with **the Centre holding ultimate control** over them |
| 10 | **Integrated audit machinery** | The **CAG** audits both the Centre and the states, but is **appointed and removable by the President** with no say for the states |
| 11 | **Parliament's authority over the State List** | **Articles 249, 250, 252, 253** and **356** — five routes by which Parliament can legislate on state subjects (Chapter 14) |
| 12 | **Appointment of the Governor** | Appointed by the **President**, holds office **during his pleasure** — *"he acts as an agent of the Centre"* |
| 13 | **Integrated election machinery** | A single **Election Commission** conducts elections to both Parliament and the state legislatures, appointed by the **President** with no state role |
| 14 | **Veto over state bills** | **Article 200/201** — the Governor may **reserve** a state bill for the President, who may **withhold assent even in the second round**, giving an absolute veto |

> 🧠 **Mnemonic for the big five:** **S-E-A-G-V** — **S**trong Centre · **E**mergency provisions · **A**ll-India Services · **G**overnor as agent · **V**eto over state bills. If you can only remember five, these five carry the argument.

> ⭐ **Article 3 is the sharpest single fact in the chapter.** In the USA, a state's territory cannot be altered without its consent. In India, Parliament can **redraw, rename or dissolve** a state by a simple majority; the state legislature's views must be *sought*, but **need not be followed**. Reorganisation in 1956, the creation of Telangana in 2014, and the reorganisation of Jammu & Kashmir in 2019 all rest on this article.

## PART C — What the experts called it ⭐

*A question on the "nature of Indian federalism" is answered largely by deploying these labels correctly. Note that they disagree with each other — that disagreement is the answer.*

| Scholar | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **K.C. Wheare** | **"Quasi-federal"** — *"a system of government which is quasi-federal… a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features"* |
| **Sir Ivor Jennings** | *"A federation with a strong centralising tendency"* |
| **Morris-Jones** | **"Bargaining federalism"** |
| **Alexandrowicz** | *"India is a case ***sui generis*** (unique in character)"* — it fits no existing model |
| **Granville Austin** | ⭐ **"Cooperative federalism"** — *"though the Constitution of India has created a strong Central government, it has not made the state governments weak… it is a new kind of federation to meet India's peculiar needs"* |
| **D.D. Basu** | The Constitution is *"neither purely federal nor unitary, but a **combination of both**"* |

### What the Supreme Court has said

| Case | Held |
|---|---|
| ***State of West Bengal v. Union of India*** (1963) | The Indian Constitution is **not federal in the traditional sense**; the Court declined to treat the states as sovereign |
| ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*** (1994) ⭐ | **Federalism is part of the basic structure.** *"The States have an independent constitutional existence… they are not satellites or agents of the Centre."* Also made **Article 356 judicially reviewable** |
| ***Kuldip Nayar*** (2006) | Upheld removal of the domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha candidates — federalism does not require it |

> ⭐ **The dialectic to write:** Wheare (1950s) called it quasi-federal when the Centre was overwhelmingly dominant and one party ruled everywhere. **Bommai (1994)** came after coalition politics, regional parties and repeated misuse of Article 356 — and the Court moved decisively the other way. **The verdict on Indian federalism has shifted with the party system, not with the text.** That single observation is worth more than any list.

## PART D — The USA comparison

*Directly useful: [Mains 2014 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2014.md) and [Mains 2020 Q11](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md) both turn on it.*

| Point | **USA** | **India** |
|---|---|---|
| **How formed** | **Coming together** — by agreement among sovereign states | **Holding together** — units created by the Constitution |
| **Description** | *"Federation of States"* | ⭐ *"**Union of States**"* (Article 1) |
| **Right to secede** | Historically claimed (settled by the Civil War) | **None** — *"the federation is a Union because it is indestructible"* |
| **Territorial integrity of units** | A state's boundaries **cannot** be altered without its consent | **Article 3** — Parliament may alter area, boundaries or name **without consent** |
| **Constitutions** | **Dual** — each state has its own | **Single** for the whole country |
| **Citizenship** | **Dual** — of the US and of the state | **Single** |
| **Residuary powers** | With the **states** | With the **Centre** (Article 248) |
| **Upper house** | **Equal** representation — two Senators per state | **By population** |
| **Judiciary** | **Double** system — federal and state courts | **Integrated**, single |
| **Services** | Separate federal and state services | **All-India Services** common to both |
| **Emergency** | No comparable provision | Emergency provisions convert it to a **unitary** system |

## PART E — The honest verdict

**Do not answer "is India federal?" with yes or no.** The examinable answer has three moves:

1. **In form, federal.** Dual polity, written and rigid constitution, division of powers in the Seventh Schedule, supremacy of the Constitution, independent judiciary, bicameralism. And since **Bommai**, federalism is **basic structure** — unamendable.
2. **In operation, strongly centralised.** Fourteen unitary features, five routes for Parliament into the State List, a Governor appointed by and serving at the pleasure of the Centre, All-India Services, integrated audit and election machinery, and an Article 3 that makes states destructible.
3. **By design, deliberately so.** The framers were not confused. They had just seen Partition and were absorbing 565 princely states. **The centralisation is a response to a specific historical fear.** As Ambedkar put it, the Constitution can be *"both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances"* — the flexibility **is** the feature.

**And the modern trajectory matters.** The direction of travel since 1990 has been **towards** the states — coalition governments, powerful regional parties, *Bommai* restraining Article 356, the **Inter-State Council** (1990), the **GST Council** as genuine shared sovereignty, and Finance Commission devolution rising to **41%**. Pulling the other way: **centrally sponsored schemes** with tied conditions, the shrinking share of divisible-pool taxes through **cesses and surcharges**, and Governors' conduct in reserving bills. *(All of this is detailed in [Chapter 14](14_Centre_State_Relations.md).)*

> **The formulation to close with:** India is a federation whose **structure is federal, whose spirit is national, and whose balance is political rather than textual.** It shifts with the party system — and the Constitution was written loosely enough to let it.

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The one-sentence definition
> **India is a "Union of States" — federal in structure because power is genuinely divided by the Seventh Schedule, but unitary in spirit because the Centre can redraw the states, legislate in their field, and take over entirely in an emergency.**

### Ambedkar's two reasons for the word "Union"
> **(a) not the result of an agreement by the units · (b) the units have no freedom to secede.** *"The federation is a Union because it is indestructible."*

### The Seventh Schedule numbers
> **Union 100 · State 61 · Concurrent 52** *(originally **97 / 66 / 47**)*. The State List shrank via the **42nd Amendment** — **education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, administration of justice**.

### The five sharpest unitary features — S-E-A-G-V
> **S**trong Centre · **E**mergency provisions · **A**ll-India Services · **G**overnor as the Centre's agent · **V**eto over state bills.

### The labels, by author
> **Wheare** → quasi-federal · **Jennings** → strong centralising tendency · **Morris-Jones** → bargaining federalism · **Alexandrowicz** → *sui generis* · **Granville Austin** → **cooperative federalism** · **Basu** → a combination of both.

### The two cases
> ***West Bengal v. Union of India* (1963)** — not federal in the traditional sense.
> ***S.R. Bommai* (1994)** — **federalism is basic structure**; states are *"not satellites or agents of the Centre"*; **Article 356 is judicially reviewable**.

### The one-line India–USA contrast
> **USA = coming-together, indestructible states. India = holding-together, indestructible Union.** *(Note which noun carries "indestructible" in each — that is the whole difference.)*

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Though the federal principle is dominant in our Constitution and that principle is one of its basic features, it is equally true that federalism under the Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong Centre, a feature that militates against the concept of strong federalism." Discuss. (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2014 Q2](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2014.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) Establish the federal principle as basic structure — *Bommai*, and the six federal features. (ii) Establish the tilt — Article 3, residuary powers, Articles 249/250/252/253/356, Governor, All-India Services, emergency provisions. (iii) Resolve the apparent contradiction: **basic structure protects federalism as a principle, not any particular distribution of powers** — the Centre may be strong so long as the states retain an independent constitutional existence. (iv) Conclude with the trajectory: GST Council, Inter-State Council and 41% devolution show the balance is **negotiated**, not fixed.

**2. "Indian Constitution exhibits centralising tendencies to maintain unity and integrity of the nation. Enumerate the essential features of the Constitution which have resulted in a strong Centre." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2020 Q11](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Structure the fourteen features into **four buckets** rather than listing them flat — **legislative** (Union List's weight, residuary powers, Articles 249/250/252/253), **administrative** (Governor, All-India Services, Centre's directions, integrated election and audit machinery), **financial** (dependence on devolution and grants), and **crisis** (emergency provisions, Article 356). Then justify: Partition, 565 princely states, secessionist movements. Conclude with the safeguards that have since developed — *Bommai*, the Inter-State Council, the GST Council.

**3. "How far do you think cooperation, competition and confrontation have shaped the nature of federation in India? Cite some recent examples to validate your answer." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2020 Q3](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2020.md).*
> *Skeleton:* **Cooperation** — GST Council, Inter-State Council, zonal councils, NITI Aayog's Governing Council, disaster response. **Competition** — Ease of Doing Business rankings, race for investment, state-level performance indices; note that competitive federalism can **widen** inter-state inequality. **Confrontation** — Governor's conduct and delayed assent to bills, CBI "general consent" withdrawals, disputes over cesses and the divisible pool, the Delhi services dispute. Conclude: the three coexist, and **the institutional design determines which one dominates in a given decade**.

**4. Did the Government of India Act, 1935 lay down a federal constitution? Discuss. (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2016 Q7](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2016.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Yes in design — it provided for an **All India Federation** of British Indian provinces and princely states, a **three-fold division of powers** (Federal, Provincial, Concurrent Lists), a **Federal Court** (1937), and **provincial autonomy** replacing dyarchy in the provinces. **But the federation never came into existence** — the princely states did not accede — and **Defence and External Affairs remained reserved with the Governor-General**, i.e. dyarchy at the Centre. Conclude: federal in blueprint, unitary in operation — and note that it is the **direct ancestor** of our Seventh Schedule. *(This was tested in [Prelims 2024 Q62](../../03_PYQ_Prelims/2024.md) too.)*

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **The word "federation" never appears.** **Article 1: "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States."** Ambedkar: **not an agreement by units; no right to secede; "indestructible."**
- **Federal features (7):** dual polity · written Constitution · **division of powers (Seventh Schedule)** · supremacy of the Constitution · rigid Constitution · independent judiciary · bicameralism.
- **Seventh Schedule: Union 100 · State 61 · Concurrent 52** (originally **97/66/47**). **42nd Amendment** moved **education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, administration of justice** to Concurrent.
- **Unitary features (14):** strong Centre · **Article 3 — states not indestructible** · single Constitution · flexible amendment · **no equality of state representation** · **emergency provisions** · single citizenship · **integrated judiciary** · **All-India Services** · integrated audit (CAG) · **Parliament's authority over the State List** · **Governor appointed by the Centre** · integrated election machinery · **veto over state bills**.
- **The labels:** Wheare **quasi-federal** · Jennings **strong centralising tendency** · Morris-Jones **bargaining federalism** · Alexandrowicz ***sui generis*** · Granville Austin **cooperative federalism** · Basu **a combination of both**.
- **The cases:** *West Bengal v. Union* (1963) — not federal in the traditional sense. ⭐ ***S.R. Bommai*** (1994) — **federalism is basic structure**, states *"not satellites or agents"*, **Article 356 justiciable**.
- **USA vs India:** coming-together vs holding-together · Federation vs **Union** · dual vs single constitution and citizenship · residuary with **states** vs **Centre** · equal vs population-based upper house · double vs **integrated** judiciary · no emergency provision vs one that makes the system **unitary**.
- **The verdict:** **federal in form, unitary in spirit, deliberately so** — and the balance moves with the **party system**, not the text. Since 1990 it has drifted toward the states (*Bommai*, Inter-State Council 1990, **GST Council**, **41% devolution**) while cesses, surcharges and centrally sponsored schemes pull the other way.
