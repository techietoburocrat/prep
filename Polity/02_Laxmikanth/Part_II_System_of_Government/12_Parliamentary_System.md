# Chapter 12 — Parliamentary System

[← Chapter 11](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md) · [Part Index](00_INDEX.md) · [Master](../00_INDEX.md) · [Chapter 13 →](13_Federal_System.md)

**Read time ~10 min** · **First chapter of Part II — System of Government** · Prelims: asked in **2013, 2017 (twice), 2020** · Mains: **2000, 2001, 2002, 2003, 2007**

> 📌 **Part I asked *what* the Constitution is. Part II asks *how the government actually runs*.** This chapter is the foundation for everything in Part III — the President, the Prime Minister, the Council of Ministers and Parliament all make sense only once you have this chapter's logic.

---

## 🎯 The one-line point

> **India chose a system in which the executive is not separate from the legislature — it is *drawn from* it and *answerable to* it every single day.** The government exists only as long as the Lok Sabha wants it to. That daily, revocable permission is the whole idea, and Ambedkar chose it knowing it would cost him stability.

---

## 📖 The story

Two ways to build a democracy.

**Option A — separate them.** Elect a President directly for a fixed term. Elect a legislature separately. Neither can dismiss the other. Each checks the other. This is the **American presidential system**.

**Option B — fuse them.** Let the people elect only a legislature. Whoever commands a majority there becomes the executive. If the majority evaporates, the executive falls — immediately, without waiting for the next election. This is the **British parliamentary system**, and it is what India adopted.

> 💡 **The analogy that fits it best:** a presidential executive is like a **fixed-term contract** — agreed in advance, hard to terminate early, and both sides are stuck with each other until it expires. A parliamentary executive is like **employment at will** — the employer (the Lok Sabha) can end it any day, for any reason, by a simple vote. It is far less secure, and that insecurity is precisely the point: it keeps the employee answerable continuously rather than once every five years.

The Constituent Assembly debated this seriously, and **Ambedkar's answer is the most quoted passage on the subject:**

> **"A democratic executive must satisfy two conditions — *stability* and *responsibility*. Unfortunately, it has not been possible so far to devise a system which can ensure both in equal degree… The American system gives more stability but less responsibility. The British system, on the other hand, gives more responsibility but less stability. The Draft Constitution, in recommending the parliamentary system of Executive, has preferred more responsibility to more stability."**

⭐ **Read that as a deliberate trade-off, not a compromise.** Ambedkar knew he was buying instability. He paid for it because a newly independent, deeply diverse country needed a government that could be **questioned every day** more than it needed one that could not be removed.

**And the bill came due.** Between 1977 and 1999, India had **nine Prime Ministers**. Charan Singh (1979) resigned without ever facing Parliament. Vajpayee's first government lasted **13 days** in 1996; his second fell in 1999 by **a single vote**. That is the price of "more responsibility" — and the system absorbed it without a constitutional breakdown, which is the case for the defence.

## PART A — Where it is written

The Constitution never uses the phrase "parliamentary system". It builds it out of four articles.

| Article | Level | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Article 74** | Centre | *"There shall be a **Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head** to **aid and advise** the President who shall… act in accordance with such advice"* |
| **Article 75** | Centre | Other provisions as to Ministers — appointment, **collective responsibility**, oath, salaries |
| **Article 163** | State | Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the **Governor** — *"except in so far as he is… required to exercise his functions… in his discretion"* |
| **Article 164** | State | Other provisions as to Ministers at the state level |

### ⚠️ How Article 74 became binding — the two amendments

| | Position |
|---|---|
| **Originally** | The President "shall act **in accordance with** the advice" — but the courts held the advice was **not justiciable**, and the text did not say it was binding |
| **42nd Amendment (1976)** | Made the advice **explicitly binding** on the President |
| **44th Amendment (1978)** | Kept it binding, but added: the President **may require the Council to reconsider** such advice — and **"shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration"** |

⭐ **So the President's real power is exactly one round of delay.** He can send it back once; if it comes back unchanged, he must sign. This is the single most examinable fact about Article 74, and it is where the "pocket veto" discussion belongs (see [Chapter 11](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md) for how the 42nd/44th pair recurs across the syllabus).

> **Note the contrast with Article 163 immediately.** The Governor has an express **discretion** clause; the President does not. That one difference generates most of the Centre–State litigation in [Chapter 14](14_Centre_State_Relations.md).

## PART B — The eight features ⭐

This list is the highest-frequency Prelims item in the chapter.

| # | Feature | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Nominal and Real Executives** | The **President is the nominal (*de jure*) executive**; the **Prime Minister is the real (*de facto*) executive**. The President is head of **State**; the PM is head of **Government** |
| 2 | **Majority Party Rule** | The party (or coalition) with a majority in the **Lok Sabha** forms the government; its leader is appointed Prime Minister |
| 3 | ⭐ **Collective Responsibility** | **Article 75(3)** — the Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**. *"The bedrock principle of parliamentary government"* |
| 4 | **Political Homogeneity** | Ministers usually belong to the same party and share a common political ideology — less true in coalition governments |
| 5 | **Double Membership** | Ministers are members of **both the legislature and the executive** — a person who is not a member of Parliament can be a minister for a **maximum of six months** |
| 6 | **Leadership of the Prime Minister** | The PM is the leader of the Council of Ministers, of Parliament, and of the ruling party |
| 7 | **Dissolution of the Lower House** | The **Lok Sabha can be dissolved** by the President on the PM's recommendation. The Rajya Sabha cannot be |
| 8 | **Secrecy** | Ministers take an **oath of secrecy** before entering office, administered by the President |

> 🧠 **Mnemonic — "NM-C-P-D-L-D-S":** Nominal/real · Majority · **Collective responsibility** · Political homogeneity · Double membership · Leadership of PM · Dissolution · Secrecy.

### ⭐ Collective responsibility — the feature everything else hangs on

Article 75(3) has three practical consequences UPSC tests repeatedly:

1. **They swim or sink together.** A vote of no confidence against **any one minister** — or against the Council — means **the entire Council must resign**, including ministers from the Rajya Sabha, who are not even members of the House that voted them out.
2. **Cabinet decisions bind every minister**, whether or not he attended the meeting or agreed with it. A minister who cannot defend a decision in public **must resign**. *"They sink or swim together."*
3. **Individual responsibility (Article 75(2))** is separate: ministers hold office **during the pleasure of the President**, which means the President can remove a minister — but only **on the advice of the Prime Minister**. This is the PM's real disciplinary power.

**The real incident that shows it working:** in **1999**, the Vajpayee government lost a confidence motion **269–270 — by one vote**. Two things decided it: the AIADMK withdrew support, and **Giridhar Gamang**, who had become Chief Minister of Odisha but had not yet resigned his Lok Sabha seat, voted against the government. The entire Council of Ministers fell on a single vote by a man who was already a Chief Minister. **That is collective responsibility, executed literally.**

### ⚠️ The six-month rule — with the real examples

A person who is **not a member of either House** may be appointed a minister, but **must become a member within six months** or cease to be a minister.

- **P.V. Narasimha Rao** became Prime Minister in **1991** without being a member of either House; he was later elected from **Nandyal**.
- **Manmohan Singh** was Prime Minister for **ten years (2004–2014) as a member of the Rajya Sabha**, never having won a Lok Sabha seat — perfectly constitutional, and a favourite Prelims trap.

> **The trap:** candidates assume the PM must be a Lok Sabha member. **In India the PM may belong to either House.** In **Britain**, by convention, the PM must be a member of the **House of Commons**. That difference is examinable (see Part E).

## PART C — The presidential system, for contrast

You cannot answer a comparison question without knowing what you are comparing to. The **American** model:

| Feature | Presidential system |
|---|---|
| **The office** | The President is **both head of State and head of government** |
| **Election** | Elected by an **electoral college** for a **fixed term of four years** |
| **Removal** | **Cannot be removed except by impeachment** for a grave unconstitutional act |
| **His ministers** | Called **Secretaries** — *"only advisers"*, **appointed and removed by him alone**; the "Kitchen Cabinet" is his informal advisory body |
| **Responsibility** | The President and his Secretaries are **not responsible to Congress**; they **do not sit in it** and **cannot be removed by it** |
| **Dissolution** | The President **cannot dissolve** the House of Representatives |
| **Underlying doctrine** | **Separation of powers** — the legislature, executive and judiciary are separate and independent |

**The mirror-image summary:** parliamentary government rests on **cooperation and coordination** between the legislative and executive organs; presidential government rests on their **separation**.

## PART D — Merits and demerits ⭐

*This table is the whole answer to "aberrations of the parliamentary system" ([Mains 2001](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2001.md)) and to the presidential-system question ([Mains 2002](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2002.md)).*

### ✅ Merits

| | Merit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Harmony between legislature and executive** | The executive **is** part of the legislature; both organs work in cooperation, so deadlock of the American kind is structurally impossible |
| 2 | **Responsible government** | Question Hour, discussions, adjournment motions, no-confidence motions, censure motions — Parliament can hold the executive to account **continuously**, not once in five years |
| 3 | **Prevents despotism** | Authority is vested in a **group of persons (the Council of Ministers)**, not one individual, and that group can be removed at any time |
| 4 | **Ready alternative government** | If the ruling party loses its majority, the Head of State can invite the opposition to form a government — **no fresh elections are needed** |
| 5 | **Wide representation** | The PM can build a Council representing **different regions, castes, communities and minorities** — critical in a society as diverse as India's |

### ❌ Demerits

| | Demerit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Unstable government** | No guarantee of a full term; a loss of confidence or defection can bring it down. **1977–1999: nine Prime Ministers** |
| 2 | **No continuity of policies** | Frequent changes of government mean policies change with them; permanent civil servants find long-term planning difficult |
| 3 | ⭐ **Dictatorship of the Cabinet** | When the ruling party has an **absolute majority and strong internal discipline**, the Cabinet becomes autocratic and Parliament's control becomes formal — this is the most serious modern criticism |
| 4 | **Against separation of powers** | The same people make and execute the law; the legislature cannot really check an executive that controls its majority |
| 5 | **Government by amateurs** | Ministers are chosen for **political** reasons — party loyalty, regional balance — not administrative expertise. *"The system is not conducive for administrative efficiency"* |

> 💡 **How to deploy this in Mains.** Do not simply list. The strongest structure pairs each demerit with the merit it is the *shadow of*: the cooperation that prevents deadlock (merit 1) is the same fusion that produces cabinet dictatorship (demerit 3); the responsibility that allows daily removal (merit 2) is the same mechanism that produces instability (demerit 1). **They are not separate lists — they are the same features seen from two sides.** That observation alone lifts an answer.

## PART E — Why India chose it, and how India's differs from Britain's

### ⭐ The four reasons for adopting it

| # | Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Familiarity with the system** | Indians had **already worked** a limited parliamentary system under the **Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935** — it was not an experiment |
| 2 | **Preference for more responsibility** | Ambedkar's stability-vs-responsibility trade-off (quoted above) |
| 3 | **Need to avoid legislature–executive conflicts** | The framers wanted to avoid the **deadlocks** that separation of powers produces in the American system, which a newly independent country could not afford |
| 4 | **Nature of Indian society** | India is one of the most **heterogeneous** societies on earth, with enormous diversity — a parliamentary system offers **greater scope for giving representation to all sections** |

### The differences from the British system ⭐

| | **India** | **Britain** |
|---|---|---|
| **Form of state** | **Republican** — an elected Head of State | **Monarchical** — a hereditary Head of State |
| **Which House the PM belongs to** | May be a member of **either House** *(Manmohan Singh — Rajya Sabha)* | By convention, must be a member of the **House of Commons** |
| **Non-members as ministers** | A person **not a member of Parliament** can be a minister for up to **six months** | **Only members of Parliament** can be appointed ministers |
| **Legal responsibility of ministers** | **No system of legal responsibility** — no countersignature required on the President's official acts | Ministers must **countersign** the official acts of the Head of State |
| **Shadow Cabinet** | **No such institution** | The **'shadow cabinet'** is a unique institution of the British cabinet system — the opposition forms a parallel cabinet to criticise and prepare for office |
| **Sovereignty of the legislature** | Parliament is **not sovereign** — bound by a **written Constitution**, **federal division of powers**, **judicial review** and **Fundamental Rights** | Parliament **is sovereign** |

> **The most tested row is "sovereignty".** Indian Parliament is not sovereign — and [Chapter 11's](../Part_I_Constitutional_Framework/11_Basic_Structure.md) basic structure doctrine is the sharpest proof of that.

### The three names the system is known by

**Westminster model** of government · **Responsible government** · **Cabinet government**.

## 🧠 Memory hooks

### The one-sentence definition
> **A parliamentary system is one in which the real executive is drawn from the legislature, sits in it, and remains in office only so long as it retains the confidence of the popularly elected House.**

### The Ambedkar trade-off (say it verbatim)
> **"The American system gives more stability but less responsibility. The British system gives more responsibility but less stability. The Draft Constitution… has preferred more responsibility to more stability."**

### The four articles
> **74 and 75** = Centre · **163 and 164** = States. **74** creates the Council; **75(3)** makes it collectively responsible; **163** adds the Governor's **discretion**, which 74 does not give the President.

### The 42nd–44th pair
> **42nd (1976)** made the advice **binding**. **44th (1978)** added **one reconsideration**, after which the President **must** comply. *The President's entire independent power is one round of delay.*

### The numbers
> **Six months** — maximum period a non-member may remain a minister
> **Four years** — fixed term of the US President
> **Nine Prime Ministers** between **1977 and 1999**
> **269–270** — the single vote that ended the Vajpayee government in **1999**
> **13 days** — Vajpayee's first government, **1996**

### Which system has what
> **Fixed term · separation · Secretaries · impeachment only · no dissolution** = **Presidential**
> **Confidence · fusion · Council of Ministers · no-confidence motion · dissolution** = **Parliamentary**

## ❓ Expected Mains questions

**1. "Bring out the aberrations of the parliamentary system of government in India." (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2001 Q6(b)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2001.md).*
> *Skeleton:* (i) State the design intent — responsibility preferred over stability. (ii) The aberrations: **cabinet dictatorship** under a disciplined majority; **defection and horse-trading**, which produced the anti-defection law; **instability and coalition compulsions** (nine PMs in 22 years); **decline of Parliament** — falling sittings, bills passed without scrutiny, disrupted Question Hour; **government by amateurs**; the **ordinance route** bypassing the House. (iii) Concede the counter-evidence — power has transferred peacefully every time; the 1999 one-vote defeat shows the mechanism still works. (iv) Conclude: the aberrations are of **practice, not design** — the remedies are stronger committees, guaranteed opposition time, and an anti-defection law that penalises the *party* as well as the defector.

**2. "The issue of a hung Parliament adversely affects the stability of Indian Government." Discuss, and point out how far changing over to the Presidential form would be a solution. (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2002 Q6(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2002.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Accept the premise partially (1989–1999 instability), then attack the proposed remedy: a presidential system imports **deadlock** in place of instability; India's **diversity** needs a Council that can represent many groups, which a single directly elected President cannot; a fixed-term President who loses legitimacy **cannot be removed** — far worse in a plural society; and the fusion of powers is arguably **basic structure**. Conclude that the fix is **within** the parliamentary system — anti-defection reform, constructive vote of no confidence (the German model), fixed-term parliaments.

**3. "How does Parliament control the Union Executive? How effective is that control?" (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2000 Q7(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2000.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Instruments — Question Hour, zero hour, half-an-hour discussion, adjournment/calling attention motions, **no-confidence and censure motions**, financial control (budget, cut motions, PAC/Estimates/Public Undertakings committees), and **Article 75(3)**. Then the effectiveness critique: a disciplined majority makes most of these formal; declining sittings; the guillotine; ordinances; money-bill routing. Conclude: control is **strongest through committees and weakest on the floor**.

**4. What are the exceptions when the President is not bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers? (250 words)**
> *Actually asked — [Mains 2007 Q8(a)](../../04_PYQ_Mains/2007.md).*
> *Skeleton:* Start from Article 74 as amended by the 42nd and 44th. Then the genuine situational discretion: **appointing a PM** when no party has a clear majority; **dismissing a government** that has lost confidence but will not resign; **dissolving the Lok Sabha** when the Council has lost majority support; **returning advice once** for reconsideration; **withholding assent / pocket veto** on ordinary bills (Zail Singh and the Indian Post Office Bill, 1986); and assent to **Constitutional Amendment Bills is obligatory** (24th Amendment). Conclude: the discretion is **residual and political, not legal**.

## ⚡ 60-second revision

- **The choice:** fusion of legislature and executive (parliamentary/Westminster) over separation (presidential). **Ambedkar: responsibility over stability.**
- **Articles: 74 and 75** (Centre) · **163 and 164** (States). **75(3) = collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha — the bedrock principle.**
- **42nd Amendment (1976)** made the advice **binding**; **44th (1978)** allows the President **one reconsideration**, after which he must comply.
- **Eight features:** nominal/real executives · majority party rule · **collective responsibility** · political homogeneity · double membership · leadership of the PM · dissolution of the Lower House · secrecy.
- **Collective responsibility in practice:** no confidence against one = **all must resign**; Cabinet decisions bind all; a dissenting minister must resign. Individual responsibility under **75(2)** — pleasure of the President, exercised **on the PM's advice**.
- **Six-month rule** for a non-member minister. **PM may be from either House** — Manmohan Singh, Rajya Sabha, 10 years. *(Britain: Commons only.)*
- **Presidential system:** head of State **and** government · **fixed four-year term** · removable **only by impeachment** · **Secretaries are advisers** appointed and removed by him · **not responsible to Congress** · **cannot dissolve** the House · built on **separation of powers**.
- **Merits:** harmony · responsible government · prevents despotism · **ready alternative government** · wide representation.
- **Demerits:** instability · no continuity of policy · **dictatorship of the Cabinet** · against separation of powers · government by amateurs.
- **Four reasons India chose it:** familiarity (**1919 and 1935 Acts**) · more responsibility · avoid deadlocks · **heterogeneous society**.
- **India vs Britain:** republican vs monarchical · either House vs Commons only · **6-month non-member rule** vs members only · **no legal responsibility/countersignature** · **no shadow cabinet** · **Parliament not sovereign** vs sovereign.
- **Real incidents:** **1996** Vajpayee 13 days · **1999** lost **269–270** by one vote (Giridhar Gamang) · **1991** Narasimha Rao PM without a seat, later elected from Nandyal.
