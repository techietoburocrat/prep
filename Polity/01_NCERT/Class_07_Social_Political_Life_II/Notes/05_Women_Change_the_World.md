# Chapter 5: Women Change the World

## The Big Idea (in one line)
People wrongly believe certain jobs are "only for men" and others "only for women" (**stereotypes**), and for a long time girls were denied education. But brave women fought to learn, work, and win their rights — a fight that continues as the **Women's Movement.**

---

## Part 1: "Who does what work?" — the classroom experiment 🎨
Rosie Ma'am asked her 30 students to *draw* a farmer, factory worker, nurse, scientist, pilot, and teacher. Then she counted how many drew each as a man vs a woman:

| Job | Drawn as MAN | Drawn as WOMAN |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher | 5 | 25 |
| Farmer | 30 | **0** |
| Factory worker | 25 | 5 |
| Nurse | **0** | 30 |
| Scientist | 25 | 5 |
| Pilot | 27 | 3 |

**Notice the pattern?** Nurses and teachers → almost all women. Farmers, scientists, pilots → almost all men. The children weren't told to do this — their **minds were already trained** by society to link certain jobs with certain genders.

> **The shocking truth:** **83.6% of India's working women do agricultural work** (planting, weeding, harvesting, threshing)! Yet **not a single child** drew a farmer as a woman. Women farmers are everywhere — but society has made them *invisible* in our imagination.

---

## Part 2: Stereotypes and rigid expectations
A **stereotype** is a fixed idea that a whole group *must* have certain qualities or can only do certain work.

- "Women make better nurses because they're patient and gentle." → stereotype
- "Science needs a technical mind, and girls can't handle technical things." → stereotype

**Why are stereotypes dangerous?** Because when people *believe* them:
- Girls don't get the same support boys get to become doctors or engineers.
- Many families push girls to see **marriage as their main aim** after finishing school, instead of a career.

> **Analogy — the invisible fence:** A stereotype is like an invisible fence around a person. No one *physically* stops a girl from becoming a pilot, but the constant message "that's not for girls" fences her in until she believes it herself. The chapter is about girls who *jumped the fence.*

### 🚂 Breaking the stereotype: Laxmi Lakra
"Engine drivers are men" — everyone believed this. Then **Laxmi Lakra**, a 27-year-old from a poor tribal family in Jharkhand, became the **first woman engine driver for Northern Railways.**
- Her parents couldn't read or write, but they struggled to educate their children.
- Laxmi studied in a government school, did housework and odd jobs, earned a diploma in electronics, and **passed the railway board exam on her first attempt.**
- Her attitude: *"The moment somebody says it is not for girls, I make sure I go ahead and do it."*
- Her philosophy: *"As long as I am having fun without harming anyone... why should I not lead a lifestyle of my choice?"*

## Part 3: Boys feel pressure too! (The Xavier story)
It's not only girls who are boxed in. **Xavier** did well in his favourite subjects — **History and Languages** — but only got 65% in Maths and low marks in Physics. His parents were upset:
- *"Why History? It has no scope! You have to get a good job!"*
- *"Be sensible, son. Take Maths and computers — the job market is very good."*

Xavier keeps saying *"But I don't like Maths or Science!"* — and no one listens.

> **The point:** Society pressures **boys** to chase high-paying jobs and to "be tough" (remember from Class 6 — boys are told not to cry). Boys who don't behave "like other boys" get teased and bullied. Gender stereotypes trap *everyone*, just in different ways.

---

## Part 4: Learning for change — why education was a battlefield for women
Today we think it's *normal* for all children to go to school. But long ago, **reading and writing were known to very few** — most children just learned their family's trade. And for girls, it was worse:
- In families that taught **sons** to read, **daughters were forbidden** to learn the alphabet.
- Even in craft families (pottery, weaving), women did key work but got no credit. *Example:* in pottery, women collected the mud and prepared the earth — but because they didn't spin the wheel, they were **not counted as "potters."**

In the **19th century**, new ideas spread and schools became common — but there was **fierce opposition to educating girls.** Meet three women who fought back:

### 📖 Rashsundari Devi (1800–1890)
- A housewife from a rich Bengali family. Back then people believed **if a woman learned to read, her husband would die and she'd become a widow!**
- Despite this terrifying superstition, she **secretly taught herself to read** — after marriage — by hiding a page of her son's book in the kitchen and matching letters over years of "tremendous care and effort."
- At age 60, she wrote *Amar Jiban* — the **first known autobiography written by an Indian woman.**

### 📖 Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932)
- From a rich family; allowed to learn Urdu but *stopped* from learning Bangla and English (English was thought to give girls "dangerous new ideas").
- She learned anyway, secretly helped by her **elder brother and sister**, and became a writer.
- In 1905 she wrote *Sultana's Dream* — a story imagining **"Ladyland,"** where women are free to study, work, and invent (like flying air-cars and controlling rain!), while men are kept in seclusion. She dreamed of women flying planes **before girls were even allowed in school!**
- In 1910 she started a **girls' school in Kolkata that still runs today.**

### 📖 Ramabai (1858–1922)
- Never went to school, but learned from her parents. She earned the title **"Pandita"** for reading and writing **Sanskrit** — extraordinary, since women were forbidden that knowledge.
- In 1898 she set up a **Mission near Pune** where widows and poor women learned to be *independent* — trained in everything from carpentry to running a printing press. Still active today.

> **The common thread:** For these women, learning the alphabet wasn't just about books — it was the **key to freedom, dignity, and helping other women.** They didn't stop at educating themselves; they opened doors for others.

## Part 5: Education today — better, but still unequal 📊
India holds a **Census every 10 years** to count the population and gather data (age, schooling, work, literacy).

**Literacy (people who can read/write):**
| Year | Boys & Men literate | Girls & Women literate |
|---|---|---|
| 1961 | ~40% | just 15% |
| 2011 | 82% | 65% |

Both improved a lot — but a **gap between men and women still remains.**

**School drop-out rates (2014–15)** show another problem — it's worst for **SC (Dalit), ST (Adivasi), and Muslim** girls:
- ST (Adivasi) children drop out the most at **every** level (e.g., 6.93% at primary vs 4.13% for "All").
- Drop-out is **highest at the Secondary level (Classes 9–10)** — around 17% overall, and nearly **25% for Adivasi students.**
- A Muslim girl stays in school ~3 years on average; girls from other communities ~4 years.

**Why do these children leave school?** (It is *not* because they "aren't interested"!)
- No proper school nearby, or no regular teachers.
- No transport, so parents won't send girls far away.
- Families too poor to educate all children → **boys get preference.**
- Children face **discrimination** from teachers and classmates.

*(The government's **Beti Bachao Beti Padhao** campaign, launched 2014, tackles exactly this.)*

## Part 6: The Women's Movement — how change actually happened ✊
Rights didn't fall from the sky — women (and supportive men) **struggled** for them, individually and together. This struggle is called the **Women's Movement.** It uses many strategies:

| Strategy | What it means | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| **Campaigning** | Fighting for new laws against discrimination/violence | The **2006 law** against **domestic violence**; **1997 Supreme Court guidelines** against **sexual harassment** at work; 1980s campaigns against **dowry deaths** → dowry laws made stricter |
| **Raising Awareness** | Spreading the message widely | Street plays, songs, public meetings |
| **Protesting** | Raising voice against violations and unfair laws | Public rallies and demonstrations |
| **Showing Solidarity** | Standing together with other women and causes | **8 March – International Women's Day** celebrated worldwide |

> **The big message of the chapter:** When change seems impossible for one person alone, people join together. **Change on a large scale usually happens through collective struggle** — many hands, many voices, over many years.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stereotype | Believing a whole group (by religion, wealth, gender, etc.) must have fixed traits or can only do certain work; it stops us seeing people as unique individuals |
| Discrimination | Not treating people equally or with respect; acting on one's prejudices |
| Violation | Forcefully breaking a law/rule, or openly showing disrespect |
| Sexual harassment | Physical or verbal behaviour of a sexual nature, against a woman's wishes |
| Women's Movement | The struggle — by women individually and in groups (with men's support) — for equality and rights |
| Census | The counting of a country's whole population every 10 years, with detailed data |

## Quick Recap (6 points to remember)
1. **Stereotypes** wrongly label jobs as "for men" or "for women" — the classroom drawing experiment (0 women farmers, though 83.6% of working women farm!) proves how deep this bias runs.
2. Stereotypes **hurt everyone**: girls are pushed toward marriage over careers; boys (like Xavier) are pushed toward "high-scope" jobs and told to be tough.
3. **Laxmi Lakra** (first woman engine driver, Northern Railways) shows stereotypes can be broken.
4. Girls were historically **denied education**; **Rashsundari Devi, Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain, and Ramabai** fought to learn and to teach other women.
5. Literacy has risen (1961 → 2011) but a **gender gap remains**, and SC/ST/Muslim girls drop out most — due to poverty, distance, and discrimination, *not* lack of interest.
6. The **Women's Movement** wins rights through campaigning, raising awareness, protesting, and showing solidarity — change comes from **collective struggle.**
