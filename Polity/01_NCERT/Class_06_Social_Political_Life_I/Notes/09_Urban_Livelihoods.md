# Chapter 9: Urban Livelihoods

## The Big Idea (in one line)
In towns and cities, people earn their living in many different jobs, which can be broadly grouped into the **organised sector** (secure, regular jobs) and the **unorganised sector** (insecure, irregular jobs).

## Why are city livelihoods so varied?
Unlike villages, where farming dominates, cities have factories, offices, shops, construction sites, and many kinds of businesses — so people's jobs in cities are much more varied. This chapter groups all these different jobs into two broad categories.

## The Organised Sector
Jobs in the organised sector usually have:
- A **fixed salary**, paid regularly (e.g., monthly).
- **Job security** — the person is not likely to suddenly lose their job.
- Official **registration** with the government, and the employer must follow labour laws.
- Benefits like paid leave, medical benefits, and sometimes a pension (money paid after retirement).

**Examples**: workers in registered factories, government employees, bank employees, teachers in established schools, employees in large private companies.

## The Unorganised (Informal) Sector
Jobs in the unorganised sector usually have:
- **No fixed employer** in many cases, or the employer does not officially register the worker.
- **No job security** — work can stop any day without notice.
- **No fixed wages guaranteed** — income can go up or down unpredictably.
- **No benefits** — usually no paid leave, no medical benefits, no pension.
- Workers are often **more vulnerable** — for example, street vendors can be forced to move by police, and construction workers may not get paid if there is a dispute.

**Examples from the textbook**: street vendors/hawkers (e.g., a person selling vada pav or other snacks from a cart), domestic workers (who work in others' homes), construction site workers, rickshaw pullers, small self-employed workers like cobblers.

## Case Studies from the Chapter (Typical Examples)
The textbook illustrates this difference through stories, such as:
- A **factory worker with a permanent job**, who gets a fixed monthly salary, provident fund (savings for retirement), and other benefits — representing the **organised sector**.
- A **daily-wage construction worker**, who is hired only when there is work available, and has no guarantee of work or pay the next day — representing the **unorganised sector**.
- A **street food vendor** (like someone selling vada pav) who is self-employed, works long hours, but has no fixed income and no protection if business is slow, or if authorities remove their cart from a particular spot.

## Struggles Faced by Unorganised Sector Workers
- **Uncertain and irregular income** — some days good earnings, some days very little or none.
- **Long working hours**, often without breaks or fixed timings.
- **No safety net** — no paid sick leave, no pension, no medical insurance through the job.
- **Vulnerability** — street vendors can be asked to move by authorities; workers can be underpaid or not paid at all in disputes; no legal protection like organised sector workers have.
- Rising prices (inflation) affect them severely since their income does not automatically increase to match rising costs.

## A Closer Look: Protecting Street Vendors — the Street Vendors Act, 2014
Street vendors are one of the biggest and most visible groups in the unorganised sector — India has close to **one crore (10 million) street vendors**, selling everything from vegetables and snacks to clothes and toys, mostly in cities. For decades, they had no legal protection at all: municipal staff or police could confiscate their goods or force them to move on at any time, even though vending was often their only way of earning a living.

To fix this, the government passed the **Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014.** This is an important example of a law made specifically to protect an unorganised-sector livelihood. Its key features:
- **Town Vending Committee (TVC):** Every city's Municipal Corporation must set up a TVC — a committee that includes government officials, municipal officers, and **street vendors themselves** — to survey and register all vendors in the city, at least once every five years.
- **Certificate of Vending:** Once registered, a vendor (who must be at least 14 years old) is given a **Certificate of Vending** — official legal proof of their right to vend at a particular spot.
- **Protection from sudden eviction:** A registered vendor cannot be moved from their spot or have their goods confiscated **without proper notice** (at least 30 days), and cannot be evicted at all until the survey and registration process is complete.
- **Fair allocation of space:** If a vending zone cannot fit all the vendors who want space there, spots are given out by a fair method (such as a draw of lots), and those who miss out are given space in a nearby zone instead.

This law shows how the government can step in to bring some of the security of the organised sector — legal recognition, protection from arbitrary action — into a part of the unorganised sector, without taking away a vendor's independence as a self-employed worker.

## Both Sectors are Important and Connected
- A city cannot function without **both** kinds of workers. Organised sector workers (like factory employees, office staff) rely on unorganised sector workers (like street food vendors, domestic help, delivery workers) for daily needs — and vice versa.
- Even though unorganised sector jobs often look "small," they form a **huge part** of a city's economy and workforce.
- The chapter encourages us to see the **dignity in all kinds of work**, whether it is a big office job or a small roadside stall — every job contributes to keeping the city running.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Livelihood | The way a person earns money to live |
| Organised Sector | Jobs with fixed salary, job security, and benefits (e.g., government jobs, registered company jobs) |
| Unorganised (Informal) Sector | Jobs with no fixed employer, no job security, and no guaranteed wages or benefits |
| Self-employed | A person who works for themselves rather than for an employer (e.g., a street vendor) |
| Job Security | The assurance that a person will not easily lose their job |
| Pension | Money paid regularly to a person after they retire from work |
| Street Vendors Act, 2014 | The law protecting street vendors' right to livelihood through registration and a Certificate of Vending |
| Town Vending Committee | The committee (with officials and vendors) that registers street vendors and issues vending certificates |
| Certificate of Vending | Legal proof that a registered vendor has the right to sell at a specific spot |

## Quick Recap (6 points to remember)
1. Urban livelihoods are grouped into two categories: organised sector and unorganised (informal) sector.
2. Organised sector jobs have fixed salaries, job security, and benefits (e.g., factory workers with contracts, government employees).
3. Unorganised sector jobs have no job security, no fixed wages, and no benefits (e.g., street vendors, domestic workers, daily-wage construction workers).
4. Unorganised sector workers face uncertain income, long hours, no safety net, and vulnerability (like eviction or non-payment).
5. The **Street Vendors Act, 2014** protects vendors through a Town Vending Committee and a Certificate of Vending, preventing arbitrary eviction and giving them legal recognition.
6. Both sectors are essential and interconnected — a city cannot run without either, and every kind of work deserves dignity and respect.
