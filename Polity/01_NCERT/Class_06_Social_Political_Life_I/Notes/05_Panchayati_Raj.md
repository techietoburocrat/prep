# Chapter 5: Panchayati Raj

## The Big Idea (in one line)
**Panchayati Raj** is the system of self-government in villages, where people elect their own local leaders to manage local matters like water, roads, and schools.

## Why do villages need their own government?
The central and state governments cannot personally handle every small issue in every village — like a broken streetlight or a dirty pond. So India has a system where **villages govern many of their own local affairs**, through elected local bodies. This system is called **Panchayati Raj** ("Panchayat" = village council, "Raj" = rule/governance).

## The Three-Tier (Three-Level) Structure
Panchayati Raj works at **three levels**, like a pyramid:

```
           ZILA PARISHAD (District level)
                    |
          PANCHAYAT SAMITI (Block level)
                    |
           GRAM PANCHAYAT (Village level)
```

### 1. Gram Panchayat (Village Level)
- Covers one village or a group of small nearby villages.
- The village is divided into **wards**; each ward elects one member called a **Ward Panch** (or Panchayat member).
- All the Ward Panchs together form the Gram Panchayat.
- The head of the Gram Panchayat is called the **Sarpanch** (in some states also called Panchayat President/Pradhan), who is elected.
- The Gram Panchayat has a **Secretary**, who is not elected but appointed by the government, to help with paperwork and implementation.

### 2. Panchayat Samiti (Block Level)
- A "block" is made up of several Gram Panchayats together.
- The Panchayat Samiti looks after matters that involve more than one village — for example, coordinating development work across several villages in the block.
- Its administrative head — the officer who helps implement the Samiti's decisions and coordinates government schemes at the block level — is called the **Block Development Officer (BDO)**, appointed by the government (not elected).

### 3. Zila Parishad (District Level)
- This is the top level, covering the whole district (many blocks together).
- Members of the Zila Parishad include representatives from the Panchayat Samitis, along with MLAs (Members of the Legislative Assembly) and MPs (Members of Parliament) of that district.
- The Zila Parishad looks at bigger issues affecting the entire district and coordinates the work of all the Panchayat Samitis under it.

## The Gram Sabha — the most important meeting
- The **Gram Sabha** is a meeting of **all the adult members (voters)** of the village — not just the elected representatives.
- It is considered the most powerful and important body in the Panchayati Raj system because it keeps the Gram Panchayat **accountable** to the people.
- In the Gram Sabha meeting:
  - The Gram Panchayat presents its budget (how much money it has and how it plans to spend it).
  - Villagers can ask questions, raise complaints, and suggest new development work.
  - Past work and future plans are discussed openly.
- This ensures that ordinary villagers — not just the elected leaders — have a direct say in how their village is run.

## The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992)
This is an important fact to remember:
- In 1992, the Indian Constitution was amended (changed) to give **constitutional status** to Panchayati Raj — meaning it became a permanent, legally guaranteed part of India's system of government, not just an optional idea. This amendment came into force on **24 April 1993**, and it added a whole new part to the Constitution — **Part IX** (Articles 243 to 243-O) — dealing entirely with Panchayats.
- This amendment made it compulsory to:
  - Hold **Panchayat elections regularly, every 5 years**. If a Panchayat is dissolved early, fresh elections must be held within 6 months.
  - **Reserve seats for women** — at least one-third (1/3) of all seats and leadership positions (like Sarpanch) must be held by women.
  - **Reserve seats for Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST)**, in proportion to their population, so that these communities are properly represented in local government.
  - Set up a **State Election Commission** in every state to independently conduct Panchayat elections, and a **State Finance Commission** every 5 years to decide how money should be shared between the state government and the Panchayats.

### The Eleventh Schedule — what subjects can Panchayats handle?
The 73rd Amendment also added a brand-new **Eleventh Schedule** to the Constitution. This Schedule is simply a **list of 29 subjects** (areas of work) that state governments can hand over to Panchayats to plan and manage, so that local matters are decided locally instead of always from the state capital.

Some important subjects on this list include:
- Agriculture, land improvement, and soil conservation
- Minor irrigation, water management, and watershed development
- Animal husbandry, dairying, and fisheries
- Rural housing and drinking water
- Roads, culverts, and other means of communication
- Rural electrification
- Poverty alleviation programmes
- Education, including primary and secondary schools
- Family welfare and health, including hospitals and primary health centres
- Women and child development
- Welfare of the weaker sections, including Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes
- Public distribution system (ration shops)
- Maintenance of community assets

Not every Panchayat handles all 29 subjects — it depends on how much a particular state government has actually **devolved (handed down)** to its Panchayats. The more subjects and funds a state gives its Panchayats, the more real power local communities have to plan their own development.

### District Planning Committee
The 73rd Amendment also created a **District Planning Committee** in every district. Its job is to collect the development plans made by all the Panchayats and Municipalities in the district and combine them into one single development plan for the whole district — making sure village-level and town-level planning fit together.

## What does the Gram Panchayat actually do?
Everyday responsibilities of a Gram Panchayat include:
- Maintaining sources of **drinking water** (wells, hand-pumps, tanks).
- Maintaining village **roads, drains, and streetlights**.
- Helping run or maintain the local **primary school**.
- Ensuring basic **sanitation** and cleanliness in the village.
- Taking care of other small public facilities used by villagers.

### Where does the Gram Panchayat get its money from?
- Grants (money given) by the state government.
- Some local taxes collected within the village.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Simple Meaning |
|---|---|
| Panchayati Raj | The system of village self-government in India |
| Gram Panchayat | The elected village-level governing body |
| Ward | A small division of a village, which elects one representative |
| Sarpanch | The elected head of the Gram Panchayat |
| Panchayat Samiti | The block-level (group of villages) governing body |
| Block Development Officer (BDO) | The appointed (not elected) officer who heads the administration of a Panchayat Samiti and helps implement its decisions |
| Zila Parishad | The district-level governing body |
| Gram Sabha | The meeting of all adult voters of a village; keeps the Panchayat accountable |
| 73rd Constitutional Amendment | The 1992 law that gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj |
| Eleventh Schedule | The list of 29 subjects (added by the 73rd Amendment) that state governments can hand over to Panchayats to plan and manage |
| Devolve | To hand down power, subjects, or funds from a higher level of government to a lower one (e.g., from the state to the Panchayat) |
| District Planning Committee | A body in every district that combines the development plans of all Panchayats and Municipalities into one district-level plan |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. Panchayati Raj is the three-tier system of village self-government: Gram Panchayat (village) → Panchayat Samiti (block) → Zila Parishad (district).
2. The Gram Panchayat is headed by an elected Sarpanch and made up of Ward Panchs elected from each ward.
3. The Gram Sabha (meeting of all adult villagers) is the most important body — it keeps the Panchayat accountable by discussing its budget and work.
4. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment (1992, in force from 24 April 1993) gave Panchayati Raj constitutional status, mandated elections every 5 years, and reserved seats for women and SC/ST communities.
5. The amendment added the **Eleventh Schedule** — a list of 29 subjects (agriculture, health, education, rural housing, poverty alleviation, and more) that states can devolve to Panchayats.
6. A **District Planning Committee** combines the plans of all Panchayats and Municipalities in a district into one overall district plan.
7. Gram Panchayats manage everyday village needs: drinking water, roads, streetlights, sanitation, and primary schools.
