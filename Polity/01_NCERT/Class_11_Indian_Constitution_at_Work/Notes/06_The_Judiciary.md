# Chapter 6: Judiciary

## 🎯 Chapter Outcome — what you should walk away with

> **The one thing this chapter exists to teach:** judicial independence is **not** the absence of accountability. The judiciary is free of restraint, interference and fear or favour — and is answerable to the Constitution, to democratic traditions and to the people.

**After reading it, here is what you now understand:**

1. **The six safeguards of independence**, and the design behind them: the **executive appoints** and the **legislature removes**, so neither controls a judge alone.
2. **How the collegium evolved between 1982 and 1998** — from "consultation" to advice that must be followed, exercised by the CJI with the four senior-most judges — and that the seniority convention for appointing the CJI was broken twice (A.N. Ray in 1973 over three seniors, M.H. Beg in 1975 over H.R. Khanna).
3. **What a special majority really means, through the Ramaswami case (1991–93).** The motion cleared two-thirds of those present and voting — but the Congress abstained, so it never reached a majority of the **total strength** of the House. He survived. **No Supreme Court judge has ever been removed.**
4. **That India has a single integrated judiciary** — there are **no separate State courts**, which distinguishes India from federations running parallel systems, and lets the Supreme Court transfer judges and move cases between High Courts.
5. **The five jurisdictions**, and the detail that matters most: **advisory jurisdiction is optional at both ends** — the Court need not advise, and the President need not accept the advice.
6. **PIL from 1979** — *Hussainara Khatoon* (undertrials jailed longer than their maximum sentence), *Sunil Batra*, *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* — and Bhagwati's reason for relaxing procedure: the problems of the poor are **"qualitatively different"**, and adversarial procedure would leave them unable to enforce their rights at all. You also know the *costs* — overburdened courts and a blurred separation of powers.
7. **That judicial review is nowhere written in the Constitution.** It is implicit in having a written constitution plus the power to strike down laws (Arts 13, 32, 226), and rests on two grounds: violation of Fundamental Rights, and violation of the federal distribution of powers.
8. **Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and its three effects** — the amending power is limited by basic structure; any part may be amended within that limit; and **the Court decides what basic structure is**.

*"Many times, courts are seen only as **arbitrators in disputes between individuals or private parties.** But **judiciary performs some POLITICAL functions also.**"*

⭐⭐ **"The Supreme Court of India is in fact ONE OF THE VERY POWERFUL COURTS IN THE WORLD. Right from 1950 the judiciary has played an important role in INTERPRETING and in PROTECTING the Constitution."**

**Four things this chapter delivers:** the meaning of **independence of the judiciary**; the judiciary's role in **protecting our rights**; its role in **interpreting the Constitution**; and the **relationship between the Judiciary and Parliament.**

## PART 1 — WHY DO WE NEED AN INDEPENDENT JUDICIARY?

### The rule of law
*"In any society, disputes are bound to arise **between individuals, between groups, and between individuals or groups and government.** All such disputes must be settled by an **independent body** in accordance with the principle of **RULE OF LAW.**"*

⭐⭐ **Definition to memorise:** *"This idea of rule of law implies that **ALL individuals — rich and poor, men or women, forward or backward castes — are subjected to the SAME LAW.**"*

### ⭐ The four roles of the judiciary
1. **"The principal role of the judiciary is to PROTECT RULE OF LAW and ENSURE SUPREMACY OF LAW."**
2. **"It SAFEGUARDS RIGHTS of the individual."**
3. **"It SETTLES DISPUTES in accordance with the law."**
4. ⭐ **"It ensures that DEMOCRACY DOES NOT GIVE WAY TO INDIVIDUAL OR GROUP DICTATORSHIP."**

⭐ **"In order to be able to do all this, it is necessary that the judiciary is INDEPENDENT of any political pressures."**

### ⭐⭐ Independence of Judiciary — the three-part definition
*"Simply stated, independence of judiciary means that:"*
1. **"The other organs of the government like the executive and legislature MUST NOT RESTRAIN THE FUNCTIONING of the judiciary in such a way that it is UNABLE TO DO JUSTICE."**
2. **"The other organs of the government should NOT INTERFERE WITH THE DECISION of the judiciary."**
3. ⭐ **"Judges must be able to PERFORM THEIR FUNCTIONS WITHOUT FEAR OR FAVOUR."**

### ⭐⭐ The crucial qualification — independence is NOT immunity
> **"Independence of the judiciary does NOT imply ARBITRARINESS or ABSENCE OF ACCOUNTABILITY. Judiciary is a part of the democratic political structure of the country. It is therefore ACCOUNTABLE to (i) the CONSTITUTION, (ii) the DEMOCRATIC TRADITIONS and (iii) the PEOPLE of the country."**

*(This is the direct answer to Exercise Q2 — learn the three objects of accountability.)*

### ⭐⭐ The SIX constitutional measures securing judicial independence
| # | Measure | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐ **The legislature is not involved in the appointment of judges** | *"Thus, it was believed that **party politics would not play a role** in the process of appointments."* |
| **2** | ⭐ **Merit-based qualification** | *"In order to be appointed as a judge, a person must have **experience as a lawyer and/or must be well versed in law.** **Political opinions of the person or his/her political loyalty should NOT be the criteria** for appointments to judiciary."* |
| **3** | ⭐⭐ **Security of tenure** | *"The judges have a **fixed tenure. They hold office till reaching the age of retirement.** Only in exceptional cases may judges be removed. **Security of tenure ensures that judges could function without fear or favour.**"* |
| **4** | ⭐⭐ **A very difficult removal procedure** | *"The Constitution prescribes a **very difficult procedure for removal** of judges. The Constitution makers believed that **a difficult procedure of removal would provide security of office** to the members of judiciary."* |
| **5** | ⭐⭐ **Financial independence** | *"The judiciary is **NOT financially dependent** on either the executive or legislature. The Constitution provides that **the salaries and allowances of the judges are NOT subjected to the approval of the legislature.**"* |
| **6** | ⭐⭐ **Immunity from criticism — contempt of court + no parliamentary discussion** | *"The actions and decisions of the judges are **immune from personal criticisms.** The judiciary has the power to **penalise those who are found guilty of CONTEMPT OF COURT.** This authority of the court is seen as an **effective protection to the judges from unfair criticism.** ⭐ **Parliament CANNOT discuss the conduct of the judges EXCEPT when the proceeding to remove a judge is being carried out.** This gives the judiciary independence to adjudicate **without fear of being criticised.**"* |

**Mnemonic for the six: "No-Legislature / Merit / Tenure / Hard-Removal / Money / Contempt."**

> *The chapter voices the student's puzzlement, which is worth thinking through:* *"In a democracy, you can criticise the Prime Minister or even the President, but **not the judges!** And what is this contempt of court? **But am I being guilty of contempt if I asked about these matters?**"*

## ⭐⭐ PART 2 — APPOINTMENT OF JUDGES

### Why appointment is inherently political
⭐ **"The appointment of judges has NEVER been free from political controversy. IT IS PART OF THE POLITICAL PROCESS."**

⭐⭐ **Why it matters so much:** *"**It makes a difference who serves in the Supreme Court and High Court — a difference in HOW THE CONSTITUTION IS INTERPRETED.** The **political philosophy** of the judges, their views about an **ACTIVE AND ASSERTIVE judiciary** or a **CONTROLLED AND COMMITTED judiciary**, have an impact on **the fate of the legislations enacted.**"*

⭐ **Who influences appointments (four actors):** *"**Council of Ministers, Governors and Chief Ministers, and the Chief Justice of India — ALL influence the process of judicial appointment.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Appointment of the Chief Justice of India — the convention and its two breaches
- **"As far as the appointment of the Chief Justice of India (CJI) is concerned, over the years a CONVENTION had developed whereby the SENIOR-MOST JUDGE of the Supreme Court was appointed as the Chief Justice of India."**
- ⭐⭐ **"This convention was however BROKEN TWICE:"**
  1. ⭐ **"In 1973, A.N. Ray was appointed as CJI SUPERSEDING THREE SENIOR JUDGES."**
  2. ⭐ **"Again, Justice M.H. Beg was appointed SUPERSEDING Justice H.R. Khanna (1975)."**

*(Both supersessions belong to the Emergency-era conflict between the executive and the judiciary — link this to the Kesavananda Bharati and ADM Jabalpur context in Part 6 and in Class 12's chapter on the Emergency.)*

### ⭐⭐ The evolution of "consultation" → the COLLEGIUM (1982–1998)
**The starting position:** *"The other Judges of the Supreme Court and the High Court are appointed by the **President** after **'CONSULTING' the CJI.** This, in effect, meant that **the final decisions in matters of appointment rested with the COUNCIL OF MINISTERS.** What then was the status of the consultation with the Chief Justice?"*

⭐⭐ **"This matter came up before the Supreme Court AGAIN AND AGAIN BETWEEN 1982 AND 1998."** Three successive positions:

| Stage | The Court's view |
|---|---|
| **Initially** | ⭐ **"The role of the Chief Justice was PURELY CONSULTATIVE."** |
| **Then** | ⭐ **"The OPINION of the Chief Justice MUST BE FOLLOWED by the President."** |
| ⭐⭐ **Finally** | **"The Supreme Court has come up with a NOVEL PROCEDURE: it has suggested that the CHIEF JUSTICE SHOULD RECOMMEND NAMES of persons to be appointed IN CONSULTATION WITH THE FOUR SENIOR-MOST JUDGES of the Court."** |

⭐⭐ **"Thus, the Supreme Court has established the PRINCIPLE OF COLLEGIALITY in making recommendations for appointments."**

⭐ **The present position:** *"At the moment therefore, in matters of appointment **the decision of the group of senior judges of the Supreme Court carries GREATER WEIGHT.** Thus, in matters of appointment to the judiciary, **the Supreme Court AND the Council of Ministers play an important role.**"*

*(So the answer to the chapter's own question — *"is the judiciary a self-appointing body?"* — is: **effectively the senior judges' recommendation carries greater weight, but both the Court and the Council of Ministers have a role.**)*

## ⭐⭐ PART 3 — REMOVAL OF JUDGES

### The procedure
- ⭐ **"The removal of judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts is also EXTREMELY DIFFICULT."**
- ⭐⭐ **The sole grounds:** *"A judge of the Supreme Court or High Court can be removed **ONLY on the ground of PROVEN MISBEHAVIOUR or INCAPACITY.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The procedure:** *"A **motion containing the charges** against the judge must be **approved by SPECIAL MAJORITY in BOTH Houses of the Parliament.**"* *(Special majority = **two-thirds of those present and voting** PLUS **a simple majority of the total membership** — see Ch. 3.)*
- ⭐ **"It is clear that removal of a judge is a very difficult procedure and UNLESS THERE IS A GENERAL CONSENSUS AMONG MEMBERS OF PARLIAMENT, A JUDGE CANNOT BE REMOVED."**

### ⭐⭐ The elegant balance the Constitution strikes (a very quotable point)
> **"It should also be noted that while in making APPOINTMENTS, the EXECUTIVE plays a crucial role; the LEGISLATURE has the powers of REMOVAL. THIS HAS ENSURED BOTH BALANCE OF POWER AND INDEPENDENCE OF THE JUDICIARY."**

### ⭐⭐ Box: The Justice V. Ramaswami case (1991–93) — the only removal attempt ever
- ⭐ **"So far, ONLY ONE case of removal of a judge of the Supreme Court came up for consideration before Parliament."**
- ⭐ **"In 1991 the FIRST-EVER motion to remove a Supreme Court Justice was signed by 108 MEMBERS OF PARLIAMENT."**
- **The charge:** *"**Justice V. Ramaswami**, during his tenure as the **Chief Justice of the Punjab and Haryana High Court**, was accused of **misappropriating funds.**"*
- ⭐ **The inquiry:** *"In **1992**, a year after Parliament had started the removal proceedings, a **high-profile inquiry commission consisting of Judges of the Supreme Court** found Justice V. Ramaswami **"guilty of wilful and gross misuses of office . . . and MORAL TURPITUDE by using public funds for private purposes and reckless disregard of statutory rules"** while serving as Chief Justice of the Punjab and Haryana High Court.*
- ⭐⭐ **The outcome — and the arithmetic that saved him:** *"**Despite this strong indictment, Ramaswami SURVIVED the parliamentary motion.** The motion recommending his removal **GOT the required two-thirds majority among the members who were PRESENT AND VOTING**, ⭐ **but the CONGRESS PARTY ABSTAINED from voting in the House. Therefore, the motion could NOT get the support of ONE-HALF OF THE TOTAL STRENGTH of the House.**"*

> **⭐ Why this box is gold for exams:** it is the **only real-world demonstration of what "special majority" actually means.** The motion cleared the **first** condition (2/3 present and voting) but failed the **second** (majority of total membership) — because of **abstention.** Learn this as the definitive illustration.

## ⭐⭐ PART 4 — STRUCTURE OF THE JUDICIARY

### The single integrated judicial system
⭐⭐ **"The Constitution of India provides for a SINGLE INTEGRATED JUDICIAL SYSTEM. This means that UNLIKE SOME OTHER FEDERAL COUNTRIES of the world, INDIA DOES NOT HAVE SEPARATE STATE COURTS."**

⭐ **"The structure of the judiciary in India is PYRAMIDAL, with the Supreme Court at the top, High Courts below them, and district and subordinate courts at the lowest level. THE LOWER COURTS FUNCTION UNDER THE DIRECT SUPERINTENDENCE OF THE HIGHER COURTS."**

*(This is a key point for the **federalism** chapter too: a single integrated judiciary is one of the features that makes India **quasi-federal** rather than fully federal — contrast the USA, which has parallel state and federal court systems.)*

### ⭐⭐ The four tiers and what each does
| Court | Powers as listed in the chapter |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Supreme Court of India** | • **"Its decisions are BINDING on ALL courts"**<br>• **"Can TRANSFER Judges of High Courts"**<br>• **"Can MOVE CASES from any court to ITSELF"**<br>• **"Can TRANSFER cases from one High Court to another"** |
| ⭐ **High Court** | • **"Can hear APPEALS from lower courts"**<br>• **"Can ISSUE WRITS for restoring Fundamental Rights"**<br>• **"Can deal with cases within the JURISDICTION OF THE STATE"**<br>• **"Exercises SUPERINTENDENCE AND CONTROL over courts below it"** |
| **District Court** | • **"Deals with cases arising in the District"**<br>• **"Considers APPEALS on decisions given by lower courts"**<br>• **"Decides cases involving SERIOUS CRIMINAL OFFENCES"** |
| **Subordinate Courts** | • **"Consider cases of CIVIL and CRIMINAL nature"** |

## ⭐⭐ PART 5 — JURISDICTION OF THE SUPREME COURT

*"The Supreme Court of India is **one of the very powerful courts anywhere in the world.** **However, it functions WITHIN THE LIMITATIONS IMPOSED BY THE CONSTITUTION.** The functions and responsibilities of the Supreme Court are **defined by the Constitution.**"*

### The five heads of jurisdiction (three main + writ + special)
| Jurisdiction | What it covers |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **ORIGINAL** | **"Settles disputes between Union and States, and amongst States"** |
| ⭐⭐ **APPELLATE** | **"Tries appeals from lower courts in Civil, Criminal and Constitutional cases"** |
| ⭐⭐ **ADVISORY** | **"Advises the President on matters of public importance and law"** |
| ⭐⭐ **WRIT** | **"Can issue writs of Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto to protect the Fundamental Rights of the individual"** |
| ⭐ **SPECIAL POWERS** | **"Can grant SPECIAL LEAVE to an appeal from ANY judgement or matter passed by ANY court in the territory of India"** |

### ⭐⭐ Original Jurisdiction — the Court as federal umpire
- ⭐ **Definition:** *"Original jurisdiction means **cases that can be DIRECTLY considered by the Supreme Court WITHOUT going to the lower courts** before that."*
- ⭐⭐ **"Cases involving FEDERAL RELATIONS go DIRECTLY to the Supreme Court. The Original Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court establishes it as an UMPIRE IN ALL DISPUTES REGARDING FEDERAL MATTERS."**
- **Why it exists:** *"In any federal country, legal disputes are bound to arise **between the Union and the States, and among the States themselves.** The power to resolve such cases is entrusted to the Supreme Court of India."*
- ⭐⭐ **Why it is called "original":** *"because **the Supreme Court ALONE has the power to deal with such cases. NEITHER the High Courts NOR the lower courts can deal with such cases.**"*
- ⭐ **The larger function:** *"In this capacity, the Supreme Court **not just settles disputes but also INTERPRETS THE POWERS of Union and State government as laid down in the Constitution.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Writ Jurisdiction
- **"Any individual whose fundamental right has been violated can DIRECTLY move the Supreme Court for remedy."**
- **"The Supreme Court can give special orders in the form of WRITS."**
- ⭐⭐ **The choice of forum:** *"**The High Courts can ALSO issue writs**, but the persons whose rights are violated have **the CHOICE of either approaching the High Court OR approaching the Supreme Court directly.**"*
- ⭐ **"Through such writs, the Court can give orders to the EXECUTIVE to ACT or NOT TO ACT in a particular way."**

### ⭐⭐ Appellate Jurisdiction
- **"The Supreme Court is the HIGHEST COURT OF APPEAL. A person can appeal to the Supreme Court against the decisions of the High Court."**
- ⭐⭐ **The certification requirement:** *"**However, the High Court MUST CERTIFY that the case is FIT FOR APPEAL** — that is to say, that **it involves a serious matter of INTERPRETATION OF LAW OR CONSTITUTION.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The death-sentence route:** *"In addition, **in criminal cases, if the lower court has sentenced a person to DEATH, then an appeal can be made to the High Court OR Supreme Court.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The override:** *"Of course, **the Supreme Court holds the powers to decide whether to ADMIT APPEALS EVEN WHEN APPEAL IS NOT ALLOWED BY THE HIGH COURT.**"* *(This is the **special leave** power.)*
- ⭐ **What appellate jurisdiction actually means:** *"the Supreme Court will **RECONSIDER the case AND the legal issues involved in it.** **If the Court thinks that the law or the Constitution has a DIFFERENT MEANING from what the lower courts understood, then the Supreme Court will CHANGE THE RULING and, along with that, also GIVE A NEW INTERPRETATION of the provision involved.**"*
- **"The High Courts too have appellate jurisdiction over the decisions given by courts below them."**

### ⭐⭐ Advisory Jurisdiction — the double optionality
- **"The PRESIDENT of India can refer any matter that is (i) of PUBLIC IMPORTANCE or (ii) that involves INTERPRETATION OF THE CONSTITUTION, to the Supreme Court for advice."**
- ⭐⭐ **The two-way non-binding rule (a favourite exam trap):** *"**However, the Supreme Court is NOT BOUND TO GIVE ADVICE on such matters, AND the President is NOT BOUND TO ACCEPT such advice.**"*

> *The chapter's own wry aside:* *"Isn't it funny that **giving advice is optional and accepting that advice is also optional?** I thought that the Courts gave decisions that were binding!"*

⭐⭐ **So what is the utility? "The utility is TWO-FOLD:"**
1. **"It allows the government to SEEK LEGAL OPINION on a matter of importance BEFORE TAKING ACTION on it. THIS MAY PREVENT UNNECESSARY LITIGATIONS LATER."**
2. **"In the light of the advice of the Supreme Court, the government can MAKE SUITABLE CHANGES IN ITS ACTION OR LEGISLATIONS."**

### ⭐⭐ Two Articles to memorise
> **Article 137:** *"........ the **Supreme Court shall have power to REVIEW any judgment pronounced or order made BY IT.**"*
>
> **Article 144:** *"...... **ALL AUTHORITIES, CIVIL AND JUDICIAL, in the territory of India shall ACT IN AID OF the Supreme Court.**"*

**What these two Articles establish (the "unified nature" of the judiciary):**
- ⭐ **"Decisions made by the Supreme Court are BINDING ON ALL OTHER COURTS within the territory of India."**
- ⭐ **"Orders passed by it are ENFORCEABLE THROUGHOUT THE LENGTH AND BREADTH of the country."**
- ⭐⭐ **"The Supreme Court ITSELF IS NOT BOUND BY ITS OWN DECISION and CAN AT ANY TIME REVIEW IT."** (Art. 137)
- ⭐ **"Besides, if there is a case of CONTEMPT of the Supreme Court, then THE SUPREME COURT ITSELF DECIDES such a case."**

## ⭐⭐ PART 6 — JUDICIAL ACTIVISM

### ⭐⭐ Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — the origin story
⭐ **"The CHIEF INSTRUMENT through which judicial activism has flourished in India is PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION (PIL) or SOCIAL ACTION LITIGATION (SAL)."**

**The old rule:** *"In the normal course of law, **an individual can approach the courts ONLY IF HE/SHE HAS BEEN PERSONALLY AGGRIEVED.** That is to say, a person **whose rights have been violated, or who is involved in a dispute**, could move the court of law."*

⭐⭐ **The change:** *"This concept underwent a change **around 1979. In 1979, the Court set the trend when it decided to HEAR A CASE WHERE THE CASE WAS FILED NOT BY THE AGGRIEVED PERSONS BUT BY OTHERS ON THEIR BEHALF.** As this case involved a consideration of **an issue of PUBLIC INTEREST**, it and such other cases came to be known as **public interest litigations.**"*

⭐ **"Around the same time, the Supreme Court also took up the case about RIGHTS OF PRISONERS."**

⭐⭐ **What this opened up (four areas):** *"This opened the gates for a large number of cases where **public spirited citizens and voluntary organisations** sought judicial intervention for:*
1. **protection of existing rights**
2. **betterment of life conditions of the poor**
3. **protection of the environment**
4. **and many other issues in the interest of the public."**

⭐⭐ **"PIL has become the MOST IMPORTANT VEHICLE OF JUDICIAL ACTIVISM."**

### ⭐ Why it is called "judicial activism"
*"Judiciary, which is an institution that traditionally **confined itself to responding to cases brought before it**, began considering **many cases MERELY ON THE BASIS OF NEWSPAPER REPORTS AND POSTAL COMPLAINTS received by the court.** **Therefore, the term JUDICIAL ACTIVISM became the more popular description of the role of the judiciary.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Box: Some Early PILs (learn both cases with names, years and facts)

**➊ *Hussainara Khatoon vs. Bihar* (1979) — the undertrials case**
- **"In 1979, newspapers published reports about 'UNDER TRIALS'."**
- ⭐ **"There were many prisoners in BIHAR who had spent LONG YEARS IN JAIL — LONGER THAN WHAT THEY WOULD HAVE SPENT IF THEY HAD BEEN PUNISHED for the offences for which they were arrested."**
- **"This report prompted an ADVOCATE to file a petition. The Supreme Court heard this case."**
- ⭐ **"It became famous as ONE OF THE EARLY PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATIONS (PILs). This was the *Hussainara Khatoon vs. Bihar* case."**

**➋ *Sunil Batra vs. Delhi Administration* (1980) — the letter-as-petition case**
- ⭐ **"In 1980, a prison inmate of the TIHAR JAIL managed to send a SCRIBBLED PIECE OF PAPER to JUSTICE KRISHNA IYER of the Supreme Court narrating PHYSICAL TORTURE of the prisoners."**
- ⭐⭐ **"THE JUDGE GOT IT CONVERTED INTO A PETITION."**
- ⭐ **"Though LATER ON, THE COURT ABANDONED THE PRACTICE OF CONSIDERING LETTERS, this case, known as *Sunil Batra vs. Delhi Administration* (1980), also became one of the PIONEERS of public interest litigation."**

### ⭐⭐ The two great achievements of PIL
**➊ It expanded the very idea of rights.**
> **"Through the PIL, the court has EXPANDED THE IDEA OF RIGHTS. CLEAN AIR, UNPOLLUTED WATER, DECENT LIVING, etc., are RIGHTS FOR THE ENTIRE SOCIETY. Therefore, it was felt by the courts that INDIVIDUALS AS PARTS OF THE SOCIETY MUST HAVE THE RIGHT TO SEEK JUSTICE WHEREVER SUCH RIGHTS WERE VIOLATED."**

**➋ It reached those who cannot reach the courts.**
> **"Through PIL and judicial activism of the POST-1980 period, the judiciary has also shown readiness to TAKE INTO CONSIDERATION RIGHTS OF THOSE SECTIONS WHO CANNOT EASILY APPROACH THE COURTS. For this purpose, the judiciary ALLOWED PUBLIC SPIRITED CITIZENS, SOCIAL ORGANISATIONS AND LAWYERS TO FILE PETITIONS ON BEHALF OF THE NEEDY AND THE DEPRIVED."**

> ### ⭐⭐ Justice Bhagwati's justification — the definitive quotation
> *"It must be remembered that **the problems of the poor ...are QUALITATIVELY DIFFERENT from those which have hitherto occupied the attention of the Court** and they need ....**a DIFFERENT KIND OF JUDICIAL APPROACH. If we blindly follow the ADVERSARIAL PROCEDURE in their case, THEY WOULD NEVER BE ABLE TO ENFORCE THEIR FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS.**"*
> — **Justice Bhagwati in *Bandhua Mukti Morcha vs. Union of India*, 1984**

### ⭐⭐ The FOUR positive impacts of judicial activism
*"Judicial activism has had manifold impact on the political system:"*
1. ⭐ **"It has DEMOCRATISED THE JUDICIAL SYSTEM by giving not just to individuals but also GROUPS access to the courts."**
2. ⭐ **"It has FORCED EXECUTIVE ACCOUNTABILITY."**
3. ⭐ **"It has also made an attempt to make the ELECTORAL SYSTEM much more free and fair."**
4. ⭐⭐ **The concrete example:** *"**The court asked candidates contesting elections to FILE AFFIDAVITS INDICATING THEIR ASSETS AND INCOME ALONG WITH EDUCATIONAL QUALIFICATIONS, so that the people could elect their representatives based on ACCURATE KNOWLEDGE.**"*
*(A fifth impact mentioned elsewhere in the chapter: **the judiciary has ruled that *bandhs* and *hartals* are illegal.**)*

### ⭐⭐ The THREE negative sides of a proactive judiciary
*"There is however a negative side to the large number of PILs and the idea of a proactive judiciary:"*
1. ⭐ **"In the first place it has OVERBURDENED THE COURTS."**
2. ⭐⭐ **"Secondly, judicial activism has BLURRED THE LINE OF DISTINCTION between the EXECUTIVE and LEGISLATURE on the one hand and the JUDICIARY on the other. The court has been involved in resolving questions WHICH BELONG TO THE EXECUTIVE."**
   - ⭐ **The examples given:** *"Thus, for instance, **reducing air or sound pollution**, or **investigating cases of corruption**, or **bringing about electoral reform**, is **NOT EXACTLY THE DUTY OF THE JUDICIARY. These are matters to be handled by the ADMINISTRATION under the supervision of the LEGISLATURES.**"*
3. ⭐⭐ **"Therefore, some people feel that judicial activism has made THE BALANCE AMONG THE THREE ORGANS OF GOVERNMENT VERY DELICATE. Democratic government is based on EACH ORGAN OF GOVERNMENT RESPECTING THE POWERS AND JURISDICTION OF THE OTHERS. JUDICIAL ACTIVISM MAY BE CREATING STRAINS ON THIS DEMOCRATIC PRINCIPLE."**

> *The chapter's sharp aside:* *"I think judicial activism is more about **telling the legislature and the executive what they should do. What will happen if the legislature and executive started giving justice?**"* And: *"I have heard someone say that **PIL means 'private interest litigation'. Why would that be so?"***

### ⭐⭐ Box: "You are the judge" — the slum-clearance PIL (an excellent essay prompt)
*A group of citizens from a city approach the court through a **PIL asking for an order to the city municipal authorities to REMOVE SLUMS AND BEAUTIFY THE CITY in order to ATTRACT INVESTORS.** They argue **this is in the "public interest."** **The residents of the slum localities respond that this will ENCROACH ON THEIR RIGHT TO LIFE.** They argue that **the right to life is MORE CENTRAL to "public interest" than the right to a clean city.***

**How to reason about it (a model structure):**
- **On "public interest":** PIL exists precisely to reach *"those sections who cannot easily approach the courts"* and to better *"the life conditions of the poor"* — **so a petition by the comfortable to displace the poor inverts the doctrine's very purpose.** This is the *"private interest litigation"* critique made concrete.
- **On the rights hierarchy:** the Supreme Court has read into **Article 21** the **right to live with human dignity, the right to shelter and the right to livelihood** (Ch. 2). A **clean and attractive city** is a legitimate public good, but **investor attraction is not a fundamental right.**
- **The likely balanced order:** the environmental/planning objective may be pursued **only with prior rehabilitation and resettlement**, not by summary eviction — the standard judicial resolution.

### ⭐ Box: PIL as a global export
> **"The practice of public interest litigation is now becoming more and more acceptable in many other countries. While many courts across the world — particularly in SOUTH ASIA and AFRICA — practise some form of judicial activism comparable to that of the Indian judiciary, ⭐⭐ THE CONSTITUTION OF SOUTH AFRICA HAS INCORPORATED PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION IN ITS BILL OF RIGHTS. Thus, in South Africa, IT IS A FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT OF THE CITIZEN TO BRING BEFORE THE CONSTITUTIONAL COURT CASES OF VIOLATION OF OTHER PERSONS' RIGHTS."**

*(A neat closure of the loop from Chapter 1: India's Constitution "became an exemplar for many other constitutions, most notably South Africa.")*

## ⭐⭐ PART 7 — JUDICIARY AND RIGHTS

### ⭐⭐ The two constitutional remedies (learn the Article numbers)
*"The Constitution provides TWO ways in which the Supreme Court can remedy the violation of rights:"*
| # | Remedy | Article |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐ **"It can RESTORE fundamental rights by ISSUING WRITS of Habeas Corpus, Mandamus etc."** | ⭐⭐ **Article 32** *(and the **High Courts** also have this power under ⭐⭐ **Article 226**)* |
| **2** | ⭐ **"The Supreme Court can DECLARE THE CONCERNED LAW AS UNCONSTITUTIONAL and therefore NON-OPERATIONAL."** | ⭐⭐ **Article 13** |

⭐⭐ **"Together these two provisions of the Constitution establish the Supreme Court as (i) the PROTECTOR OF FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS of the citizen on the one hand and (ii) INTERPRETER OF THE CONSTITUTION on the other."**

### ⭐⭐ Judicial Review — the most important power
⭐⭐ **Definition to memorise:** *"**Judicial Review means the power of the Supreme Court (or High Courts) to EXAMINE THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF ANY LAW.** If the Court arrives at the conclusion that the law is **inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution**, such a law is **declared as UNCONSTITUTIONAL and INAPPLICABLE.**"*

⭐⭐ **The point everyone misses — it is NOT in the text:** *"**The term judicial review is NOWHERE MENTIONED IN THE CONSTITUTION.** However, **the fact that (i) India has a WRITTEN constitution and (ii) the Supreme Court can strike down a law that goes against fundamental rights, IMPLICITLY gives the Supreme Court the power of judicial review.**"*

### ⭐⭐ The TWO grounds of judicial review
| Ground | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **1. Violation of Fundamental Rights** | Under **Article 13** — the law is declared unconstitutional and inapplicable |
| ⭐⭐ **2. Violation of the FEDERAL DISTRIBUTION OF POWERS** | *"In the case of federal relations too, the Supreme Court can use the review powers **if a law is inconsistent with the DISTRIBUTION OF POWERS laid down by the Constitution.** ⭐ **The worked example:** "Suppose the **central government makes a law which, according to some States, concerns a subject from the STATE LIST. Then the States can go to the Supreme Court, and if the court agrees with them, it would declare that the law is unconstitutional.**"* |

⭐ **"The review power extends to the laws passed by STATE LEGISLATURES also."**

### ⭐⭐ Writ power + Review power = a very powerful judiciary
- **"Together, the writ powers and the review power of the Court make the judiciary VERY POWERFUL."**
- ⭐ **"In particular, the review power means that the judiciary CAN INTERPRET the Constitution and the laws passed by the legislature."**
- **"Many people think that this feature enables the judiciary to **protect the Constitution effectively** and also to **protect the rights of citizens.** ⭐ **The practice of entertaining PILs has FURTHER ADDED to the powers of the judiciary in protecting rights of citizens.**"*

### ⭐⭐ How PIL made the Right against Exploitation real (link back to Chapter 2)
*"This right prohibits **forced labour, trade in human flesh**, and prohibits **employment of children in hazardous jobs.** **But the question is: HOW COULD THOSE WHOSE RIGHTS WERE VIOLATED APPROACH THE COURT?** **PIL and judicial activism made it possible for courts to consider these violations.**"*

⭐ **"Thus, the court considered a whole set of cases:**
- **the BLINDING OF THE JAIL INMATES BY THE POLICE**
- **INHUMAN WORKING CONDITIONS IN STONE QUARRIES**
- **SEXUAL EXPLOITATION OF CHILDREN**, and so on.

⭐⭐ **"THIS TREND HAS MADE RIGHTS REALLY MEANINGFUL FOR THE POOR AND DISADVANTAGED SECTIONS."**

### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — two precise questions
1. **"When does the Court use the review powers?"** → **When a law is challenged as (i) violating Fundamental Rights, or (ii) violating the federal distribution of powers** between Union and States. It applies to **both central and State legislation.**
2. ⭐⭐ **"What is the difference between judicial review and a writ?"** →
   | **Writ (Art. 32 / 226)** | **Judicial Review (Art. 13)** |
   |---|---|
   | An **order directed at the executive/an authority** — to act or not act in a particular way | A **determination about a LAW** — declaring it unconstitutional and inapplicable |
   | Remedies a **specific violation** of a specific person's fundamental right | Strikes at the **source** — the statute itself |
   | Available in **both** the Supreme Court and High Courts | Available in **both**, but rests on the Constitution's written supremacy rather than an express provision |
   | Restores the right | Removes the offending law |

## ⭐⭐ PART 8 — JUDICIARY AND PARLIAMENT

### ⭐ Extending review into previously untouched areas
*"Apart from taking a very active stand on the matter of rights, the court has been active in seeking to **PREVENT SUBVERSION OF THE CONSTITUTION THROUGH POLITICAL PRACTICE.** ⭐⭐ **Thus, areas that were considered BEYOND THE SCOPE OF JUDICIAL REVIEW — such as the POWERS OF THE PRESIDENT AND GOVERNOR — WERE BROUGHT UNDER THE PURVIEW OF THE COURTS.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Directions to executive agencies (three named cases)
*"There are many other instances in which the Supreme Court **actively involved itself in the administration of justice by GIVING DIRECTIONS TO EXECUTIVE AGENCIES.** Thus, it gave **directions to the CBI to initiate investigations against POLITICIANS AND BUREAUCRATS** in:*
1. ⭐ **"the HAWALA case"**
2. ⭐ **"the NARASIMHA RAO case"**
3. ⭐ **"the ILLEGAL ALLOTMENT OF PETROL PUMPS case"**

**"Many of these instances are the products of JUDICIAL ACTIVISM."**

### ⭐⭐ Limited separation of powers and checks and balances
⭐⭐ **"The Indian Constitution is based on a DELICATE PRINCIPLE OF LIMITED SEPARATION OF POWERS AND CHECKS AND BALANCES. This means that EACH ORGAN OF THE GOVERNMENT HAS A CLEAR AREA OF FUNCTIONING:"**

| Organ | Where it is supreme |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Parliament** | **"Supreme in MAKING LAWS and AMENDING THE CONSTITUTION"** |
| ⭐ **The Executive** | **"Supreme in IMPLEMENTING them"** |
| ⭐ **The Judiciary** | **"Supreme in SETTLING DISPUTES and DECIDING WHETHER THE LAWS THAT HAVE BEEN MADE ARE IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE PROVISIONS OF THE CONSTITUTION"** |

⭐ **"Despite such clear-cut division of power, the CONFLICT between the Parliament and judiciary, and executive and the judiciary, has remained a RECURRENT THEME in Indian politics."**

### ⭐⭐ The property-rights conflict recapitulated (the classic sequence)
*"Immediately after the implementation of the Constitution began, a controversy arose over Parliament's power to restrict the right to property:"*
1. **"Parliament wanted to put some restrictions on the right to hold property SO THAT LAND REFORMS COULD BE IMPLEMENTED."**
2. **"The Court held that PARLIAMENT CANNOT THUS RESTRICT FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS."**
3. **"Parliament then tried to AMEND THE CONSTITUTION."**
4. ⭐ **"But the Court said that EVEN THROUGH AN AMENDMENT, A FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT CANNOT BE ABRIDGED."**

### ⭐⭐ The FOUR issues at the centre of the Parliament–judiciary controversy (memorise all four)
1. **"What is the SCOPE OF THE RIGHT TO PRIVATE PROPERTY?"**
2. **"What is the scope of Parliament's power to CURTAIL, ABRIDGE or ABROGATE FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS?"**
3. **"What is the scope of Parliament's power to AMEND THE CONSTITUTION?"**
4. ⭐ **"Can Parliament make laws that ABRIDGE FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS WHILE ENFORCING DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES?"**

### ⭐⭐ The period 1967–1973 — when the conflict became "very serious"
*"During the period **1967 and 1973**, this controversy became very serious."* **The flashpoints, apart from land reform laws:**
- ⭐ **"laws enforcing PREVENTIVE DETENTION"**
- ⭐ **"laws governing RESERVATIONS IN JOBS"**
- ⭐ **"regulations ACQUIRING PRIVATE PROPERTY FOR PUBLIC PURPOSES"**
- ⭐ **"laws deciding the COMPENSATION for such acquisition of private property"**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The *Kesavananda Bharati* case (1973) — the single most important judgment in this book
⭐ **"In 1973, the Supreme Court gave a decision that has become VERY IMPORTANT IN REGULATING THE RELATIONS BETWEEN THE PARLIAMENT AND THE JUDICIARY SINCE THEN. This case is famous as the *KESAVANANDA BHARATI* case."**

**The Court did THREE things (learn all three — most students remember only the first):**
1. ⭐⭐ **"The Court ruled that THERE IS A BASIC STRUCTURE OF THE CONSTITUTION and NOBODY — NOT EVEN THE PARLIAMENT (through amendment) — CAN VIOLATE THE BASIC STRUCTURE."**
2. ⭐⭐ **"First, it said that the RIGHT TO PROPERTY (the disputed issue) WAS NOT PART OF BASIC STRUCTURE and therefore COULD BE SUITABLY ABRIDGED."**
3. ⭐⭐ **"Secondly, THE COURT RESERVED TO ITSELF THE RIGHT TO DECIDE WHETHER VARIOUS MATTERS ARE PART OF THE BASIC STRUCTURE of the Constitution."** ← *this third holding is what makes the doctrine so powerful*

⭐⭐ **"This case is perhaps the BEST EXAMPLE OF HOW JUDICIARY USES ITS POWER TO INTERPRET THE CONSTITUTION."**

> *The chapter's own pointed question:* *"**Why can't the Court tell us once and for all what are those aspects that are 'basic structure' of the Constitution?**"* — **the answer, of course, is holding (3): keeping the list open is precisely the source of the Court's continuing power.**

### ⭐ How the conflict cooled
- **"This ruling has CHANGED THE NATURE of conflicts between the legislature and the judiciary."**
- ⭐ **"As we studied earlier, the RIGHT TO PROPERTY WAS TAKEN AWAY FROM THE LIST OF FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS IN 1979, and this also HELPED IN CHANGING THE NATURE OF THE RELATIONSHIP between these two organs of government."**

> **⚠️ A factual note worth being aware of:** this chapter says the right to property was removed **"in 1979"**; Chapter 2's box dates the **44th Amendment to 1978** (it was passed in 1978 and its property provisions came into force in **June 1979**). **In an exam, the 44th Amendment is dated 1978.** Both statements in the book refer to the same reform.

### ⭐⭐ The unresolved issues — potential future conflict
*"Some issues STILL REMAIN A BONE OF CONTENTION between the two — **can the judiciary intervene in and regulate the functioning of the legislatures?**"*
- **The legislature's position:** *"In the parliamentary system, **the legislature has the power to GOVERN ITSELF and REGULATE THE BEHAVIOUR OF ITS MEMBERS.** Thus, the legislature **can punish a person whom the legislature holds guilty of BREACHING PRIVILEGES** of the legislature."*
- ⭐ **The open questions:** *"**Can a person who is held guilty for breach of parliamentary privileges seek protection of the courts? Can a member of the legislature against whom the legislature has taken disciplinary action get protection from the court? THESE ISSUES ARE UNRESOLVED and are matters of POTENTIAL CONFLICT between the two.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The mutual criticism problem:** *"the Constitution provides that **the conduct of judges CANNOT be discussed in the Parliament.** **There have been several instances where the Parliament and State legislatures have CAST ASPERSIONS on the functioning of the judiciary.** Similarly, **the judiciary too has CRITICISED THE LEGISLATURES and ISSUED INSTRUCTIONS TO THE LEGISLATURES about the conduct of legislative business.** ⭐ **The legislatures see this as VIOLATING THE PRINCIPLE OF PARLIAMENTARY SOVEREIGNTY.**"*

⭐⭐ **"These issues indicate HOW DELICATE THE BALANCE BETWEEN ANY TWO ORGANS OF THE GOVERNMENT IS, and HOW IMPORTANT IT IS FOR EACH ORGAN OF THE GOVERNMENT IN A DEMOCRACY TO RESPECT THE AUTHORITY OF OTHERS."**

### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — the four main issues of Parliament–judiciary conflict
1. **Appointment of judges**
2. **Salaries and allowances of judges**
3. **Scope of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution**
4. **Interference by the Parliament in the functioning of the judiciary**

> ### ⭐⭐ Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar's warning — a superb balancing quotation
> *"While there can be no two opinions on the need for the maintenance of judicial independence, ...it is also necessary to keep in view one important principle. **The doctrine of independence is NOT TO BE RAISED TO THE LEVEL OF A DOGMA so as to enable the judiciary to function as a kind of SUPER-LEGISLATURE or SUPER-EXECUTIVE. The judiciary is there to INTERPRET THE CONSTITUTION or ADJUDICATE UPON THE RIGHTS...**"*
> — **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, CAD, Vol. XI, p. 837, 23 November 1949**
>

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Rule of law** | **All individuals — rich and poor, men or women, forward or backward castes — are subjected to the SAME law** |
| **Independence of judiciary** | Other organs must not **restrain** its functioning, must not **interfere** with its decisions, and judges must act **without fear or favour** — but it does **not** mean arbitrariness or absence of accountability |
| **Security of tenure** | Judges hold office **till the age of retirement**; removable only exceptionally — so they can function without fear or favour |
| **Contempt of court** | The court's power to **penalise** those guilty of it; seen as protection against unfair criticism |
| **Collegiality / collegium** | The Supreme Court's procedure whereby **the CJI recommends names in consultation with the four senior-most judges** |
| **Supersession** | Appointing a junior over senior judges — done **twice**: **A.N. Ray over three seniors (1973)** and **M.H. Beg over H.R. Khanna (1975)** |
| **Proven misbehaviour or incapacity** | The **only** grounds on which a judge of the Supreme Court or a High Court may be removed |
| **Single integrated judicial system** | India has **no separate State courts**; a pyramid with the Supreme Court at the apex, and lower courts under the **direct superintendence** of higher courts |
| **Original jurisdiction** | Cases the Supreme Court hears **directly**, chiefly **Union–State and inter-State disputes**; it alone can hear them — making it the **federal umpire** |
| **Appellate jurisdiction** | The Supreme Court as **highest court of appeal**; the High Court must normally **certify** the case as fit, but the SC may **admit an appeal even when the High Court refuses**; and **death sentences** may be appealed |
| **Advisory jurisdiction** | The President may refer a matter of **public importance** or of **constitutional interpretation** — but **the Court need not advise and the President need not accept** the advice |
| **Special leave** | The power to grant leave to appeal from **any judgement of any court** in India |
| **Article 32** | The right to move the **Supreme Court** for writs to enforce Fundamental Rights |
| **Article 226** | The **High Courts'** power to issue writs |
| **Article 13** | The basis for declaring a law **unconstitutional and non-operational** |
| **Article 137** | The Supreme Court's power to **review its own judgments** |
| **Article 144** | **All authorities, civil and judicial, shall act in aid of** the Supreme Court |
| **Judicial review** | The power to **examine the constitutionality of any law** and declare it unconstitutional and inapplicable — **nowhere mentioned in the Constitution**, but implicit in a **written** constitution plus the power to strike down laws violating Fundamental Rights |
| **Public Interest Litigation (PIL) / Social Action Litigation (SAL)** | Litigation filed **not by the aggrieved person but by others on their behalf**, on an issue of public interest — begun **around 1979**; **the most important vehicle of judicial activism** |
| **Judicial activism** | The judiciary going beyond responding to cases brought before it — taking up matters on **newspaper reports and postal complaints** |
| **Adversarial procedure** | The conventional two-party litigation model which, Justice Bhagwati argued, would leave the poor **never able to enforce their fundamental rights** |
| **Basic structure** | Features of the Constitution that **not even Parliament can violate by amendment** — laid down in ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**, with the Court **reserving to itself** the right to decide what belongs to it |
| **Limited separation of powers and checks and balances** | Parliament supreme in **law-making and amendment**; the executive in **implementation**; the judiciary in **settling disputes and testing constitutionality** |

## Quick Recap (16 points)
1. **The judiciary performs political as well as private-dispute functions**, and **the Supreme Court of India is one of the most powerful courts in the world.** Its **four roles:** protect the **rule of law** and supremacy of law, **safeguard rights**, **settle disputes** by law, and **prevent democracy sliding into individual or group dictatorship.**
2. **Independence means:** no **restraint** on its functioning, no **interference** with its decisions, and judges acting **without fear or favour.** ⭐ **But independence is not arbitrariness or absence of accountability — the judiciary is accountable to the Constitution, to democratic traditions, and to the people.**
3. **Six safeguards:** legislature **excluded from appointments**; **legal merit, not political loyalty**, as the criterion; **security of tenure to retirement**; a **very difficult removal procedure**; **salaries and allowances not subject to legislative approval**; and immunity via **contempt powers** plus the bar on **Parliament discussing judges' conduct** except during removal proceedings.
4. **Appointment is inherently political** — *"it makes a difference who serves... a difference in how the Constitution is interpreted"*; a judge's view of an **active and assertive** versus **controlled and committed** judiciary shapes the fate of legislation. **The Council of Ministers, Governors, Chief Ministers and the CJI all influence it.**
5. ⭐ **The CJI convention (senior-most judge) was broken twice: A.N. Ray in 1973 (superseding three seniors) and M.H. Beg in 1975 (superseding H.R. Khanna).**
6. ⭐⭐ **The collegium evolved between 1982 and 1998** through three positions: the CJI's role is **purely consultative** → the CJI's opinion **must be followed** → finally, **the CJI recommends names in consultation with the four senior-most judges** — the **principle of collegiality.** Today the **senior judges' decision carries greater weight**, though both the Court and the Council of Ministers have a role.
7. **Removal** is only for **proven misbehaviour or incapacity**, on a motion passed by **special majority in both Houses.** ⭐ **The design is elegant: the executive appoints, the legislature removes — ensuring both balance of power and judicial independence.**
8. ⭐⭐ **The only removal attempt: Justice V. Ramaswami.** **108 MPs signed the motion in 1991**; a **1992 inquiry commission of Supreme Court judges** found him guilty of *"wilful and gross misuses of office... and moral turpitude"* as **Chief Justice of the Punjab and Haryana High Court**; yet he **survived**, because although the motion got **two-thirds of those present and voting**, the **Congress abstained**, so it failed the **one-half of total strength** test. **The perfect illustration of "special majority."**
9. **India has a SINGLE INTEGRATED judiciary — no separate State courts, unlike some federal countries** — a pyramid of **Supreme Court → High Courts → District Courts → Subordinate Courts**, with lower courts under the **direct superintendence** of higher ones. The Supreme Court's decisions **bind all courts**; it can **transfer High Court judges**, **move any case to itself**, and **transfer cases between High Courts.**
10. **Five heads of Supreme Court jurisdiction:** **Original** (Union–State and inter-State disputes; it **alone** may hear them — the **federal umpire**), **Appellate** (highest court of appeal; **High Court certification** normally required, **death sentences** appealable, and the SC may **admit an appeal the High Court refused**), **Advisory** (President may refer matters of **public importance** or **constitutional interpretation**; ⭐ **the Court need not advise and the President need not accept** — its utility is **pre-emptive legal opinion** avoiding later litigation, and a basis for **modifying action or legislation**), **Writ** (all five writs; and a litigant may choose **either** the High Court **or** the Supreme Court directly), and **Special Powers** (**special leave** from any court in India).
11. **Article 137** lets the Supreme Court **review its own judgments**; **Article 144** requires **all authorities, civil and judicial, to act in aid of** it; and the Court **itself decides** cases of contempt of itself.
12. ⭐⭐ **PIL began around 1979**, when the Court heard a case **filed not by the aggrieved persons but by others on their behalf.** The two landmark early PILs: ***Hussainara Khatoon vs. Bihar* (1979)** — Bihar undertrials who had spent **longer in jail than the maximum sentence for their offence**, taken up after **newspaper reports** prompted an advocate's petition; and ***Sunil Batra vs. Delhi Administration* (1980)** — a **Tihar jail inmate's scribbled note to Justice Krishna Iyer** about torture, which the **judge converted into a petition** (though the Court **later abandoned** treating letters as petitions).
13. **PIL's two achievements:** it **expanded the idea of rights** (clean air, unpolluted water, decent living as rights of the whole society) and it **reached those who cannot reach the courts**, by allowing **public-spirited citizens, social organisations and lawyers** to petition on behalf of the needy. **Justice Bhagwati (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984): the problems of the poor are "qualitatively different"; blindly following the adversarial procedure would mean "they would never be able to enforce their fundamental rights."** It made the **Right against Exploitation** real in cases on the **blinding of jail inmates, stone-quarry conditions, and sexual exploitation of children** — and PIL is now **written into South Africa's Bill of Rights** as a fundamental right.
14. **Judicial activism's four gains:** it **democratised access** (groups, not just individuals), **forced executive accountability**, pushed **electoral reform** — notably **candidates' affidavits on assets, income and educational qualifications** — and declared ***bandhs* and *hartals* illegal.** **Its three costs:** it has **overburdened the courts**, **blurred the line** between judiciary and the other organs (pollution control, corruption investigation and electoral reform are properly the **administration's** job under **legislative** supervision), and made the **balance among the three organs very delicate.** ⭐ **Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar warned in 1949 that independence must not become "a dogma" turning the judiciary into "a super-legislature or super-executive."**
15. **Two constitutional remedies:** **writs under Article 32** (and **Article 226** for High Courts) restore rights; **Article 13** lets the Court declare a law **unconstitutional and non-operational.** Together they make the Supreme Court both **protector of fundamental rights** and **interpreter of the Constitution.** ⭐⭐ **Judicial review is nowhere mentioned in the Constitution** — it is implicit in a **written** constitution plus the power to strike down rights-violating law — and lies on **two grounds: violation of Fundamental Rights, and violation of the federal distribution of powers** (a Centre-made law trespassing on the **State List** can be struck down). **It extends to State legislation too.**
16. ⭐⭐ **Judiciary vs Parliament:** the Court brought even the **powers of the President and Governor** under review, and directed the **CBI** in the **hawala**, **Narasimha Rao** and **illegal petrol-pump allotment** cases. The Constitution rests on **limited separation of powers and checks and balances** — Parliament supreme in **law-making and amendment**, the executive in **implementation**, the judiciary in **settling disputes and testing constitutionality.** The **four contested issues** were the scope of the **right to property**, of Parliament's power to **abridge Fundamental Rights**, of its power to **amend the Constitution**, and whether it may **abridge rights while enforcing Directive Principles** — with the conflict at its most serious **1967–1973**, over land reform, **preventive detention**, **job reservation**, and **acquisition of private property and its compensation.** ⭐⭐ ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** settled it in three moves: there **is** a **basic structure** no amendment may violate; the **right to property is not part of it** and may be abridged; and **the Court reserved to itself the right to decide what belongs to the basic structure.** Removing property from Part III (**44th Amendment, in force 1979**) further cooled the conflict. **Still unresolved:** whether courts may protect someone punished for **breach of parliamentary privilege** — with legislatures viewing judicial instructions on legislative business as an assault on **parliamentary sovereignty.**

SC → HC → District → Subordinate.** **Jurisdictions: Original (Union–State, SC alone, federal umpire) | Appellate (HC certificate, death sentences, SC can admit anyway) | Advisory (SC need not advise, President need not accept; utility = pre-empt litigation + amend action/legislation) | Writ (HM-PQC; litigant may choose HC or SC) | Special leave (any court).** **Art 137 = review own judgments; Art 144 = all authorities act in aid; SC decides its own contempt.** **PIL from 1979. Hussainara Khatoon vs Bihar (undertrials longer than sentence, newspaper report). Sunil Batra vs Delhi Admin 1980 (Tihar note to Justice Krishna Iyer; letters later abandoned).** **Bhagwati, Bandhua Mukti Morcha 1984: problems of the poor are "qualitatively different"; adversarial procedure would leave them unable to enforce rights.** **PIL gains: group access, executive accountability, candidate affidavits on assets/income/education, bandhs & hartals illegal. Costs: overburdened courts, blurred separation, delicate balance. Ayyar 1949: independence must not become dogma → "super-legislature or super-executive."** **Art 32 (SC writs) / Art 226 (HC writs) / Art 13 (strike down law). Judicial review is NOT in the text — implicit from a written constitution + power to strike down. Two grounds: Fundamental Rights + federal distribution of powers; extends to State laws.** **CBI directed in hawala, Narasimha Rao, petrol pump cases. Presidential & Governor's powers brought under review.** **Four contested issues: property, abridging FRs, amending power, abridging FRs to enforce DPSP. 1967–73 = peak conflict.** **KESAVANANDA BHARATI 1973: (i) basic structure inviolable, (ii) property NOT basic structure, (iii) Court decides what IS basic structure.** **44th Amendment → property to Art 300A (in force 1979).** **Unresolved: judicial protection against breach-of-privilege punishment vs parliamentary sovereignty.**

## Try These Questions

### NCERT Exercises (with answers/pointers)
1. **Different ways independence of the judiciary is ensured — choose the ODD ones out:**
   - i. *Chief Justice of the Supreme Court is consulted in the appointment of other judges of the Supreme Court* → ✅ **a genuine safeguard**
   - ii. *Judges are generally not removed before the age of retirement* → ✅ **a genuine safeguard (security of tenure)**
   - iii. *A judge of a High Court cannot be transferred to another High Court* → ❌ **ODD ONE OUT — this is FACTUALLY WRONG.** The chapter expressly states the Supreme Court **"can transfer Judges of High Courts."**
   - iv. *Parliament has no say in the appointment of judges* → ✅ **a genuine safeguard** *(though note Parliament **does** have the power of **removal**)*
2. **Does independence of the judiciary mean that the judiciary is not accountable to anyone? (100 words)** → **No.** *Use the chapter's exact formulation: independence "does not imply arbitrariness or absence of accountability. Judiciary is a part of the democratic political structure of the country. It is therefore accountable to **the Constitution, to the democratic traditions and to the people** of the country." Add the concrete mechanisms: **removal by Parliament for proven misbehaviour or incapacity**; the Court's own **power of review under Art. 137**; the requirement to give **reasoned judgments**; and appeal to higher courts. Close with **Ayyar's warning** that independence must not become a dogma producing a super-legislature.*
3. **What are the different provisions in the Constitution to maintain the independence of the judiciary?** → *The six measures in Part 1: exclusion of the legislature from appointments; legal qualification as the criterion; **fixed tenure to retirement**; removal only for **proven misbehaviour or incapacity** by **special majority in both Houses**; **salaries and allowances outside legislative approval**; and **contempt powers** plus the bar on **Parliament discussing judges' conduct** except during removal proceedings.*
4. ⭐ **The Dahanu chikoo farmers case (*Supreme Court orders REL to pay Rs 300 crore to Dahanu farmers*, 24 March 2005):**
   | Question | Answer |
   |---|---|
   | **What is the case about?** | **Pollution from a thermal power plant at Dahanu (150 km from Mumbai)**, operational since **1989**, whose **fly ash entered ground water and polluted the entire eco-system.** The first crop failure came the **next year**; now **70% of the crop of what was once "the fruit bowl of Maharashtra" is gone**, the **fisheries have shut** and the **forest cover has thinned.** The **Dahanu Taluka Environment Protection Authority** ordered a **pollution control unit to reduce sulphur emissions**; despite a **Supreme Court order backing it, the unit was not set up even by 2002.** **Reliance acquired the station in 2003**, re-submitted an installation schedule in **2004**, and — the plant still not built — the Authority demanded a **bank guarantee of Rs. 300 crore.** |
   | **Who has been the beneficiary?** | **The chikoo (sapota) growers / farmers of the Dahanu area** — and, more broadly, the **local fisherfolk and the ecosystem.** |
   | **Who is the petitioner?** | **The chikoo growers**, who *"petitioned the court against the pollution caused by Reliance's thermal power plant"* — supported by **environmentalists.** |
   | **Company's likely arguments** | It **acquired the plant only in 2003** and inherited the default; it had **submitted a fresh installation schedule (2004)**; the technology is **costly and complex**; the plant supplies **essential electricity in the public interest**; the **causal link** between fly ash and crop failure is not proven; **Rs. 300 crore is disproportionate.** |
   | **Farmers' likely arguments** | **Right to life and livelihood under Article 21** — including the **right to a clean environment, unpolluted water and decent living** that PIL jurisprudence has established; the **polluter pays** principle; a **Supreme Court order was defied for over a decade**; **70% crop loss, closed fisheries and thinned forests** are measurable harm; and **liability runs with the plant**, not with a change of ownership. |
   *(Note how neatly this case demonstrates the chapter's claim that PIL "expanded the idea of rights" to **clean air, unpolluted water and decent living.**)*
5. ⭐ **The Delhi CNG case (*Centre, Delhi join hands on CNG issue*, 23 September 2001):**
   | Question | Answer |
   |---|---|
   | **Governments at different levels** | **Union government** (Union Petroleum and Natural Gas Minister **Ram Naik**) and the **State/NCT government of Delhi** (Chief Minister **Sheila Dikshit**). *(Municipal transport authorities are implicitly a third tier.)* |
   | **Role of the Supreme Court** | It had **directed the phasing out of non-CNG commercial vehicles** and **refused to relax the CNG-only norm for the city's buses** — while clarifying it **"had never insisted on CNG for taxis and auto rickshaws."** Both governments had to **file affidavits and plead before the Court** for an extension. |
   | **Working of the judiciary and executive** | ⭐ **A textbook case of judicial activism on environmental policy**, and of the friction the chapter warns about: *"reducing air or sound pollution... is not exactly the duty of the Judiciary."* The **executive** must implement; the **Court** set the deadline; and the executive returned to **seek time and modification** rather than legislate. Note also the **cooperative federalism** — separate affidavits with **common points**, the Centre supporting Delhi's stand. |
   | **Policy / legislation / implementation / interpretation** | **Policy:** a **dual fuel policy** rather than single-fuel dependence (*"full of dangers and would result in disaster"*); **discouraging CNG use by private vehicle owners**; permitting **0.05% low sulphur diesel** for buses; allowing all **Euro-II compliant** commercial vehicles. **Legislation/expert input:** the **Dr. R.A. Mashelkar high-powered Committee** on a national **"Auto Fuel Policy"**, reporting within six months. **Implementation:** converting a **10,000-odd bus fleet**, **long queues outside CNG filling stations**, CNG requirements, and reliance on **pipeline supply** whose disruption *"would throw the public transport system out of gear."* **Interpretation:** what the Court's directive requires, and whether a **deadline extension** is permissible. |
6. **The Ecuador statement — similarities and differences with India:** *"Judges are not forced to respect the rulings of higher courts in previous cases. Unlike the US, an appellate judge in Ecuador... need not provide a written decision explaining the legal basis of a ruling. A judge may rule one way today and the opposite way, in a similar case, tomorrow, without explaining why."*
   → ⭐ **Sharp contrast with India on three counts:** (i) India has a **single integrated judiciary** in which *"decisions made by the Supreme Court are binding on all other courts"* and **lower courts function under the direct superintendence of higher courts** — so **precedent binds**; (ii) Indian courts deliver **reasoned, written judgments** setting out the legal basis, which is what makes **appeal and review** meaningful; (iii) consequently India *does* have the *"body of common law, or judicial precedent"* whose absence the writer laments. **The similarity:** in both systems a court **can depart** from earlier positions — but in India only through **reasoned reconsideration** (Art. 137 review, or a larger bench), not arbitrarily. **The lesson: predictability of law depends on binding precedent plus written reasons.**
7. ⭐ **Match the statements with the jurisdiction:**
   | Statement | Jurisdiction |
   |---|---|
   | *The government wanted to know if it can pass a law about the citizenship status of residents of Pakistan-occupied areas of Jammu and Kashmir* | ⭐ **ADVISORY** *(a matter of public importance / constitutional interpretation referred by the President)* |
   | *In order to resolve the dispute about river Cauvery, the government of Tamil Nadu wants to approach the court* | ⭐ **ORIGINAL** *(an inter-State dispute — the Supreme Court as federal umpire)* |
   | *Court rejected the appeal by people against the eviction from the dam site* | ⭐ **APPELLATE** |
8. **In what way can public interest litigation help the poor?** → *Because in the normal course **only a personally aggrieved person could move the court**, and the poor cannot easily do so. PIL lets **public-spirited citizens, social organisations and lawyers petition on their behalf**, and lets courts act on **newspaper reports and postal complaints.** It **expanded rights** to **clean air, unpolluted water and decent living**, and it made the **Right against Exploitation** enforceable — the **blinding of jail inmates**, **stone-quarry working conditions**, **sexual exploitation of children**, **Bihar's undertrials (*Hussainara Khatoon*)**, **Tihar jail torture (*Sunil Batra*)**. As Justice Bhagwati held, without such a departure from **adversarial procedure**, *"they would never be able to enforce their fundamental rights."*
9. **Can judicial activism lead to conflict between the judiciary and the executive? Why?** → **Yes.** *Because the Constitution rests on **limited separation of powers**: the executive is supreme in implementation, the judiciary in adjudication. Activism has the Court **giving directions to executive agencies** (the CBI in the **hawala**, **Narasimha Rao** and **petrol-pump** cases), setting **environmental policy** (the Delhi CNG deadline), and ordering **electoral reform** — matters *"to be handled by the administration under the supervision of the legislatures."* It even brought the **powers of the President and Governor** under review. Hence the charge of a **"super-legislature or super-executive"** (Ayyar) and the chapter's own conclusion that activism **"may be creating strains"** on the principle that each organ respect the others.*
10. **How is judicial activism related to the protection of fundamental rights? Has it helped in expanding the scope of fundamental rights?** → **Yes, decisively.** *Two mechanisms: **relaxed *locus standi*** (others may petition on the aggrieved person's behalf), and **liberal use of Art. 32 writs and Art. 13 review.** The expansion, concretely: **Article 21** has been read to include the **right to live with human dignity, free from exploitation**, the **right to shelter** and the **right to livelihood**; and PIL added **clean air, unpolluted water and decent living** as rights of the whole society. Under the **Right against Exploitation**, below-minimum wages were held to be ***begar*** (the Asian Games case, Ch. 2). ⭐ The measure of success: *"This trend has made rights really meaningful for the poor and disadvantaged sections"* — and **South Africa has written PIL into its Bill of Rights** as a fundamental right.*
