# Chapter 9: Constitution as a Living Document

## The Big Idea
**The riddle this chapter solves:** how has India **managed to be governed by the SAME Constitution** for over 69 years, when so many other countries have rewritten theirs repeatedly?

**Four findings:**
1. **"The Indian Constitution CAN BE AMENDED according to the needs of the time."**
2. ⭐ **"Though MANY such amendments have already taken place, the Constitution HAS REMAINED INTACT and its BASIC PREMISES HAVE NOT CHANGED."**
3. **"The JUDICIARY has played an important role in PROTECTING the Constitution and also in INTERPRETING the Constitution."**
4. ⭐⭐ **"The Constitution is a document that KEEPS EVOLVING and RESPONDING TO CHANGING SITUATIONS."**

## PART 1 — ARE CONSTITUTIONS STATIC?

### ⭐⭐ Why nations rewrite constitutions
⭐ **"It is not uncommon for nations to REWRITE their constitutions in response to (i) CHANGED CIRCUMSTANCES or (ii) CHANGE OF IDEAS WITHIN THE SOCIETY or even due to (iii) POLITICAL UPHEAVALS."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The comparative record (learn these — they are the standard contrast)
| Country | Constitutional history |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Soviet Union** | **"The Soviet Union had FOUR constitutions in its life of 74 years — 1918, 1924, 1936 and 1977."** ⭐ **"In 1991, the rule of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union came to an end and soon the Soviet federation disintegrated. After this political upheaval, the newly formed RUSSIAN FEDERATION adopted a NEW CONSTITUTION IN 1993."** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **France** | **"France had NUMEROUS constitutions in the last two centuries."** *(Learn the five republics:)*<br>• **Post-revolution constitution of 1793 → the FIRST French republic**<br>• **1848 → the SECOND French republic**<br>• **1875 → the THIRD French republic (a new constitution)**<br>• **1946 → the FOURTH French republic**<br>• ⭐ **"Finally, in 1958, the FIFTH French republic came into being with yet another constitution."** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **USA** | **"The Constitution of the US came into existence MORE THAN 200 YEARS AGO and so far it has been amended ONLY 27 TIMES!"** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **India** | **"The Constitution of India was ADOPTED ON 26 NOVEMBER 1949. Its implementation formally started from 26 JANUARY 1950. MORE THAN 69 YEARS after that, THE SAME CONSTITUTION CONTINUES to function as the framework within which the government of our country operates."** |

> ⭐ **The margin comment that draws the lesson:** *"It seems to me that **constitutional changes are very closely linked to POLITICAL DEVELOPMENTS.**"* **Note the pattern: France's constitutions track its revolutions and regime changes; the USSR's track its consolidations; Russia's 1993 constitution follows the 1991 collapse. India changed *within* one framework instead.**

### ⭐⭐ Why has India's Constitution survived? — the two questions and the honest answer
*"Is it that our Constitution is so good that it needs no change? Was it that our Constitution makers were so farsighted and wise that they had foreseen all the changes that would take place in the future?"*

⭐ **"In some sense BOTH the answers are correct:"**
- **"It is true that we have inherited a VERY ROBUST Constitution. THE BASIC FRAMEWORK of the Constitution IS VERY MUCH SUITED TO OUR COUNTRY."**
- **"It is also true that the Constitution makers were VERY FARSIGHTED and PROVIDED FOR MANY SOLUTIONS FOR FUTURE SITUATIONS."**

⭐⭐ **But the honest qualification:** *"**BUT NO CONSTITUTION CAN PROVIDE FOR ALL EVENTUALITIES. NO DOCUMENT CAN BE SUCH THAT IT NEEDS NO CHANGE.**"*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The real answer — the two mechanisms of survival
⭐⭐ **"Then how does the SAME Constitution continue to serve the country?"**
1. ⭐⭐ **"One of the answers is that OUR CONSTITUTION ACCEPTS THE NECESSITY OF MODIFICATIONS ACCORDING TO CHANGING NEEDS OF THE SOCIETY."** *(→ formal **amendment**)*
2. ⭐⭐ **"Secondly, in the ACTUAL WORKING of the Constitution, there has been ENOUGH FLEXIBILITY OF INTERPRETATIONS. Both POLITICAL PRACTICE and JUDICIAL RULINGS have shown MATURITY AND FLEXIBILITY in implementing the Constitution."** *(→ **interpretation**)*

⭐⭐⭐ **"THESE FACTORS HAVE MADE OUR CONSTITUTION A LIVING DOCUMENT RATHER THAN A CLOSED AND STATIC RULEBOOK."**

### ⭐⭐ The universal dilemma of constitution-makers
*"In any society, those responsible for drafting the constitution at a particular time would face one common challenge:"*
- **"The provisions of the constitution would naturally reflect efforts to tackle the problems that the society is facing AT THE TIME OF MAKING of the constitution."**
- **"AT THE SAME TIME, the constitution must be a document that provides the framework of the government FOR THE FUTURE as well. Therefore, the constitution has to be able to RESPOND TO THE CHALLENGES THAT MAY ARISE IN THE FUTURE."**
- ⭐⭐ **"In this sense, THE CONSTITUTION WILL ALWAYS HAVE SOMETHING THAT IS CONTEMPORARY AND SOMETHING THAT HAS A MORE DURABLE IMPORTANCE."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The dual nature of a constitution — sacred *and* instrumental
- ⭐ **"A constitution is NOT A FROZEN AND UNALTERABLE DOCUMENT. It is a document MADE BY HUMAN BEINGS and may need REVISIONS, CHANGES AND RE-EXAMINATION."**
- **"It is true that the constitution reflects the DREAMS AND ASPIRATIONS of the concerned society."**
- ⭐⭐ **"It must also be kept in mind that the constitution is a FRAMEWORK FOR THE DEMOCRATIC GOVERNANCE of the society. In this sense, IT IS AN INSTRUMENT THAT SOCIETIES CREATE FOR THEMSELVES."**

⭐⭐⭐ **The two extreme questions this dual role produces:**
> **"Is it so SACRED that NOBODY EVER CAN CHANGE IT? Alternatively, is it so ORDINARY an instrument that it can be MODIFIED JUST LIKE ANY OTHER ORDINARY LAW?"**

### ⭐⭐⭐ How the framers struck the balance (the core answer)
1. ⭐ **"They PLACED THE CONSTITUTION ABOVE ORDINARY LAW and expected that the FUTURE GENERATIONS WILL RESPECT THIS DOCUMENT."**
2. ⭐ **"AT THE SAME TIME, they RECOGNISED that in the future, THIS DOCUMENT MAY REQUIRE MODIFICATIONS."**
3. ⭐⭐ **The subtle third point:** *"**Even at the time of writing the Constitution, they were aware that ON MANY MATTERS THERE WERE DIFFERENCES OF OPINION. WHENEVER SOCIETY WOULD VEER TOWARD ANY PARTICULAR OPINION, A CHANGE IN THE CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS WOULD BE REQUIRED.**"*

⭐⭐⭐ **"Thus, the Indian Constitution is a COMBINATION OF BOTH the approaches: that the constitution is a SACRED DOCUMENT and that it is an INSTRUMENT THAT MAY REQUIRE CHANGES from time to time. In other words, OUR CONSTITUTION IS NOT A STATIC DOCUMENT, IT IS NOT THE FINAL WORD ABOUT EVERYTHING; IT IS NOT UNALTERABLE."**

### ⭐⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — four student statements to evaluate
| Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **"The Constitution is like any other law. It simply tells us the rules and regulations governing the government."** | ❌ **Wrong on both counts.** The framers **"placed the Constitution ABOVE ordinary law"** — hence special majorities, State ratification, and the President's inability to return an amendment bill. And it is far more than rules: it expresses the **dreams and aspirations** of society (Ch. 1's Functions 4 and 5). |
| **"The Constitution is the expression of the will of the people, so there must be a provision to change the Constitution after every ten or fifteen years."** | ❌ **Wrong.** A **periodic rewrite** would give **"no security, predictability or identity to a people"** (Ch. 1) — the *too-flexible* failure. Change should come **when needed**, through amendment, not on a timetable. Compare **France's five republics** with the **USA's 27 amendments in 200 years.** |
| **"The Constitution is a statement of the philosophy of the country. It can never be changed."** | ❌ **Wrong.** *"No constitution can provide for all eventualities... It is a document MADE BY HUMAN BEINGS."* The *too-rigid* constitution **"is likely to break under the weight of change"** (Ch. 1). |
| **"The Constitution is a sacred document. Therefore any talk of changing it is against democracy."** | ❌ **Wrong — and inverted.** ⭐ Amendment is **the most democratic act of all**, because *"only elected representatives of the people are empowered to consider and take final decisions on the question of amendments."* The Constitution is **both** sacred **and** an instrument societies create for themselves. |
| **The correct position** | ✅ **The Constitution is placed above ordinary law but is not unalterable — it is a combination of the sacred and the instrumental, kept alive by amendment AND interpretation.** |

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 2 — HOW TO AMEND THE CONSTITUTION

> ### Bare text to memorise — **Article 368**
> *"...**Parliament may in exercise of its CONSTITUENT POWER amend by way of ADDITION, VARIATION or REPEAL any provision of this Constitution** in accordance with the procedure laid down in this article."*

### ⭐⭐ Flexible vs Rigid — the definitions
- ⭐ **"FLEXIBLE means OPEN TO CHANGES and RIGID means RESISTANT TO CHANGES."**
- **"A constitution that can be VERY EASILY CHANGED OR MODIFIED is often called FLEXIBLE."**
- **"In the case of constitutions which are VERY DIFFICULT TO AMEND, they are described as RIGID."**
- ⭐⭐ **"THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION COMBINES BOTH THESE CHARACTERISTICS."**

> *The chapter's own sharp margin question:* *"I don't understand how a constitution can be flexible or rigid. **Isn't it the POLITICS of that period which makes the constitution rigid or flexible?**"*

### ⭐⭐⭐ WHY there are different amendment procedures — the four considerations
| # | Consideration | What it demanded |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐ **"There may be some FAULTS OR MISTAKES in the Constitution; they knew that the Constitution COULD NOT BE TOTALLY FREE OF ERRORS. Whenever such mistakes would come to light, they wanted the Constitution to be EASILY AMENDED and to be able to GET RID OF THESE MISTAKES."** | **Flexibility** |
| **2** | ⭐ **"Then there were some provisions in the Constitution that were of TEMPORARY NATURE, and it was decided that these could be ALTERED LATER ON once the new Parliament was elected."** | **Flexibility** |
| **3** | ⭐⭐ **"BUT AT THE SAME TIME, the Constitution was framing a FEDERAL POLITY, and therefore THE RIGHTS AND POWERS OF THE STATES COULD NOT BE CHANGED WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE STATES."** | **State ratification** |
| **4** | ⭐⭐ **"Some other features were SO CENTRAL TO THE SPIRIT OF THE CONSTITUTION that the Constitution makers were ANXIOUS TO PROTECT THESE FROM CHANGE. THESE PROVISIONS HAD TO BE MADE RIGID."** | **Rigidity** |

⭐ **"These considerations led to DIFFERENT WAYS OF AMENDING THE CONSTITUTION."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The THREE methods of amendment (a certain exam question)
| Method | Requirement | Which articles |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **1. Similar to ordinary law** | **SIMPLE MAJORITY in Parliament** — *"as mentioned in some articles"* | Articles whose text says **"by law"** — ⭐ **no recourse to Article 368 at all** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **2. Special majority** | **SPECIAL MAJORITY in BOTH Houses SEPARATELY** — *"as per Article 368"* | The bulk of the Constitution |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **3. Special majority + State ratification** | **SPECIAL MAJORITY + legislatures of HALF the States** — *"Article 368"* | Federal provisions and representation |

### ⭐⭐ Method 1 — Amendment by simple law
⭐ **"There are MANY articles in the Constitution which mention that these articles CAN BE AMENDED BY A SIMPLE LAW OF THE PARLIAMENT. NO SPECIAL PROCEDURE for amendment is required in such cases, and THERE IS NO DIFFERENCE AT ALL BETWEEN AN AMENDMENT AND AN ORDINARY LAW. THESE PARTS OF THE CONSTITUTION ARE VERY FLEXIBLE."**

> ⭐⭐ **The textual tell — the words "by law":**
> **Article 2:** *"**Parliament may BY LAW admit into the union .....new states....**"*
> **Article 3:** *"**Parliament may BY LAW... b) increase the area of any state....**"*
>
> ⭐⭐ **"In both these articles, the wording 'BY LAW' indicates that these articles can be modified by the Parliament WITHOUT RECOURSE TO THE PROCEDURE LAID DOWN IN ARTICLE 368."**

*(Note the link to Chapter 7: this is exactly why *"the very existence of a State including its territorial integrity is in the hands of Parliament"* — creating, merging or renaming a State needs only a **simple majority**. And it is how the **J&K Reorganisation Act 2019** converted a State into two Union Territories.)*

### ⭐⭐⭐ Four features of the amendment process worth memorising
1. ⭐⭐ **"Note that ALL AMENDMENTS to the Constitution are INITIATED ONLY IN THE PARLIAMENT."** *(States cannot propose one.)*
2. ⭐⭐ **"Besides the special majority in the Parliament, NO OUTSIDE AGENCY — like a CONSTITUTION COMMISSION or a SEPARATE BODY — IS REQUIRED for amending the Constitution."**
3. ⭐⭐ **"After the passage in the Parliament and, in some cases, in State legislatures, NO REFERENDUM IS REQUIRED for ratification of the amendment."**
4. ⭐⭐⭐ **"An amendment bill, like all other bills, goes to the President for his assent, BUT IN THIS CASE, THE PRESIDENT HAS NO POWERS TO SEND IT BACK FOR RECONSIDERATION."** *(Contrast Ch. 4: for an ordinary non-money bill the President **can** require reconsideration once — and can even exercise a **pocket veto**. **Not for an amendment bill.**)*

⭐⭐⭐ **Why this relative simplicity matters — the governing principle:**
> **"These details show HOW RIGID AND COMPLICATED THE AMENDING PROCESS COULD HAVE BEEN. Our Constitution AVOIDS these complications. This makes the amendment procedure RELATIVELY SIMPLE. But more importantly, this process underlines an important principle: ⭐⭐⭐ ONLY ELECTED REPRESENTATIVES OF THE PEOPLE ARE EMPOWERED TO CONSIDER AND TAKE FINAL DECISIONS ON THE QUESTION OF AMENDMENTS. Thus, SOVEREIGNTY OF ELECTED REPRESENTATIVES (PARLIAMENTARY SOVEREIGNTY) IS THE BASIS OF THE AMENDMENT PROCEDURE."**

> *The chapter's own federal objection, worth noting:* *"**What happens if some States want an amendment to the Constitution? Can't they propose an amendment?** I think this is another example of favouring the centre against the States!"*

### ⭐⭐⭐ Special Majority — the precise arithmetic
⭐ **"Ordinarily, all business of the legislature requires that a motion or resolution or bill should get the support of a SIMPLE MAJORITY OF THE MEMBERS VOTING AT THAT TIME."** *The chapter's example:* *"if at the time of voting on a bill, **247 members were present** and all participated, the bill would be passed if **at least 124 members** voted in favour."*

⭐⭐⭐ **"NOT SO in the case of an amendment bill. Amendment to the Constitution requires TWO DIFFERENT KINDS OF SPECIAL MAJORITIES:"**
1. ⭐⭐⭐ **"Those voting IN FAVOUR of the amendment bill should constitute AT LEAST HALF OF THE TOTAL STRENGTH OF THAT HOUSE."**
2. ⭐⭐⭐ **"The supporters of the amendment bill must ALSO constitute TWO-THIRDS OF THOSE WHO ACTUALLY TAKE PART IN VOTING."**

⭐⭐⭐ **"BOTH Houses of the Parliament must pass the amendment bill SEPARATELY in this same manner — ⭐ THERE IS NO PROVISION FOR A JOINT SESSION. For EVERY amendment bill, this special majority is required."**

*(⭐ **Contrast with Chapter 5:** an ordinary bill deadlock is resolved by a **joint session** — where *"the decision has always gone in favour of the Lok Sabha."* **For amendments, there is no such escape route: both Houses must independently clear the bar.**)*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The worked calculation (do this — it is exam-standard)
⭐ **"In the Lok Sabha there are 545 members. Therefore, ANY AMENDMENT MUST BE SUPPORTED BY A MINIMUM OF 273 MEMBERS."** *(= half of the total strength)*

⭐⭐ **"Even if only 300 members are present at the time of voting, the amendment bill MUST GET THE SUPPORT OF 273 OUT OF THEM."** *(Because condition 1 is an absolute floor — two-thirds of 300 is only 200, which is **not enough**.)*

⭐⭐⭐ **The chapter's own question: "Imagine that 400 members of the Lok Sabha have voted on an amendment bill. How many members should support the bill to get the bill passed?"**
> **Answer: 267 by the two-thirds test (2/3 × 400 = 266.67 → 267), but condition 1 requires at least 273. Since BOTH conditions must be satisfied, the answer is 273.**
>
> ⭐⭐ **The general rule to remember: the binding constraint is whichever number is HIGHER.** With **545 members**, half the strength = **273**; two-thirds of those voting only exceeds 273 once **more than ~409 members** vote. **So for turnouts below ~409, the "half of total strength" test binds; above it, the "two-thirds of those voting" test binds.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why special majority — the political consequence
> **"Can you see the significance of this requirement? ... unless there is SUFFICIENT CONSENSUS over the proposed amendment, IT CANNOT BE PASSED. ⭐⭐ IF THE PARTY IN POWER ENJOYS VERY THIN MAJORITY, IT CAN PASS LEGISLATION OF ITS CHOICE AND CAN GET BUDGET APPROVED EVEN IF THE OPPOSITION DOES NOT AGREE. BUT IT WOULD NEED TO TAKE AT LEAST SOME OPPOSITION PARTIES INTO CONFIDENCE IF IT WANTED TO AMEND THE CONSTITUTION."**

⭐⭐⭐ **"So, the basic principle behind the amending procedure is that IT SHOULD BE BASED ON BROAD SUPPORT AMONG THE POLITICAL PARTIES AND PARLIAMENTARIANS."**

> ### ⭐⭐⭐ Ambedkar on the special majority — the definitive justification
> *"**If those who are dissatisfied with the Constitution have only to obtain a 2/3 majority, and if they cannot obtain even (that)..., THEIR DISSATISFACTION WITH THE CONSTITUTION CANNOT BE DEEMED TO BE SHARED BY THE GENERAL PUBLIC.**"*
> — **Dr. Ambedkar, CAD, Vol. XI, p. 976, 25 November 1949**
>
> ⭐⭐ **The chapter's own gloss, which is the key insight:** *"Note that Dr. Ambedkar is talking here **NOT ONLY of parliamentary majority. He refers to 'SHARING (OF THE VIEWS) BY THE GENERAL PUBLIC'. This indicates that BEHIND THE MAJORITY THERE IS THE PRINCIPLE OF PUBLIC OPINION that governs decision-making.**"*
>
> **In other words: the two-thirds bar is a *proxy* for public opinion. If you cannot muster two-thirds of Parliament, the country probably does not want the change.**

### ⭐⭐ Box: The two world principles of amendment (a good comparative box)
> **"TWO PRINCIPLES DOMINATE the various procedures of amending constitutions in most modern constitutions:"**
> 1. ⭐⭐ **The principle of SPECIAL MAJORITY** — *"For instance, the constitutions of **U.S., South Africa, Russia**, etc. have employed this principle: **in the case of the constitution of US, it is TWO-THIRDS majority, while in SOUTH AFRICA and RUSSIA, for some amendments, THREE-FOURTHS majority is required.**"*
> 2. ⭐⭐ **The principle of PEOPLE'S PARTICIPATION** — *"⭐ **In SWITZERLAND, PEOPLE CAN EVEN INITIATE AN AMENDMENT.** Other examples of countries where **people initiate or approve** amendment to the constitution are **RUSSIA and ITALY**, among others."*
>
> ⭐⭐⭐ **India uses ONLY the FIRST principle** — hence *"no referendum is required"* and *"only elected representatives... are empowered."* **This is the direct link back to Chapter 1: "The Indian Constitution was never subjected to such a referendum, but nevertheless carried enormous public authority."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Ratification by States
⭐ **"For SOME articles of the Constitution, SPECIAL MAJORITY IS NOT SUFFICIENT."**

⭐⭐⭐ **When State ratification is needed — the two categories:**
1. **"When an amendment aims to modify an article related to the DISTRIBUTION OF POWERS BETWEEN THE STATES AND THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT"**
2. **"or articles related to REPRESENTATION"**

⭐⭐ **The federal rationale:** *"**FEDERALISM MEANS THAT POWERS OF THE STATES MUST NOT BE AT THE MERCY OF THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT.** The Constitution has ensured this by providing that **LEGISLATURES OF HALF THE STATES HAVE TO PASS THE AMENDMENT BILL BEFORE THE AMENDMENT COMES INTO EFFECT.**"*

⭐ **"We can say that for some parts of the Constitution, GREATER OR WIDER CONSENSUS IN THE POLITY IS EXPECTED. This provision also RESPECTS THE STATES AND GIVES THEM PARTICIPATION IN THE PROCESS OF AMENDMENT."**

⭐⭐⭐ **But even here, flexibility is preserved — two deliberate relaxations:**
1. ⭐⭐ **"CONSENT OF ONLY HALF THE STATES is required"** *(not all, not two-thirds)*
2. ⭐⭐ **"SIMPLE MAJORITY OF THE STATE LEGISLATURE IS SUFFICIENT"** *(not a special majority)*

⭐ **"Thus, the amendment process is NOT IMPRACTICABLE even after taking into consideration this more stringent condition."**

### ⭐⭐ The summary of the design
> **"We may summarise that the Constitution of India can be amended through LARGE-SCALE CONSENSUS AND LIMITED PARTICIPATION OF THE STATES. THE FOUNDING FATHERS TOOK CARE THAT THE CONSTITUTION WOULD NOT BE OPEN TO EASY TAMPERING. AND YET, FUTURE GENERATIONS WERE GIVEN THE RIGHT TO AMEND AND MODIFY ACCORDING TO THE NEEDS AND REQUIREMENTS OF THE TIME."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — which procedure for which amendment? (work this table)
| Subject of amendment | Special majority | Ratification by States |
|---|---|---|
| **Citizenship clause** | ✅ | ❌ *(not a federal-distribution or representation matter)* |
| **Right to freedom of religion** | ✅ | ❌ *(a Fundamental Right — Part III — needs special majority only)* |
| ⭐ **Changes in the Union List** | ✅ | ⭐ ✅ *(this is the **distribution of powers between States and the Centre** — the Seventh Schedule)* |
| ⭐ **Changes in State boundaries** | ⭐ **❌ — neither!** | ⭐ **❌** *(Article 3 says **"by law"** → a **SIMPLE MAJORITY** of Parliament suffices; only the concerned State's **view** is secured, not its consent)* |
| ⭐ **Provision regarding the Election Commission** | ✅ | ⭐ ✅ *(the EC conducts elections to **Parliament AND every State legislature** and to the offices of President and Vice-President — a **representation** matter under Art. 324)* |

> **⚠️ The trap in this table is "changes in State boundaries."** Intuitively it feels like the most federal question of all — yet it is the **easiest** to amend, because Articles 2 and 3 use the words **"by law."** **This is exactly the point Chapter 7 made about the unitary bias.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 3 — WHY HAVE THERE BEEN SO MANY AMENDMENTS?

### ⭐⭐⭐ The headline numbers
> **"On 26 JANUARY 2024, the Constitution of India completed 74 YEARS of its existence. In these years, IT HAS BEEN AMENDED 106 TIMES."**
>
> ⭐ **"Given the relatively difficult method of amending the Constitution, THE NUMBER OF AMENDMENTS APPEARS QUITE HIGH."**

*(Compare: **USA — 27 amendments in over 200 years.** India — **106 in 74 years.**)*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The two graphs — and the surprising story they tell
The chapter presents the same data twice:
- **Graph 1: "the number of constitution amendments made every ten years"** — *the bar shows amendments per decade.*
- **Graph 2: "the time taken for every ten amendments"** — *the bar shows years taken for ten amendments.*

⭐⭐⭐ **What the graphs reveal:**
1. ⭐ **"The TWO DECADES FROM 1970 TO 1990 saw a LARGE NUMBER of amendments."**
2. ⭐⭐⭐ **"TEN amendments took place in a short span of THREE YEARS between 1974 AND 1976."**
3. ⭐⭐⭐ **"And again, in JUST THREE YEARS, FROM 2001 TO 2003, TEN amendments took place."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The crucial comparison of those two three-year bursts
| Period | Political character |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **1974–1976** | **"A PERIOD OF CONGRESS DOMINATION. Congress party had a VAST MAJORITY in Parliament — it had 352 SEATS IN THE LOK SABHA and A MAJORITY IN MOST STATE ASSEMBLIES."** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **2001–2003** | **"A period marked by COALITION POLITICS. It was also a period when DIFFERENT PARTIES WERE IN POWER IN DIFFERENT STATES. THE BITTER RIVALRY BETWEEN THE BJP AND ITS OPPONENTS is another feature of this period. AND YET, this period saw AS MANY AS TEN AMENDMENTS IN JUST THREE YEARS."** |

⭐⭐⭐ **The conclusion — a genuinely counter-intuitive finding:**
> **"So, THE INCIDENCE OF AMENDMENTS IS NOT DEPENDENT MERELY ON THE NATURE OF MAJORITY OF THE RULING PARTY ALONE."**

### ⭐⭐ Answering the criticism
- **"There is always a criticism about the number of amendments. It is said that there have been FAR TOO MANY amendments."**
- **"On the face of it, the fact that 106 amendments took place in 74 years does seem to be SOMEWHAT ODD."**
- ⭐⭐ **"But the two graphs suggest that AMENDMENTS ARE NOT ONLY DUE TO POLITICAL CONSIDERATIONS."**
- ⭐⭐⭐ **"BARRING THE FIRST DECADE after the commencement of the Constitution, EVERY DECADE HAS WITNESSED A STEADY STREAM OF AMENDMENTS. This means that IRRESPECTIVE OF THE NATURE OF POLITICS AND THE PARTY IN POWER, AMENDMENTS WERE REQUIRED TO BE MADE FROM TIME TO TIME."**

> ⭐⭐ **The margin comment that gives the right method:** *"Yes, I think **we should be looking at the CHANGES rather than the NUMBER of amendments.** That is only what we should be doing as students of politics."*
>
> **This is the key to the whole section: count *content*, not *quantity*.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 4 — CONTENTS OF THE AMENDMENTS: THREE GROUPS

⭐ **"Amendments made so far may be classified in THREE GROUPS."**

## ⭐⭐⭐ GROUP 1 — Technical or administrative amendments
⭐⭐ **"In the first group there are amendments which are of a TECHNICAL OR ADMINISTRATIVE NATURE and were only CLARIFICATIONS, EXPLANATIONS, and MINOR MODIFICATIONS etc. of the original provisions. ⭐⭐⭐ THEY ARE AMENDMENTS ONLY IN THE LEGAL SENSE, BUT IN MATTER OF FACT, THEY MADE NO SUBSTANTIAL DIFFERENCE TO THE PROVISIONS."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The four examples given (learn each with its amendment number)
| Amendment | What it did |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **15th Amendment** | **"Increased the AGE OF RETIREMENT OF HIGH COURT JUDGES FROM 60 TO 62 YEARS."** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **54th Amendment** | **"SALARIES of judges of High Courts and the Supreme Court were INCREASED."** |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **Six amendments extending SC/ST reservation** | **"The original provision said that these reservations were for a period of TEN YEARS. However, in order to ensure fair representation of these sections, it was necessary to EXTEND THIS PERIOD BY TEN YEARS. Thus, AFTER EVERY TEN YEARS AN AMENDMENT IS MADE to extend the period by another ten years. THIS HAS LED TO SIX AMENDMENTS SO FAR. But these amendments HAVE NOT MADE ANY DIFFERENCE TO THE ORIGINAL PROVISION. In this sense, it is only a TECHNICAL amendment."** *(Links to Ch. 3: extended up to **2030**.)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **The Article 74(1) amendment** | **"In the ORIGINAL Constitution, it was ASSUMED that in our parliamentary government, the President would NORMALLY ABIDE by the advice of the Council of Ministers. This was only REITERATED by a later amendment when ARTICLE 74(1) WAS AMENDED to clarify that the ADVICE OF THE COUNCIL OF MINISTERS WILL BE BINDING ON THE PRESIDENT."** ⭐⭐ **"In reality, THIS AMENDMENT DID NOT MAKE ANY DIFFERENCE BECAUSE THAT IS EXACTLY WHAT HAS BEEN HAPPENING ALL THROUGH. THE AMENDMENT WAS ONLY BY WAY OF EXPLANATION."** *(This is the **42nd Amendment** point from Ch. 4.)* |

> ⭐⭐ **Why Group 1 matters for the "too many amendments" debate:** a large share of the 106 amendments **changed nothing of substance.** Six of them were the *same* SC/ST extension repeated every decade. **Counting amendments therefore badly overstates how much the Constitution has actually changed.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ GROUP 2 — Amendments arising from DIFFERING INTERPRETATIONS
⭐⭐ **"A number of amendments are a product of DIFFERENT INTERPRETATIONS OF THE CONSTITUTION GIVEN BY THE JUDICIARY AND THE GOVERNMENT OF THE DAY. WHEN THESE CLASHED, THE PARLIAMENT HAD TO INSERT AN AMENDMENT UNDERLINING ONE PARTICULAR INTERPRETATION AS THE AUTHENTIC ONE."**

⭐⭐ **The healthy view of this:** *"**IT IS PART OF THE DEMOCRATIC POLITICS THAT VARIOUS INSTITUTIONS WOULD INTERPRET THE CONSTITUTION — AND PARTICULARLY THE SCOPE OF THEIR OWN POWERS — IN A DIFFERENT MANNER.**"*

⭐⭐⭐ **"Many times, the Parliament DID NOT AGREE with the judicial interpretation and therefore SOUGHT TO AMEND THE CONSTITUTION TO OVERCOME THE RULING OF THE JUDICIARY. ⭐ IN THE PERIOD BETWEEN 1970 AND 1975 THIS SITUATION AROSE FREQUENTLY."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The three issues of Parliament–Judiciary difference (recalled from Chapter 6)
1. **"The relationship between FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS and DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES"**
2. **"The scope of the RIGHT TO PRIVATE PROPERTY"**
3. **"The scope of Parliament's power to AMEND THE CONSTITUTION"**

⭐ **"In the period 1970–1975, the Parliament REPEATEDLY MADE AMENDMENTS TO OVERCOME THE ADVERSE INTERPRETATIONS BY THE JUDICIARY."**

⭐⭐ **The contextual caution:** *"It may be kept in mind that **during this period (1970-75) MANY POLITICAL EVENTS WERE UNFOLDING**, and thus **this history of our constitutional development CAN BE FULLY UNDERSTOOD ONLY IN THE CONTEXT OF THE POLITICS OF THAT PERIOD.**"* *(The Class 12 book on Politics in India covers this.)*

> *The chapter's own puzzle, worth answering:* *"If there is a **written** constitution, where is the scope for different interpretations? **Or do people read in the constitution what they want to be there?**"* — the answer comes in Part 6: **the distinction between the LETTER and the SPIRIT.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ GROUP 3 — Amendments through POLITICAL CONSENSUS
⭐⭐ **"Thirdly, there is another LARGE GROUP of amendments that have been made as a result of the CONSENSUS AMONG THE POLITICAL PARTIES. We may say that this consensus made it necessary that some changes had to be made IN ORDER TO REFLECT THE PREVAILING POLITICAL PHILOSOPHY AND ASPIRATIONS OF THE SOCIETY."**

⭐⭐⭐ **"In fact, MANY OF THE AMENDMENTS OF THE POST-1984 PERIOD are instances of this trend."**

⭐⭐⭐ **This solves the 2001–2003 puzzle:** *"Remember our question above about the peculiarity that **even when there were COALITION governments, this period saw SO MANY amendments? THE REASON IS BECAUSE MANY OF THESE AMENDMENTS WERE BASED ON AN EVOLVING CONSENSUS ON CERTAIN ISSUES. Starting with the ANTI-DEFECTION AMENDMENT (52nd amendment), this period saw a SERIES OF AMENDMENTS IN SPITE OF THE POLITICAL TURBULENCE.**"*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The consensus amendments (learn every number)
| Amendment(s) | Subject |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐⭐ **52nd and 91st** | **The ANTI-DEFECTION amendments** *(Ch. 5: 52nd in 1985, modified by the 91st — which also capped the Council of Ministers at 15%, Ch. 4)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **61st** | **"Bringing down the MINIMUM AGE FOR VOTING FROM 21 TO 18 YEARS"** *(Ch. 3: the 1989 amendment)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **73rd and 74th** | **Rural and urban local government** *(Ch. 8)* |
| ⭐⭐⭐ **77th, 81st and 82nd** | **"Some amendments CLARIFYING AND EXPANDING THE SCOPE OF RESERVATIONS IN JOBS AND ADMISSIONS."** ⭐ **"AFTER 1992-93, AN OVERALL CONSENSUS EMERGED IN THE COUNTRY ABOUT THESE MEASURES, and therefore amendments regarding these measures WERE PASSED WITHOUT MUCH DIFFICULTY."** |

> ⭐⭐⭐ **The big analytical takeaway from the three groups:** the 106 amendments are **not** evidence of a defective Constitution. **Group 1 changed nothing substantive; Group 3 reflected genuine national consensus (voting age, panchayats, anti-defection, reservations); only Group 2 involved real constitutional conflict — and it was concentrated in 1970–1975.**

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 5 — CONTROVERSIAL AMENDMENTS

⭐ **"Our discussion so far should not create an impression that there has NEVER been any controversy over amending the Constitution. In fact, AMENDMENTS DURING THE PERIOD 1970 TO 1980 GENERATED A LOT OF LEGAL AND POLITICAL CONTROVERSY."**

⭐⭐ **"The parties that were in OPPOSITION during the period 1971-1976 saw many of these amendments as ATTEMPTS BY THE RULING PARTY TO SUBVERT THE CONSTITUTION."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The three most controversial amendments
> **"In particular, the 38TH, 39TH AND 42ND AMENDMENTS have been the MOST CONTROVERSIAL amendments so far. ⭐⭐⭐ THESE THREE AMENDMENTS WERE MADE IN THE BACKGROUND OF INTERNAL EMERGENCY DECLARED IN THE COUNTRY FROM JUNE 1975. THEY SOUGHT TO MAKE BASIC CHANGES IN MANY CRUCIAL PARTS OF THE CONSTITUTION."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The 42nd Amendment — what it actually did (a guaranteed exam question)
⭐ **"The 42nd amendment was particularly seen as a WIDE-RANGING amendment AFFECTING LARGE PARTS OF THE CONSTITUTION."**

| # | What the 42nd Amendment did |
|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"It was an ATTEMPT TO OVERRIDE THE RULING OF THE SUPREME COURT GIVEN IN THE KESAVANANDA CASE."** |
| **2** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"Even the DURATION OF THE LOK SABHA WAS EXTENDED FROM FIVE TO SIX YEARS."** |
| **3** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES ... were included in the Constitution by this amendment act."** *(Ch. 2: ten duties, unenforceable, and rights are **not** conditional on them.)* |
| **4** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"The 42nd amendment also PUT RESTRICTIONS ON THE REVIEW POWERS OF THE JUDICIARY."** |

⭐⭐⭐ **The scale of it — memorise these three figures:**
> **"It was said at that time that this amendment was PRACTICALLY A REWRITING OF MANY PARTS OF THE ORIGINAL CONSTITUTION. Do you know that this amendment made changes to (i) THE PREAMBLE, (ii) THE SEVENTH SCHEDULE of the Constitution and (iii) 53 ARTICLES of the Constitution?"**

⭐⭐⭐ **The circumstance that made it illegitimate:**
> **"MANY MPs BELONGING TO THE OPPOSITION PARTIES WERE IN JAIL WHEN THIS AMENDMENT WAS PASSED IN PARLIAMENT."**
>
> *(This is why the **special majority** was technically obtained yet politically hollow — Ambedkar's principle that a two-thirds majority proxies for **public opinion** fails when the opposition is imprisoned. **This is the single most important point to make about the 42nd Amendment.**)*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The restoration — the 43rd and 44th Amendments
> **"In this backdrop, ELECTIONS WERE HELD IN 1977 AND THE RULING PARTY (CONGRESS) WAS DEFEATED. The new government thought it necessary to RECONSIDER these controversial amendments, and ⭐⭐⭐ THROUGH THE 43RD AND 44TH AMENDMENTS, CANCELLED MOST OF THE CHANGES THAT WERE EFFECTED BY THE 38TH, 39TH AND THE 42ND AMENDMENTS. ⭐⭐ THE CONSTITUTIONAL BALANCE WAS RESTORED BY THESE AMENDMENTS."**

*(Recall from earlier chapters what the **44th Amendment** did: restored the President's power to require **one reconsideration** of advice (Ch. 4), and moved the **right to property out of Part III into Article 300A** (Ch. 2). It is the great **corrective** amendment.)*

> ⭐⭐ **The lesson worth drawing:** the Emergency episode is the strongest possible evidence for the **basic structure doctrine.** A ruling party with **352 Lok Sabha seats** and the opposition **in jail** could and did amend the Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles. **Only a judicially enforced outer limit could have stopped it — and the electorate then supplied the remedy in 1977.**

### ⭐ Box: Activity — two modern amendments to look up
*"Find out the amendments about the **RIGHT TO EDUCATION (RTE)** and the **GOODS AND SERVICES TAX (GST)**. What do you think is the importance of these amendments?"*
- ⭐ **The RTE amendment (86th) inserted education as a Fundamental Right** — a **Directive Principle converted into an enforceable right**, exactly the FR–DPSP relationship from Chapter 2.
- ⭐ **The GST amendment (101st)** altered the **distribution of taxation powers between the Union and the States** — so it is a **Group 2/3 amendment requiring State ratification** (a "distribution of powers" matter), and a landmark in **cooperative federalism** (Ch. 7).

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 6 — BASIC STRUCTURE AND THE EVOLUTION OF THE CONSTITUTION

⭐⭐ **"One thing that has had a LONG LASTING EFFECT on the evolution of the Indian Constitution is the THEORY OF THE BASIC STRUCTURE of the Constitution... the Judiciary advanced this theory in the famous case of KESAVANANDA BHARATI."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The three effects of the Kesavananda ruling (memorise as a trio)
| # | Effect |
|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"It has SET SPECIFIC LIMITS TO PARLIAMENT'S POWER TO AMEND the Constitution. It says that NO AMENDMENT CAN VIOLATE THE BASIC STRUCTURE of the Constitution."** |
| **2** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"It ALLOWS PARLIAMENT TO AMEND ANY AND ALL PARTS OF THE CONSTITUTION (within this limitation)."** ← *often forgotten; the doctrine is **permissive** as well as restrictive* |
| **3** | ⭐⭐⭐ **"It places the JUDICIARY AS THE FINAL AUTHORITY in deciding (i) IF AN AMENDMENT VIOLATES BASIC STRUCTURE and (ii) WHAT CONSTITUTES THE BASIC STRUCTURE."** |

### ⭐⭐⭐ Why the doctrine is *itself* proof of a living constitution
- **"The Supreme Court gave the Kesavananda ruling in 1973. In the past FOUR DECADES, this decision has GOVERNED ALL INTERPRETATIONS of the Constitution, and ALL INSTITUTIONS IN THE COUNTRY HAVE ACCEPTED the theory of basic structure."**
- ⭐⭐⭐ **The beautiful paradox:** *"**In fact, THE THEORY OF BASIC STRUCTURE IS ITSELF AN EXAMPLE OF A LIVING CONSTITUTION. THERE IS NO MENTION OF THIS THEORY IN THE CONSTITUTION. IT HAS EMERGED FROM JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION. Thus, THE JUDICIARY AND ITS INTERPRETATION HAVE PRACTICALLY AMENDED THE CONSTITUTION WITHOUT A FORMAL AMENDMENT.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **"ALL LIVING DOCUMENTS EVOLVE IN THIS MANNER — THROUGH DEBATES, ARGUMENTS, COMPETITION AND PRACTICAL POLITICS."**
- **"Since 1973, the Court has, in many cases, ELABORATED upon this theory and GIVEN INSTANCES of what constitutes the basic structure."**

⭐⭐⭐ **How the doctrine perfected the rigidity–flexibility balance:**
> **"In a sense, the basic structure doctrine has FURTHER CONSOLIDATED THE BALANCE BETWEEN RIGIDITY AND FLEXIBILITY: BY SAYING THAT CERTAIN PARTS CANNOT BE AMENDED, IT HAS UNDERLINED THE RIGID NATURE, WHILE BY ALLOWING AMENDMENTS TO ALL OTHERS IT HAS UNDERLINED THE FLEXIBLE NATURE OF THE AMENDING PROCESS."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Box: Review of the Constitution — the Venkatachaliah Commission
> - **"In the LATE NINETIES, efforts were made to REVIEW THE ENTIRE CONSTITUTION."**
> - ⭐⭐⭐ **"IN THE YEAR 2000, a COMMISSION TO REVIEW THE WORKING OF THE CONSTITUTION was appointed by the Government of India under the chairmanship of a RETIRED CHIEF JUSTICE OF THE SUPREME COURT, JUSTICE VENKATACHALIAH."**
> - ⭐⭐ **"OPPOSITION PARTIES AND MANY OTHER ORGANISATIONS BOYCOTTED THE COMMISSION."**
> - ⭐⭐⭐ **"While a lot of political controversy surrounded this commission, THE COMMISSION STUCK TO THE THEORY OF BASIC STRUCTURE AND DID NOT SUGGEST ANY MEASURES THAT WOULD ENDANGER THE BASIC STRUCTURE of the Constitution. ⭐⭐ THIS SHOWS THE SIGNIFICANCE OF THE BASIC STRUCTURE DOCTRINE IN OUR CONSTITUTIONAL PRACTICE."**
>
> **⭐ The point: even a government-appointed commission empowered to review the *whole* Constitution treated the basic structure as an outer limit it could not cross. That is how deeply the doctrine has been internalised.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Informal amendment by judicial interpretation — three more examples
⭐ **"There are many other examples of how JUDICIAL INTERPRETATION CHANGED OUR UNDERSTANDING of the Constitution:"**
1. ⭐⭐⭐ **"In many decisions the Supreme Court has held that RESERVATIONS IN JOBS AND EDUCATIONAL INSTITUTIONS CANNOT EXCEED FIFTY PER CENT OF THE TOTAL SEATS. THIS HAS NOW BECOME AN ACCEPTED PRINCIPLE."**
2. ⭐⭐⭐ **"In the case involving reservations for OTHER BACKWARD CLASSES, the Supreme Court INTRODUCED THE IDEA OF 'CREAMY LAYER' and ruled that PERSONS BELONGING TO THIS CATEGORY WERE NOT ENTITLED TO BENEFITS UNDER RESERVATIONS."**
3. ⭐⭐ **"In the same manner, the Judiciary has contributed to an INFORMAL AMENDMENT by interpreting various provisions concerning (i) RIGHT TO EDUCATION, (ii) RIGHT TO LIFE AND LIBERTY and (iii) the RIGHT TO FORM AND MANAGE MINORITY EDUCATIONAL INSTITUTIONS."**

⭐ **"These are instances of HOW RULINGS BY THE COURT CONTRIBUTE TO THE EVOLUTION OF THE CONSTITUTION."**

> *The chapter records the objection honestly:* *"It's all wrong. **First they say that an amendment requires consensus, and now we see that JUDGES CHANGE THE WHOLE MEANING of the Constitution.**"* — Exercise Q10 asks you to argue this out.

### ⭐⭐⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — correct or not?
| Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **"After the Basic Structure ruling, Parliament does NOT have power to amend the Constitution."** | ❌ **INCORRECT.** The ruling expressly **"allows Parliament to amend any and all parts of the Constitution"** — subject only to not violating the basic structure. |
| **"The Supreme Court has given a CLEAR LIST of the basic features which cannot be amended."** | ❌ **INCORRECT.** *"Nowhere does the Constitution say that such and such are part of the basic structure"*, and the Court has only **"given INSTANCES"** case by case. ⭐ **"There can still be debates about what constitutes basic structure."** |
| **"Judiciary has the power to decide whether an amendment violates basic structure or not."** | ✅ **CORRECT** — the third effect of the ruling; it made the judiciary **"the final authority."** |
| **"The Kesavananda Bharati ruling has set CLEAR LIMITS on Parliament's power to amend."** | ⚠️ **Partly correct — the distinction matters.** It set ⭐ **"SPECIFIC limits"** (an amendment must not violate the basic structure) — but **not CLEAR ones**, since the *contents* of the basic structure remain open and are decided case by case. **"Specific" ≠ "clear."** |

## ⭐⭐⭐ PART 7 — WHAT MAKES IT A "LIVING DOCUMENT"?

### ⭐⭐⭐ The meaning
> **"Almost like a LIVING BEING, this document KEEPS RESPONDING TO THE SITUATIONS AND CIRCUMSTANCES ARISING FROM TIME TO TIME. Like a living being, THE CONSTITUTION RESPONDS TO EXPERIENCE."**

⭐⭐⭐ **The answer to the opening riddle:** *"**Even after so many changes in the society, the Constitution continues to work effectively because of this ability (i) TO BE DYNAMIC, (ii) TO BE OPEN TO INTERPRETATIONS and (iii) THE ABILITY TO RESPOND TO THE CHANGING SITUATION.**"*

⭐⭐ **"THIS IS A HALLMARK OF A DEMOCRATIC CONSTITUTION. In a democracy, PRACTICES AND IDEAS KEEP EVOLVING OVER TIME and the society ENGAGES IN EXPERIMENTS according to these. ⭐⭐⭐ A CONSTITUTION WHICH PROTECTS DEMOCRACY AND YET ALLOWS FOR EVOLUTION OF NEW PRACTICES BECOMES NOT ONLY DURABLE BUT ALSO THE OBJECT OF RESPECT FROM THE CITIZENS."**

⭐ **"The important point is: HAS THE CONSTITUTION BEEN ABLE TO PROTECT ITSELF AND PROTECT DEMOCRACY?"**

### ⭐⭐⭐ The most serious question since 1950 — parliamentary supremacy
⭐ **"In terms of constitutional-legal issues, THE MOST SERIOUS QUESTION THAT CAME UP AGAIN AND AGAIN FROM 1950 WAS ABOUT THE SUPREMACY OF THE PARLIAMENT."**

⭐⭐ **The two sides of the tension:**
- **"In a parliamentary democracy, THE PARLIAMENT REPRESENTS THE PEOPLE, and therefore IT IS EXPECTED TO HAVE AN UPPER HAND OVER BOTH EXECUTIVE AND JUDICIARY."**
- ⭐ **"AT THE SAME TIME, THERE IS THE TEXT OF THE CONSTITUTION, AND IT HAS GIVEN POWERS TO OTHER ORGANS of the government. Therefore, THE SUPREMACY OF THE PARLIAMENT HAS TO OPERATE WITHIN THIS FRAMEWORK."**

⭐⭐⭐ **The wider point about democracy — a superb passage:**
> **"DEMOCRACY IS NOT ONLY ABOUT VOTES AND PEOPLE'S REPRESENTATION. IT IS ALSO ABOUT THE PRINCIPLE OF RULE OF LAW. DEMOCRACY IS ALSO ABOUT DEVELOPING INSTITUTIONS AND WORKING THROUGH THESE INSTITUTIONS. ALL THE POLITICAL INSTITUTIONS MUST BE RESPONSIBLE TO THE PEOPLE AND MAINTAIN A BALANCE WITH EACH OTHER."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Contribution of the Judiciary — the two positions stated fairly
| **Parliament's position** | **The Judiciary's position** |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **"The Parliament thought that IT HAD THE POWER AND RESPONSIBILITY TO MAKE LAWS (AND AMENDMENTS) FOR FURTHERING THE INTERESTS OF THE POOR, BACKWARD AND THE NEEDY."** | ⭐⭐ **"The Judiciary insisted that ALL THIS HAS TO TAKE PLACE WITHIN THE FRAMEWORK PROVIDED BY THE CONSTITUTION, and PRO-PEOPLE MEASURES SHOULD NOT BYPASS LEGAL PROCEDURES"** |
| | ⭐⭐⭐ **The reason — the killer argument:** *"**BECAUSE, ONCE YOU BYPASS LAWS EVEN WITH GOOD INTENTIONS, THAT CAN GIVE AN EXCUSE TO THE POWER HOLDERS TO USE THEIR POWER ARBITRARILY. AND DEMOCRACY IS AS MUCH ABOUT CHECKS ON ARBITRARY USE OF POWER AS IT IS ABOUT THE WELL-BEING OF THE PEOPLE.**"* |

⭐⭐ **"THE SUCCESS OF THE WORKING OF THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION LIES IN RESOLVING THESE TENSIONS."**

### ⭐⭐⭐ LETTER vs SPIRIT — the intellectual heart of the chapter
⭐⭐ **"The Judiciary, in its famous Kesavananda ruling, FOUND A WAY OUT of the existing complications BY TURNING TO THE SPIRIT OF THE CONSTITUTION RATHER THAN ITS LETTER."**

⭐ **"If you read the Constitution, you will NOT FIND ANY MENTION of the 'basic structure'. Nowhere does the Constitution say that such and such are part of the basic structure. IN THIS SENSE, THE 'BASIC STRUCTURE' THEORY IS THE INVENTION OF THE JUDICIARY."**

⭐⭐ **The two questions:** *"**How did it invent such a non-existent thing? And how is it that ALL OTHER INSTITUTIONS HAVE ACCEPTED THIS during the past four decades?**"*

⭐⭐⭐ **The answer — memorise this passage:**
> **"THEREIN LIES THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN LETTER AND SPIRIT. The Court came to the conclusion that IN READING A TEXT OR DOCUMENT, WE MUST RESPECT THE INTENT BEHIND THAT DOCUMENT. ⭐⭐⭐ A MERE TEXT OF THE LAW IS LESS IMPORTANT THAN THE SOCIAL CIRCUMSTANCES AND ASPIRATIONS THAT HAVE PRODUCED THAT LAW OR DOCUMENT. The Court was looking at the basic structure as ⭐⭐⭐ SOMETHING WITHOUT WHICH THE CONSTITUTION CANNOT BE IMAGINED AT ALL. This is an instance of TRYING TO BALANCE THE LETTER AND THE SPIRIT OF THE CONSTITUTION."**

> ⭐⭐ **The margin comment gives the best intuitive test of "spirit" available:** *"Of course, **if there are no rights and no elections, the Constitution won't make much sense. And if there is no well-being, elections and rights won't make sense.** Is this how we understand the 'spirit' of our Constitution?"*
>
> **That is exactly the basic structure test: what cannot be removed without the Constitution ceasing to be *this* Constitution at all.**

### ⭐⭐⭐ Maturity of the Political Leadership
⭐ **"In the background of the FIERCE CONTROVERSY THAT RAGED BETWEEN 1967 AND 1973, Parliament and the Executive ALSO REALISED THAT A BALANCED AND LONG TERM VIEW WAS NECESSARY."**

⭐⭐⭐ **The sequence after Kesavananda (learn this exact chain):**
1. **"After the Supreme Court gave the ruling in the Kesavananda case, SOME ATTEMPTS WERE MADE TO ASK THE COURT TO RECONSIDER ITS RULING."**
2. **"WHEN THESE FAILED, THE 42ND AMENDMENT WAS MADE AND PARLIAMENTARY SUPREMACY WAS ASSERTED."**
3. ⭐⭐⭐ **"BUT THE COURT AGAIN REPEATED ITS EARLIER STAND IN THE MINERVA MILLS CASE (1980)."**
4. ⭐⭐ **"Therefore, EVEN FOUR DECADES AFTER the ruling in the Kesavananda case, THIS RULING HAS DOMINATED OUR INTERPRETATION OF THE CONSTITUTION."**

⭐⭐⭐ **The acceptance:** *"**POLITICAL PARTIES, POLITICAL LEADERS, THE GOVERNMENT, AND PARLIAMENT ACCEPTED THE IDEA OF INVIOLABLE BASIC STRUCTURE. EVEN WHEN THERE WAS TALK ABOUT 'REVIEW' OF THE CONSTITUTION, THAT EXERCISE COULD NOT CROSS THE LIMITS SET BY THE THEORY OF THE BASIC STRUCTURE.**"*

*(**Kesavananda (1973) → 42nd Amendment (1976) → Minerva Mills (1980) → Venkatachaliah Commission (2000).** Four data points across three decades, all confirming the doctrine.)*

### ⭐⭐⭐ The shared vision that has held
- ⭐⭐ **"When the Constitution was made, LEADERS AND PEOPLE OF OUR COUNTRY SHARED A COMMON VISION OF INDIA. In NEHRU'S FAMOUS SPEECH at the time of independence, this vision was described as a 'TRYST WITH DESTINY'."**
- ⭐⭐⭐ **The four elements of that vision, as stated in the Constituent Assembly:**
  1. **"DIGNITY AND FREEDOM OF THE INDIVIDUAL"**
  2. **"SOCIAL AND ECONOMIC EQUALITY"**
  3. **"WELL-BEING OF ALL PEOPLE"**
  4. **"UNITY BASED ON NATIONAL INTEGRITY"**
- ⭐⭐ **"THIS VISION HAS NOT DISAPPEARED. PEOPLE AND LEADERS ALIKE HOLD TO THE VISION AND HOPE TO REALIZE IT. Therefore, the Constitution, based on this vision, HAS REMAINED AN OBJECT OF RESPECT AND AUTHORITY EVEN AFTER HALF A CENTURY. ⭐⭐⭐ THE BASIC VALUES GOVERNING OUR PUBLIC IMAGINATION REMAIN INTACT."**

> ### ⭐⭐ The dissenting prediction that was falsified
> *"Even within the Constituent Assembly, there were some members who felt that this Constitution was not suited to the Indian situation:*
> *"**The ideals on which this draft constitution is framed HAVE NO MANIFEST RELATION TO THE FUNDAMENTAL SPIRIT OF INDIA. ...this Constitution ...WOULD NOT PROVE SUITABLE AND WOULD BREAK DOWN SOON AFTER BEING BROUGHT INTO OPERATION.**"*
> — **Lakshminarayan Sahu, CAD, Vol. XI, p. 613, 17 November 1949**
>
> ⭐⭐ **Use this quotation as the counterpoint to the whole chapter: the prediction was that the Constitution would "break down soon." Seventy-five years and 106 amendments later, it has not. *That* is the achievement the chapter is explaining.**

- ⭐⭐ **"There can STILL be debates about what constitutes basic structure. THERE IS NOTHING WRONG IN SUCH DEBATES."**
- ⭐⭐⭐ **"We must remember that POLITICS IN A DEMOCRACY IS NECESSARILY FULL OF DEBATES AND DIFFERENCES. THAT IS A SIGN OF DIVERSITY, LIVELINESS AND OPENNESS. DEMOCRACY WELCOMES DEBATES."**

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Living document** | A constitution that **keeps responding to situations and circumstances**, **responds to experience**, is **dynamic and open to interpretation** — as opposed to *"a closed and static rulebook"* |
| **Flexible** | **Open to changes** — a constitution that can be very easily changed |
| **Rigid** | **Resistant to changes** — very difficult to amend. ⭐ **India's Constitution combines both** |
| **Article 368** | Empowers Parliament, **"in exercise of its CONSTITUENT POWER"**, to amend by **addition, variation or repeal** |
| **Constituent power** | The special power of Parliament to amend the Constitution, distinct from its ordinary legislative power |
| **"By law"** | The wording (as in **Articles 2 and 3**) signalling that an article can be amended by **simple majority, without recourse to Article 368** |
| ⭐ **Special majority** | **(i) At least HALF of the TOTAL STRENGTH of the House**, **AND (ii) TWO-THIRDS of those actually voting** — required **separately in both Houses**, with ⭐ **no joint session** |
| **Ratification by States** | Required where an amendment touches the **distribution of powers between States and the Centre**, or **representation**; needs **legislatures of half the States**, by **simple majority** |
| **Parliamentary sovereignty** | The principle underlying the amendment procedure: **only elected representatives may finally decide on amendments** — hence **no referendum, no outside commission** |
| **Referendum** | A direct popular vote on an amendment — used in **Switzerland, Russia, Italy**; ⭐ **not used in India** |
| **Basic structure** | Features **without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all**; **no amendment may violate them**; ⭐ **nowhere mentioned in the Constitution** — a **judicial invention** from ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** |
| ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** | The case in which the Court **repeated its Kesavananda stand** after the 42nd Amendment had asserted parliamentary supremacy |
| **Venkatachaliah Commission (2000)** | The Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution, chaired by a **retired Chief Justice**; **boycotted by the opposition**; ⭐ **stuck to the basic structure theory** |
| **Creamy layer** | The judicially introduced category of **OBC persons NOT entitled to reservation benefits** |
| **Letter vs spirit** | The interpretive distinction the Court used: **"a mere text of the law is less important than the social circumstances and aspirations that have produced that law"** |
| **Tryst with destiny** | Nehru's description, in his independence speech, of the shared national vision underlying the Constitution |
| **Informal amendment** | Change in the meaning of the Constitution achieved through **judicial interpretation without any formal amendment** |

## Quick Recap (16 points)
1. ⭐ **Nations rewrite constitutions in response to changed circumstances, changed ideas, or political upheavals.** **The USSR had four constitutions in 74 years (1918, 1924, 1936, 1977)**; after the **1991** collapse the **Russian Federation adopted a new one in 1993.** **France had five republics — 1793, 1848, 1875, 1946, 1958.** The **USA has amended its constitution only 27 times in over 200 years.**
2. ⭐⭐ **India: adopted 26 November 1949, in force 26 January 1950 — and the same Constitution still governs.** Not because it is perfect: *"no constitution can provide for all eventualities."* **It survives because (i) it accepts modification through amendment, and (ii) its working has allowed flexibility of interpretation, with both political practice and judicial rulings showing maturity.** Hence **"a living document rather than a closed and static rulebook."**
3. ⭐⭐ **Every constitution has both something contemporary and something of durable importance.** It is **made by human beings**, reflects a society's **dreams and aspirations**, and is also **"an instrument that societies create for themselves."**
4. ⭐⭐⭐ **The framers balanced the two extremes:** they **placed the Constitution above ordinary law** yet **recognised it may need modification** — and knew that **on many matters there were differences of opinion**, so that as society settled, provisions would need to change. **"Our Constitution is not a static document, it is not the final word about everything; it is not unalterable."**
5. ⭐⭐ **Article 368 gives Parliament "constituent power" to amend by addition, variation or repeal.** **Flexible = open to change; rigid = resistant.** **India combines both.**
6. ⭐⭐⭐ **Four reasons for having different procedures:** the Constitution **could not be free of errors** and mistakes should be easily correctable; some provisions were **temporary**; but **States' rights and powers could not be changed without their consent** in a federal polity; and **features central to the spirit of the Constitution had to be made rigid.**
7. ⭐⭐⭐ **Three methods:** **(i) simple majority** for articles worded **"by law"** (e.g. **Articles 2 and 3** on admitting new States and increasing a State's area) — *"no difference at all between an amendment and an ordinary law"*; **(ii) special majority in both Houses separately**; **(iii) special majority plus ratification by half the States.**
8. ⭐⭐⭐ **Four features of the process:** all amendments are **initiated only in Parliament** (States cannot propose one); **no outside agency** such as a constitution commission is needed; **no referendum** is required; and ⭐ **the President has NO power to send an amendment bill back for reconsideration.** ⭐⭐ **The governing principle: "only elected representatives of the people are empowered to... take final decisions on the question of amendments" — parliamentary sovereignty.**
9. ⭐⭐⭐ **Special majority = two tests, both of which must be met:** **at least half of the total strength of the House**, **and two-thirds of those actually voting**, **in each House separately, with no joint session.** ⭐ **With 545 Lok Sabha members, the floor is 273** — so even if only **300** vote, **273** must support it; if **400** vote, two-thirds is 267 but the answer is still **273**, because the higher requirement binds.
10. ⭐⭐⭐ **Why special majority:** a government with a thin majority **can pass ordinary laws and the budget alone, but must take opposition parties into confidence to amend the Constitution.** ⭐ **Ambedkar: if those dissatisfied cannot even obtain two-thirds, "their dissatisfaction with the Constitution cannot be deemed to be shared by the general public"** — the majority is a **proxy for public opinion.** Worldwide, two principles operate: **special majority (USA two-thirds; South Africa and Russia three-fourths for some amendments)** and **people's participation (Switzerland — where people can even initiate an amendment — plus Russia and Italy).** **India uses only the first.**
11. ⭐⭐⭐ **State ratification** is needed for amendments touching the **distribution of powers between States and the Centre** or **representation**, because *"powers of the States must not be at the mercy of the central government."* But flexibility is preserved: **only half the States, and only by simple majority.** ⭐ **Note the trap: changing State BOUNDARIES needs neither — Article 3 says "by law."**
12. ⭐⭐⭐ **On 26 January 2024 the Constitution completed 74 years and had been amended 106 times.** **1970–1990 saw the most amendments**; **ten amendments came in three years (1974–76)** under **Congress domination (352 Lok Sabha seats)**, and **another ten in three years (2001–03)** under **coalition politics with bitter BJP-versus-opposition rivalry.** ⭐ **Hence: "the incidence of amendments is not dependent merely on the nature of majority of the ruling party alone"** — and **barring the first decade, every decade has seen a steady stream.** ⭐ **The right method is to examine the CHANGES, not the NUMBER.**
13. ⭐⭐⭐ **Three groups of amendments.** **Group 1 — technical/administrative**, *"amendments only in the legal sense"*: the **15th** (High Court judges' retirement age **60 → 62**), the **54th** (judges' salaries), the **six repeated extensions of SC/ST reservation** every ten years, and the **Article 74(1)** amendment making the Council of Ministers' advice binding — which *"did not make any difference because that is exactly what has been happening all through."* **Group 2 — differing interpretations**, where **Parliament amended to overcome adverse judicial rulings**, especially **1970–1975**, on the **FR–DPSP relationship**, the **right to private property**, and the **scope of the amending power.** **Group 3 — political consensus**, especially **post-1984**: the **52nd and 91st** (anti-defection), the **61st** (voting age **21 → 18**), the **73rd and 74th** (local government), and the **77th, 81st and 82nd** on reservations in jobs and admissions, which passed easily after **an overall consensus emerged in 1992-93.**
14. ⭐⭐⭐ **The controversial amendments: the 38th, 39th and 42nd**, made **against the background of the internal Emergency declared from June 1975**, and seen by the opposition as **attempts to subvert the Constitution.** The **42nd** tried to **override *Kesavananda***, **extended the Lok Sabha's term from five to six years**, inserted the **Fundamental Duties**, and **restricted the judiciary's review powers** — altering the **Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles**, while **many opposition MPs were in jail.** ⭐ **After the Congress was defeated in the 1977 elections, the 43rd and 44th Amendments cancelled most of those changes and "the constitutional balance was restored."**
15. ⭐⭐⭐ ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** had three effects: it **set specific limits on the amending power**; it **allowed Parliament to amend any and all parts within that limit**; and it made the **judiciary the final authority on whether an amendment violates the basic structure and on what the basic structure is.** ⭐⭐ **The doctrine is itself proof of a living constitution — "there is no mention of this theory in the Constitution... the Judiciary and its interpretation have practically amended the Constitution without a formal amendment"** — and it **perfected the rigidity–flexibility balance.** Confirmed in ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** after the 42nd Amendment, and respected even by the **Venkatachaliah Commission (2000)**, which was **boycotted by the opposition** yet **"stuck to the theory of basic structure."** Other **informal amendments** by interpretation: the **50 per cent ceiling on reservations**, the **creamy layer** exclusion for OBCs, and readings of the **right to education, right to life and liberty, and minority educational institutions.**
16. ⭐⭐⭐ **The deepest issue since 1950 has been parliamentary supremacy** — Parliament represents the people, yet **"the text of the Constitution... has given powers to other organs"**, so supremacy must operate within that framework. ⭐ *"Democracy is not only about votes and people's representation. It is also about the principle of rule of law... developing institutions and working through these institutions."* **Parliament claimed the responsibility to legislate for the poor and backward; the judiciary insisted this happen within constitutional procedure, "because once you bypass laws even with good intentions, that can give an excuse to the power holders to use their power arbitrarily. And democracy is as much about checks on arbitrary use of power as it is about the well-being of the people."** ⭐⭐ **The resolution came through the distinction between LETTER and SPIRIT: "a mere text of the law is less important than the social circumstances and aspirations that have produced that law"**, and the basic structure is **"something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all."** The shared vision — Nehru's **"tryst with destiny"**, and the Assembly's commitment to the **dignity and freedom of the individual, social and economic equality, well-being of all, and unity based on national integrity** — **"has not disappeared"**, falsifying **Lakshminarayan Sahu's** prediction that the Constitution **"would break down soon after being brought into operation."** ⭐⭐⭐ **"But in the last instance, the final authority lies with the people."**

## 60-Second Revision
**USSR 4 constitutions in 74 yrs (1918/24/36/77) → Russia 1993. FRANCE five republics: 1793, 1848, 1875, 1946, 1958. USA 27 amendments in 200+ yrs. INDIA: adopted 26 Nov 1949, in force 26 Jan 1950, same Constitution — 106 amendments in 74 yrs (as on 26 Jan 2024).** **Survives via (i) amendment + (ii) flexibility of interpretation → "living document, not a closed and static rulebook."** **Art 368 = constituent power: addition, variation, repeal.** **THREE methods: simple majority ("by law" — Arts 2 & 3) | special majority | special majority + half the States.** **SPECIAL MAJORITY = half of TOTAL STRENGTH *and* two-thirds of those VOTING, in each House SEPARATELY, NO joint session. LS 545 → floor of 273; even if 400 vote, answer is still 273 (higher test binds).** **Only Parliament initiates; NO outside commission; NO referendum; PRESIDENT CANNOT return an amendment bill → parliamentary sovereignty.** **Ambedkar: can't get 2/3 → "dissatisfaction cannot be deemed to be shared by the general public" = majority proxies public opinion.** **World: special majority (USA 2/3; South Africa & Russia 3/4) vs people's participation (SWITZERLAND can INITIATE; Russia, Italy).** **State ratification for distribution of powers + representation; only HALF the States, only SIMPLE majority. TRAP: State boundaries need NEITHER (Art 3 "by law").** **1970-90 = most amendments. 10 in 1974-76 (Congress, 352 LS seats) AND 10 in 2001-03 (coalitions, BJP rivalry) → "not dependent merely on the nature of majority."** **THREE GROUPS: (1) TECHNICAL — 15th (HC judges 60→62), 54th (salaries), SIX SC/ST extensions, Art 74(1) clarification; (2) DIFFERING INTERPRETATIONS 1970-75 — FR vs DPSP, right to property, amending power; (3) CONSENSUS post-1984 — 52nd & 91st (anti-defection), 61st (21→18), 73rd & 74th, 77th/81st/82nd (reservations, consensus after 1992-93).** **CONTROVERSIAL: 38th, 39th, 42nd — Emergency from JUNE 1975. 42nd: override Kesavananda + LS term 5→6 yrs + Fundamental Duties + restricted judicial review; changed the PREAMBLE, SEVENTH SCHEDULE and 53 ARTICLES; opposition MPs IN JAIL. 1977 election → Congress defeated → 43rd & 44th cancelled most of it → "constitutional balance was restored."** **KESAVANANDA 1973 three effects: limits the amending power / allows amendment of any part within that limit / makes the judiciary the final authority on what basic structure is. The doctrine is itself a living-constitution example — nowhere in the text. MINERVA MILLS 1980 repeated it. VENKATACHALIAH Commission 2000 (retired CJ, boycotted by opposition) stuck to it.** **Other informal amendments: 50% reservation ceiling, CREAMY LAYER, right to education, life and liberty, minority institutions.** **LETTER vs SPIRIT: "a mere text of the law is less important than the social circumstances and aspirations that have produced it"; basic structure = "something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all."** **Nehru's "tryst with destiny"; vision = dignity & freedom of the individual, social & economic equality, well-being of all, unity based on national integrity. Lakshminarayan Sahu predicted it "would break down soon" — it didn't.** **"In the last instance, the final authority lies with the people."**

## Try These Questions

### NCERT Exercises (with answers/pointers)
1. ⭐ **Why does a constitution need to be amended from time to time?** → ✅ **"Circumstances change and require suitable changes in the constitution."**
   *(Reject the others: *"a document written at one point of time becomes outdated"* is too sweeping — the framers **"provided for many solutions for future situations"** and the **basic framework remains suited to the country**; *"every generation should have a constitution of its own liking"* would give **"no security, predictability or identity"** and is contradicted by the special-majority requirement; *"it must reflect the philosophy of the existing government"* is flatly wrong — a constitution is placed **above** ordinary law precisely so that a government of the day cannot remould it, which is why the **42nd Amendment** was so controversial.)*
2. **True / False:**
   | Statement | Verdict |
   |---|---|
   | **a.** *The President cannot send back an amendment bill for reconsideration of Parliament* | ✅ **TRUE** — *"in this case, the President has no powers to send it back for reconsideration."* |
   | **b.** *Elected representatives alone have the power to amend the Constitution* | ✅ **TRUE** — *"only elected representatives of the people are empowered to consider and take final decisions"*; **no referendum, no outside commission.** |
   | **c.** *The Judiciary cannot initiate the process of constitutional amendment but can effectively change the Constitution by interpreting it differently* | ✅ **TRUE** — amendments are **initiated only in Parliament**, yet *"the Judiciary and its interpretation have practically amended the Constitution without a formal amendment"* (basic structure, 50% ceiling, creamy layer). |
   | **d.** *Parliament can amend any section of the Constitution* | ⚠️ **FALSE as stated** — *"no amendment can violate the basic structure."* It can amend **any and all parts *within that limitation*.** |
3. ⭐⭐ **Who is involved in amending the Indian Constitution, and how?**
   | Actor | Involved? | How |
   |---|---|---|
   | **a. Voters** | ❌ **NO** | ⭐ **"No referendum is required for ratification of the amendment."** Voters act only **indirectly**, by electing the representatives — and, as **1977** showed, by **retrospectively judging** amendments at the polls. |
   | **b. President of India** | ✅ **YES, but only formally** | Gives **assent**, but ⭐ **has NO power to return an amendment bill** for reconsideration. |
   | **c. State Legislatures** | ✅ **YES, for some amendments** | Must ratify — by **simple majority in half the States** — where the amendment touches the **distribution of powers between States and Centre** or **representation.** They **cannot initiate** an amendment. |
   | **d. Parliament** | ✅ **YES — centrally** | **All amendments are initiated only in Parliament**, and must pass **both Houses separately by special majority**, with **no joint session.** |
   | **e. Governors** | ❌ **NO** | They have **no role whatsoever** in the amendment process. |
   | **f. Judiciary** | ✅ **YES — indirectly but decisively** | **Cannot initiate**, but **decides whether an amendment violates the basic structure** and **what the basic structure is** — and effects **informal amendment** through interpretation. |
4. ⭐⭐ **Why was the 42nd Amendment so controversial?**
   | Reason | Verdict |
   |---|---|
   | **a.** *It was made during national emergency, and the declaration of that emergency was itself controversial* | ✅ **YES** — *"made in the background of internal emergency declared in the country from June 1975"*, with **many opposition MPs in jail.** |
   | **b.** *It was made without the support of special majority* | ❌ **NO** — the special majority **was** obtained; the objection is that it was obtained while **the opposition was imprisoned**, so the majority no longer proxied public opinion (Ambedkar's principle). |
   | **c.** *It was made without ratification by State legislatures* | ❌ **NO** — this is not among the chapter's grounds of controversy. |
   | **d.** *It contained provisions which were controversial* | ✅ **YES** — it tried to **override *Kesavananda***, **extended the Lok Sabha's term from five to six years**, inserted **Fundamental Duties**, **restricted judicial review**, and altered the **Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles.** |
5. ⭐ **Which is NOT a reasonable explanation of the legislature–judiciary conflict over amendments?** → ❌ **(d) "Legislature cannot be entrusted to protect the rights of the citizens."**
   *Why it fails: it is not an *explanation* but an *accusation*, and it contradicts the chapter's account. Parliament acted from a genuine belief that it had **"the power and responsibility to make laws for furthering the interests of the poor, backward and the needy"**; and the whole amendment procedure rests on **parliamentary sovereignty** and the trust that **only elected representatives may decide.* The other four are all sound: **(a)** different interpretations are possible; **(b)** *"politics in a democracy is necessarily full of debates and differences"*; **(c)** the Constitution deliberately grades its provisions by difficulty of amendment; **(e)** the judiciary decides **constitutionality**, not the political need for a measure.*
6. ⭐⭐ **Correct or incorrect statements about the basic structure theory?**
   | Statement | Verdict |
   |---|---|
   | **a.** *Constitution specifies the basic tenets* | ❌ **INCORRECT.** ⭐ **Correction:** *"There is NO mention of this theory in the Constitution"* — the basic structure is **nowhere specified in the text**; it **emerged from judicial interpretation.** |
   | **b.** *Legislature can amend all parts of the Constitution except the basic structure* | ✅ **CORRECT** — exactly the ruling's first two effects. |
   | **c.** *Judiciary has defined which aspects can be termed basic structure and which cannot* | ⚠️ **INCORRECT as stated.** ⭐ **Correction:** the judiciary has **"given INSTANCES"** case by case and **reserved to itself the power to decide** — it has **not produced a definitive list**, and *"there can still be debates about what constitutes basic structure."* |
   | **d.** *The theory found its first expression in the Kesavananda Bharati case and has been discussed in subsequent judgments* | ✅ **CORRECT** — **1973**, reaffirmed in ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** and elaborated since. |
   | **e.** *This theory has increased the powers of the judiciary and has come to be accepted by different political parties and the government* | ✅ **CORRECT** — it made the judiciary **"the final authority"**, and *"political parties, political leaders, the government, and Parliament accepted the idea of inviolable basic structure"*, as even the **Venkatachaliah Commission** demonstrated. |
7. ⭐⭐ **What follows from the many amendments made during 2000–2003?**
   | Conclusion | Verdict |
   |---|---|
   | **a.** *Judiciary did not interfere in the amendments made during this period* | ❌ Not supported — the chapter draws no such inference. |
   | **b.** *One political party had a strong majority during this period* | ❌ **FALSE — the opposite is true.** It was *"a period marked by coalition politics... different parties in power in different States"* with *"bitter rivalry between the BJP and its opponents."* |
   | **c.** *There was strong pressure from the public in favour of certain amendments* | ⚠️ **Plausible but not the chapter's stated reason.** The mechanism given is **consensus among the political parties** reflecting *"the prevailing political philosophy and aspirations of the society."* |
   | **d.** *There were no real differences among the parties during this time* | ❌ **FALSE** — there was **"bitter rivalry"** and **"political turbulence."** |
   | ✅ **e.** *The amendments were of a non-controversial nature and parties had an agreement on the subject of amendments* | ✅ **CORRECT — this is the chapter's own explanation:** *"many of these amendments were based on an EVOLVING CONSENSUS on certain issues... a series of amendments IN SPITE OF the political turbulence."* |
8. ⭐⭐ **Explain the reason for requiring a special majority for amending the Constitution.** → *Because the Constitution is **placed above ordinary law** and must be protected **"from unnecessary and frequent changes"**, while remaining amendable. The double test — **half the total strength AND two-thirds of those voting, in each House separately, with no joint session** — means that **a government with a thin majority can pass ordinary laws and the budget alone but "would need to take at least some opposition parties into confidence"** to amend the Constitution. Hence *"the basic principle... is that it should be based on broad support among the political parties and parliamentarians."* ⭐ **And Ambedkar supplies the deeper justification: the two-thirds bar is a proxy for PUBLIC OPINION — if those dissatisfied cannot even reach it, "their dissatisfaction... cannot be deemed to be shared by the general public."** The **42nd Amendment** shows the limit of the device: the arithmetic can be satisfied while the principle is defeated, if **the opposition is in jail.**
9. ⭐⭐ **Amendments made because of differing interpretations by Judiciary and Parliament — explain with examples.** → *The mechanism: *"when these clashed, the Parliament had to insert an amendment underlining one particular interpretation as the authentic one"*, and *"many times the Parliament did not agree with the judicial interpretation and therefore sought to amend the Constitution to overcome the ruling."* **Concentrated in 1970–1975.** **The three issues:** the relationship between **Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles**; the scope of the **right to private property**; and the scope of **Parliament's power to amend.** **Examples:** Parliament amended to enable **zamindari abolition and land reform** after the Court held that fundamental rights could not be abridged (Ch. 2); the **44th Amendment** finally moved the **right to property out of Part III into Article 300A**; and the **42nd Amendment** was an explicit **"attempt to override the ruling of the Supreme Court given in the Kesavananda case"**, also **restricting the review powers of the judiciary** — to which the Court responded in ***Minerva Mills* (1980).** Also note the reverse direction: the **Article 74(1)** amendment merely **codified** what practice already was.*
10. ⭐⭐⭐ **"If amending power is with the elected representatives, the judiciary should NOT have the power to decide the validity of amendments." Do you agree? (100 words)**
    *A balanced answer: **The objection has real force** — *"only elected representatives of the people are empowered to... take final decisions on amendments"*, and **parliamentary sovereignty is the basis of the amendment procedure.** An unelected court striking down an amendment passed by a special majority in both Houses appears to override the people's representatives, and the basic structure is **"the invention of the Judiciary"**, found nowhere in the text.*
    ***Yet the Emergency answers it.** With **352 Lok Sabha seats** and **opposition MPs in jail**, the 42nd Amendment altered the **Preamble, the Seventh Schedule and 53 articles**, extended the Lok Sabha's own term, and **curtailed judicial review** — showing that a special majority can be procedurally correct and substantively arbitrary. **"Democracy is as much about checks on arbitrary use of power as it is about the well-being of the people."** The doctrine does not disable Parliament — it *"allows Parliament to amend any and all parts"* short of destroying **"something without which the Constitution cannot be imagined at all."** And the settlement is not judicial fiat but democratic: **all parties, governments and even the Venkatachaliah Commission have accepted it.** ⭐ **So: yes, the judiciary should retain the power — but exercised sparingly, on the spirit of the Constitution, with the final authority still lying with the people, as 1977 proved.**
