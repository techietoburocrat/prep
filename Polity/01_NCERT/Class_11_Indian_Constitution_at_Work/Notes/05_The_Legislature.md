# Chapter 5: Legislature

## 🎯 Chapter Outcome — what you should walk away with

> **The one thing this chapter exists to teach:** Parliament matters not chiefly because it makes laws, but because it is **the only body that can make and unmake a government**. That is why it is called the most representative organ and the centre of the democratic process.

**After reading it, here is what you now understand:**

1. **Why two Houses** — to represent all sections *and* all regions, and above all so that a decision taken in haste in one House can be reconsidered in the other. Only **six States** are bicameral: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana and Uttar Pradesh.
2. **How the Rajya Sabha is built** — indirect election, seats allotted by **population** (not the American equal-representation model), a six-year term with a third retiring every two years, which makes it a **permanent House** that is never dissolved, plus 12 members nominated by the President.
3. **The contrast with Germany's Bundesrat**, whose members are appointed state ministers voting as a bloc on their government's instructions — a genuinely state-representing chamber, unlike our Rajya Sabha, whose members represent their **parties** more than their States.
4. **What only the Lok Sabha can do** — money bills and removing the government — and *why*: the Rajya Sabha is elected by MLAs, not by the people, so it may criticise a government but cannot remove it.
5. **The money-bill rule precisely** (Article 109): the Rajya Sabha may suggest changes but cannot reject, and **fourteen days of inaction means the bill is deemed passed**. There is no joint session for a money bill — and in the few joint sessions ever held, the outcome has always favoured the Lok Sabha.
6. **Question Hour vs Zero Hour** — in Question Hour ministers **must** reply; in Zero Hour any matter may be raised but ministers **need not** reply.
7. **Committees as "miniature legislatures"**, and the CAG–PAC link that turns financial control into policy control.
8. **Anti-defection (52nd Amendment 1985, modified by the 91st)** — what counts as defection, that the presiding officer decides, and the twist the chapter insists on: it ended up **empowering party bosses** rather than curbing defection.

**Self-check — answer these three without looking:**
1. Why can the Rajya Sabha criticise a government but not remove it?
2. The Rajya Sabha simply sits on a money bill and does nothing. What happens?
3. Name the three acts that count as defection under the anti-defection law.

## The Big Idea
⭐ **"Legislature is not merely a law making body. Lawmaking is but one of the functions of the legislature. It is the CENTRE of all democratic political process."**

*"It is packed with action; walkouts, protests, demonstration, unanimity, concern and co-operation. **All these serve very vital purposes.** Indeed, a genuine democracy is inconceivable without a representative, efficient and effective legislature. The legislature also helps people in **holding the representatives accountable** — this is, indeed, the very basis of representative democracy."*

### ⭐⭐ The "decline of Parliament" debate — the chapter's own answer
*"Yet, in most democracies, **legislatures are losing central place to the executive.** In India too, **the Cabinet initiates policies, sets the agenda for governance and carries them through.** This has led some critics to remark that **the Parliament has declined.**"*

**But the rebuttal:**
- **"Even very strong cabinets must retain majority in the legislature."**
- **"A strong leader has to face the Parliament and answer to the satisfaction of the Parliament."**
- ⭐ **"Herein lies the democratic potential of the Parliament."**

**Three claims for Parliament's pre-eminence:**
1. **"It is recognised as one of the most democratic and open forum of debate."**
2. ⭐ **"On account of its composition, it is the MOST REPRESENTATIVE of all organs of government."**
3. ⭐⭐ **"It is, above all, vested with the power to CHOOSE and DISMISS the government."**

## PART 1 — WHY DO WE NEED TWO HOUSES OF PARLIAMENT?

### Terminology and structure
- ⭐ **"The term 'Parliament' refers to the NATIONAL legislature. The legislature of the States is described as STATE legislature."**
- **The Parliament in India has two houses.** ⭐ **"When there are two houses of the legislature, it is called a BICAMERAL legislature."**
- **The two Houses of the Indian Parliament: the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha).**
- ⭐ **"The Constitution has given the States the OPTION of establishing either a unicameral or bicameral legislature."**

### ⭐⭐ States having a bicameral legislature — **only SIX at present** (memorise the list)
1. **Andhra Pradesh**
2. **Bihar**
3. **Karnataka**
4. **Maharashtra**
5. **Telangana**
6. **Uttar Pradesh**

**Mnemonic: "A-B-K-M-T-U"** — or think *"two Telugu states, two Hindi-belt states, two western/southern states."*

### ⭐⭐ Why bicameralism? (three reasons)
1. **Representation of all sections.** *"Countries with large size and much diversity usually prefer to have two houses of the national legislature to give representation to **all sections in the society.**"*
2. **Representation of all regions.** *"...and to give representation to **all geographical regions or parts of the country.**"*
3. ⭐⭐ **A double check through reconsideration.** *"A bicameral legislature makes it possible to have **every decision reconsidered.** Every decision taken by one house goes to the other house for its decision. This means that **every bill and policy would be discussed twice.** This ensures a **double check** on every matter. **Even if one house takes a decision in haste, that decision will come for discussion in the other house and reconsideration will be possible.**"*

> ### ⭐ Purnima Banerji on the role of an Upper House (a set-piece quotation)
> *"...an Upper House could perform the...useful function of being a **REVISING BODY**, and ...**its views may count but not its votes**... ...those who could not enter into the **rough and tumble of active politics** could...**advise the Lower House.**"*
> — **Purnima Banerji, CAD, Vol. IX, p. 33, 30 July 1949**
>
> **The phrase to remember: "its views may count but not its votes."** This is the classic justification for both a **revising chamber** and for **nominating experts** to it.

## PART 2 — THE RAJYA SABHA (Council of States)

### Basis of representation
- ⭐ **"The Rajya Sabha represents the STATES of India. It is an INDIRECTLY elected body."**
- **The chain:** *"Residents of the State elect members to the State Legislative Assembly. **The elected members of the State Legislative Assembly in turn elect the members of the Rajya Sabha.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Two possible principles of representation in a second chamber
| Principle | How it works | Consequence | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Symmetrical representation** | **Equal representation to all parts of the country irrespective of size or population** | **Ensures equality of all the states** — but **a small state would have the same representation as the larger states** | ⭐ **the USA — "every state has equal representation in the Senate"** |
| **Representation according to population** | *"Regions or parts having larger population would have more representatives in the second chamber than regions having less population"* | Preserves proportionality between people and seats | ⭐ **INDIA (the Rajya Sabha)** |

⭐ **"The system of representation adopted for the Rajya Sabha is DIFFERENT from that in the USA."**
⭐⭐ **"The number of members to be elected from each State has been fixed by the FOURTH SCHEDULE of the Constitution."**

### ⭐ The thought experiment the chapter runs (learn the two figures)
*"What would happen if we were to follow the American system of equality of representation in the Rajya Sabha? **Uttar Pradesh with a population of 1998.12 lakhs would get seats equal to that of Sikkim whose population is only 6.10 lakhs.** The framers of the Constitution wanted to prevent such discrepancy."*

⭐⭐ **The result:** *"States with larger population get more representatives than States with smaller population. Thus, **a more populous State like Uttar Pradesh sends 31 members to the Rajya Sabha**, while **a smaller and less populous State like Sikkim has ONE seat** in the Rajya Sabha."*

### ⭐⭐ Term, rotation, and why the Rajya Sabha is "permanent"
- **Members of the Rajya Sabha are elected for a term of SIX years.** ⭐ **"They can get re-elected."**
- ⭐ **"All members of the Rajya Sabha do NOT complete their terms at the same time."**
- ⭐⭐ **"Every two years, ONE-THIRD members of the Rajya Sabha complete their term and elections are held for those one-third seats only."**
- ⭐⭐ **"Thus, the Rajya Sabha is NEVER FULLY DISSOLVED. Therefore, it is called the PERMANENT HOUSE of the Parliament."**
- ⭐ **The practical advantage:** *"even when the **Lok Sabha is dissolved and elections are yet to take place**, the meeting of the Rajya Sabha can be called and **urgent business can be conducted.**"*

### ⭐⭐ The twelve nominated members
- **"Apart from the elected members, Rajya Sabha also has TWELVE NOMINATED members."**
- **"The PRESIDENT nominates these members."**
- ⭐ **"These nominations are made from among those persons who have made their mark in the fields of LITERATURE, SCIENCE, ART and SOCIAL SERVICE."** *(Four fields — memorise all four.)*

> *The chapter voices the standard scepticism:* *"I don't understand why there is a provision for nominating these sportspersons and artists and scientists. **Whom do they represent? And do they really contribute much to the proceedings of the Rajya Sabha?**"* — **the answer is Purnima Banerji's: "those who could not enter into the rough and tumble of active politics could advise the Lower House."**

### ⭐⭐ Box: Bicameralism in Germany (a rich comparison — learn it)
- **Germany has a bicameral legislature.** The two Houses are the **Federal Assembly (*Bundestag*)** and the **Federal Council (*Bundesrat*)**.
- **The Assembly is elected by a complex system combining direct and proportional representation, for a period of FOUR years.**
- ⭐ **"The 16 federal states of Germany are represented in the Federal Council. The 69 seats of the Bundesrat are divided among states on the basis of a RANGE of populations."** *(i.e. neither strictly equal nor strictly proportional — a banded system.)*
- ⭐⭐ **"These members are generally the MINISTERS in the governments at the state level and are APPOINTED, NOT ELECTED, by the governments of the federal states."**
- ⭐⭐ **The bloc-voting rule:** *"According to the German law, **all the members from one state have to vote as a BLOC** as per the **instructions of the state governments.** Sometimes due to coalition government at the state level, they fail to reach an agreement and **may have to abstain.**"*
- ⭐ **Jurisdiction:** *"The Bundesrat does **not** vote on all legislative initiatives, **but all the policy areas on which the federal states have concurrent powers and are responsible for federal regulations must be passed by it. It can also VETO such legislation.**"*

> **⭐ The comparison that matters:** the German *Bundesrat* is a **genuinely state-representing** chamber — members are **state ministers**, appointed by state governments, **bound to vote as a bloc on state instructions**, with a **veto** over state-relevant legislation. Contrast India, where — as the chapter itself admits — *"the members of the Rajya Sabha represent their **parties** more than they represent their **States**."* **This is the strongest available critique of the Rajya Sabha's federal character.**

## PART 3 — THE LOK SABHA (House of the People)

- ⭐ **"The Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assemblies are DIRECTLY elected by the people."**
- **For the purpose of election, the entire country (or the State, in the case of a State Legislative Assembly) is divided into **territorial constituencies of roughly equal population.**
- ⭐ **"One representative is elected from each constituency through UNIVERSAL ADULT SUFFRAGE where the value of vote of every individual would be EQUAL to another."**
- ⭐⭐ **"At present there are 543 constituencies. THIS NUMBER HAS NOT CHANGED SINCE THE 1971 CENSUS."**
- **Term:** *"The Lok Sabha is elected for a period of **five years. This is the MAXIMUM.**"*
- ⭐ **Two ways it can end early** *(from the chapter on the executive)*: **"before the completion of five years, the Lok Sabha can be dissolved (i) if no party or coalition can form the government, or (ii) if the Prime Minister advises the President to dissolve the Lok Sabha and hold fresh elections."**

### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — three debate questions worth preparing
1. **"Do you think that the composition of the Rajya Sabha has protected the position of the States of India?"** *(Argue both ways: the **Fourth Schedule** and population-based seats do give big states weight, and the Rajya Sabha's consent is needed to move a subject off the State List. **But** members are chosen by party whips and — the chapter's own words — *"represent their parties more than they represent their States."* Contrast the German **Bundesrat**, whose members are state ministers bound to vote as a bloc.)*
2. **"Should indirect election of the Rajya Sabha be replaced by direct elections? What would be its advantages and disadvantages?"** *(Advantage: democratic legitimacy, ending the party-machine character. Disadvantage: it would **duplicate the Lok Sabha's mandate**, destroy the state-representing rationale, remove the case for a **revising** chamber, and undermine the argument for withholding money-bill and no-confidence powers — which rests precisely on the Rajya Sabha *not* being directly elected.)*
3. **"Since the 1971 census the number of seats in the Lok Sabha has not increased. Do you think it should be increased? What should be the basis?"** *(Note the tension: population-based redistribution would shift seats from states that controlled their population growth to those that did not — the core of the delimitation freeze debate.)*

## ⭐⭐ PART 4 — WHAT DOES THE PARLIAMENT DO? The eight functions

| # | Function | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Legislative** | **"The Parliament enacts legislations for the country."** ⭐ **BUT the honest qualification:** *"Despite being the chief law making body, **the Parliament often merely APPROVES legislations.** The **actual task of drafting the bill is performed by the BUREAUCRACY under the supervision of the minister concerned.** **The substance and even the TIMING of the bill are decided by the CABINET.** **No major bill is introduced in the Parliament without the approval of the Cabinet.** Members other than ministers can also introduce bills but **these have no chance of being passed without the support of the government.**"* |
| **2** | **Control of Executive and ensuring its accountability** | ⭐ **"Perhaps the MOST VITAL function of the Parliament is to ensure that the executive does not overstep its authority and remains responsible to the people who have elected them."** *(Detailed in Part 7.)* |
| **3** | **Financial** | *"Every government raises resources through taxation. However, in a democracy, **legislature controls taxation and the way in which money is used** by the government. ⭐ **If the Government of India proposes to introduce any new tax, it has to get the approval of the LOK SABHA.**"* The financial powers involve the **grant of resources** to the government to implement its programmes; the government **must give an account to the legislature of money spent and resources it wishes to raise**; and **the legislature ensures the government does not misspend or overspend** — **through the budget and annual financial statements.** |
| **4** | **Representation** | **"Parliament represents the divergent views of members from different regional, social, economic, religious groups of different parts of the country."** |
| **5** | **Debating** | ⭐ **"The Parliament is the HIGHEST FORUM OF DEBATE in the country. There is NO LIMITATION on its power of discussion. Members are free to speak on any matter WITHOUT FEAR.** This makes it possible for the Parliament to analyse any or every issue that faces the nation. **These discussions constitute the heart of democratic decision making.**" |
| **6** | **Constituent** | **"The Parliament has the power of discussing and enacting CHANGES TO THE CONSTITUTION."** ⭐⭐ **"The constituent powers of BOTH the houses are SIMILAR. All constitutional amendments have to be approved by a SPECIAL MAJORITY of BOTH Houses."** |
| **7** | **Electoral** | ⭐ **"It elects the President and Vice President of India."** |
| **8** | **Judicial** | ⭐ **"Considering the proposals for REMOVAL of the President, Vice-President and Judges of High Courts and the Supreme Court."** |

**Mnemonic for the eight: "L-C-F-R-D-C-E-J"** → **L**egislative, **C**ontrol of executive, **F**inancial, **R**epresentation, **D**ebating, **C**onstituent, **E**lectoral, **J**udicial.

## ⭐⭐ PART 5 — POWERS OF THE TWO HOUSES

### The powers side by side (as given in the chapter's charts)
| **Powers of the LOK SABHA** | **Powers of the RAJYA SABHA** |
|---|---|
| **Makes laws on matters included in the Union List and Concurrent List. Can introduce and enact BOTH money and non-money bills** | **Considers and approves non-money bills, and SUGGESTS amendments to money bills** |
| **Approves proposals for taxation, budgets and annual financial statements** | **Approves constitutional amendments** |
| **Controls the executive by asking questions, supplementary questions, resolutions and motions, and through the NO CONFIDENCE MOTION** | **Exercises control over the executive by asking questions, introducing motions and resolutions** *(note: **no** no-confidence motion)* |
| **Amends the Constitution** | **Participates in the election and removal of the President, Vice President, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.** ⭐ **"It can ALONE initiate the procedure for removal of the VICE PRESIDENT."** |
| **Approves the Proclamation of Emergency** | ⭐ **"Can give the Union Parliament power to make laws on matters included in the STATE LIST."** |
| **Elects the President and Vice President and removes Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts** | |
| **Establishes committees and commissions and considers their reports** | |

### ⭐⭐ Special powers of the Rajya Sabha
*"The Rajya Sabha is an **institutional mechanism to provide representation to the States. Its purpose is to protect the powers of the States.** Therefore, **any matter that affects the States must be referred to it for its consent and approval.**"*

⭐⭐ **The specific power:** *"if the Union Parliament wishes to **remove a matter from the State List** (over which only the State Legislature can make law) **to either the Union List or Concurrent List in the interest of the nation, the approval of the Rajya Sabha is necessary.** **This provision adds to the strength of the Rajya Sabha.**"*

⭐⭐ **The candid critique that follows immediately:** *"**However, experience shows that the members of the Rajya Sabha represent their PARTIES more than they represent their STATES.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Powers exercised ONLY by the Lok Sabha (and the reason why)
**Two exclusive powers:**
1. ⭐ **"The Rajya Sabha CANNOT initiate, reject or amend MONEY BILLS."**
2. ⭐⭐ **"The Council of Ministers is responsible to the LOK SABHA and NOT the Rajya Sabha. Therefore, the Rajya Sabha CAN CRITICISE the government but CANNOT REMOVE it."**

⭐⭐ **The reason — a beautifully clean piece of democratic logic worth reproducing verbatim in an answer:**
> *"The Rajya Sabha is **elected by the MLAs and not directly by the people.** Therefore, the Constitution **stopped short of giving certain powers to the Rajya Sabha.** In a democratic form as adopted by our Constitution, **the people are the final authority.** By this logic, **the representatives DIRECTLY elected by the people should have the crucial powers of REMOVING A GOVERNMENT and CONTROLLING THE FINANCES.**"*

### ⭐ Where the two Houses are CO-EQUAL
*"In all other spheres the powers of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha are **co-equal**:"*
- **Passing of non-money bills**
- **Constitutional amendments**
- **Impeaching the President**
- **Removing the Vice President**

> **⚠️ Trap alert:** The Lok Sabha *does* "control the purse" and *is* therefore the more powerful House — but **only in the two specific respects above.** In amendment, non-money legislation, and the removal of the President/VP, the Houses are **equal.** And for the **removal of the Vice President, the Rajya Sabha alone can *initiate*.**

## ⭐⭐ PART 6 — HOW DOES THE PARLIAMENT MAKE LAWS?

*"The basic function of any legislature is to make laws for its people. A definite procedure is followed. **Some of the procedures of law making are mentioned in the Constitution, while some have evolved from CONVENTIONS.** Follow a bill through the legislative process and you will clearly see that **the law making process is technical and even tedious.**"*

### ⭐ Types of Bills (learn the classification tree)
**Definition:** ⭐ **"A BILL is a DRAFT of the proposed law."**

| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| **By who proposes it** | ⭐ **Government Bill** — *"A bill proposed by a MINISTER"* • ⭐ **Private Member's Bill** — *"When a NON-MINISTER proposes a bill"* |
| **Within Government Bills** | **Non-Money Bill** • **Money Bill** |
| **Within Non-Money Bills** | **Ordinary Bill** • **Constitution Amendment Bill** |

### ⭐⭐ Stage 0 — Before the bill even reaches Parliament (the *political* stage)
*"Even before a bill is introduced in the Parliament there may be a lot of debate on the need for introducing such a bill."*
- **"A political party may pressurise the government to initiate a bill in order to FULFIL ITS ELECTION PROMISES or to IMPROVE ITS CHANCES OF WINNING FORTHCOMING ELECTIONS."**
- **"INTEREST GROUPS, MEDIA and CITIZENS' FORUMS may also persuade the government for a particular legislation."**
- ⭐⭐ **"Law making is thus NOT MERELY A LEGAL PROCEDURE but also a POLITICAL COURSE OF ACTION."**

**The considerations that go into preparing a bill:**
1. **Resources required to implement the law**
2. **The support or opposition the bill is likely to produce**
3. **The impact the law may have on the electoral prospects of the ruling party**
4. ⭐ **"In the era of coalition politics especially, a bill proposed by the government has to be ACCEPTABLE TO ALL THE PARTNERS of the coalition."**

⭐ **"Such practical considerations can hardly be ignored. THE CABINET CONSIDERS ALL THESE before arriving at a decision to enact a law."**

### Drafting
- **"Once the Cabinet approves the POLICY behind the legislation, the task of DRAFTING the legislation begins."**
- ⭐ **"The draft of any bill is prepared by the CONCERNED MINISTRY."**
- **The chapter's own example:** *"a bill **raising the marriageable age of girls from 18 to 21** will be prepared by the **law ministry.** The **ministry of women and child welfare** may also be involved in it."*

### ⭐⭐ The three stages inside Parliament
**STAGE 1 — Introduction.**
- **"Within the Parliament, a bill may be introduced in the Lok Sabha OR Rajya Sabha by a member of the House"** — *"but often a minister responsible for the subject introduces the bill."*
- ⭐⭐ **"A MONEY BILL can be introduced ONLY in the LOK SABHA. Once passed there, it is sent to the Rajya Sabha."**

> ### ⭐ Bare text — **Article 109**
> *"**Special procedure in respect of Money Bills** — (1) **A Money Bill shall NOT be introduced in the Council of States.**"*

**STAGE 2 — Committee stage.**
- ⭐⭐ **"A LARGE PART of the discussion on the bills takes place in the COMMITTEES. The recommendation of the committee is then sent to the House. THAT IS WHY COMMITTEES ARE REFERRED TO AS 'MINIATURE LEGISLATURES'."**

**STAGE 3 — Voting.**
- **"In the third and final stage, the bill is voted upon."**
- **"If a non-money bill is passed by one House, it is sent to the OTHER House where it goes through EXACTLY THE SAME PROCEDURE."**

### ⭐ The flow of a bill (as the chapter's diagram shows it)
```
Concerned minister proposes the need for a bill
        ↓
Legislative department of the Ministry of Law drafts the bill
        ↓
Introduction in EITHER House (non-money bill) / LOK SABHA ONLY (money bill)
        ↓
Sent to Committee  ←—— [People's inputs]
        ↓
Committee gives report → House MAY or MAY NOT accept the report
        ↓
Detailed discussion in the House (clause by clause, each voted upon)
        ↓
Bill is accepted or rejected
        ↓
Sent to the OTHER House
        ↓
Other House approves OR gives recommendations
        ↓
[JOINT SESSION of Parliament, if required]
        ↓
President approves OR sends back for reconsideration
        ↓
BILL BECOMES LAW
```

### ⭐⭐ Resolving disagreement between the Houses — the Joint Session
- **"A bill has to be passed by BOTH Houses for enactment."**
- **"If there is disagreement between the two Houses on the proposed bill, attempt is made to resolve it through a JOINT SESSION of Parliament."**
- ⭐⭐ **"In the FEW instances when joint sessions of the parliament were called to resolve a deadlock, THE DECISION HAS ALWAYS GONE IN FAVOUR OF THE LOK SABHA."** *(Because the Lok Sabha is far larger, a joint sitting mathematically favours it.)*

### ⭐⭐ The special money-bill procedure (a guaranteed exam point)
| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Introduction | ⭐ **Only in the Lok Sabha** (Article 109) |
| Rajya Sabha's options | ⭐ **"Can either APPROVE the bill or SUGGEST CHANGES but CANNOT REJECT it."** |
| ⭐⭐ **The 14-day rule** | **"If it takes NO ACTION within 14 DAYS the bill is DEEMED to have been passed."** |
| Fate of RS amendments | ⭐ **"Amendments to the bill, suggested by the Rajya Sabha, MAY OR MAY NOT BE ACCEPTED by the Lok Sabha."** |

### Final step
**"When a bill is passed by both Houses, it is sent to the President for his assent. THE ASSENT OF THE PRESIDENT RESULTS IN THE ENACTMENT OF A BILL INTO A LAW."** *(Recall from Chapter 4: the President may send a non-money bill back once, but must assent if Parliament passes it again — and there is **no time limit**, hence the **pocket veto**.)*

### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — is there enough time?
*"From the discussion of the law making process, do you think that Parliament can devote enough time for thorough discussion of the bills? If not, then what remedies would you suggest?"*
**Remedies to argue for:** more sitting days; **strengthening the standing committee system** (the real site of scrutiny); mandatory pre-legislative consultation and publication of drafts; limiting the passage of bills in the din; restricting the misuse of the money-bill route; and — as the chapter's own conclusion suggests — **compulsory attendance and stronger powers for the Speaker.**

## ⭐⭐ PART 7 — HOW DOES THE PARLIAMENT CONTROL THE EXECUTIVE?

### The danger: "Cabinet dictatorship"
*"In a parliamentary democracy, the executive is drawn from the party or a coalition of parties that has a majority in the Lok Sabha. **It is not difficult for the executive to exercise unlimited and arbitrary powers with the support of the majority party.**"*

⭐⭐ **"In such a situation, parliamentary democracy may slip into CABINET DICTATORSHIP, where THE CABINET LEADS AND THE HOUSE MERELY FOLLOWS."**

⭐ **"ONLY IF the Parliament is ACTIVE AND VIGILANT can it keep a regular and effective check on the executive."**

### ⭐⭐ Parliamentary privilege — the foundation of all control
*"Basic to them all is **the power and freedom of the legislators as people's representatives to work effectively and FEARLESSLY.**"*
- ⭐ **"NO ACTION CAN BE TAKEN AGAINST A MEMBER FOR WHATEVER THE MEMBER MAY HAVE SAID IN THE LEGISLATURE. This is known as PARLIAMENTARY PRIVILEGE."**
- ⭐ **"The PRESIDING OFFICER of the legislature has the FINAL POWERS in deciding matters of BREACH OF PRIVILEGE."**
- ⭐ **The purpose:** *"to enable the members of the legislature to **represent the people** and **exercise effective control over the executive.**"*

### ⭐⭐ The four instruments of parliamentary control
*"The legislature in a parliamentary system ensures executive accountability at various stages: **policy making, implementation of law or policy, and during and post-implementation stage.**"*

## ➊ Deliberation and discussion
*"During the law making process, members get an opportunity to deliberate on **the policy direction of the executive** and **the ways in which policies are implemented.** Apart from deliberating on bills, control may also be exercised during the **general discussions in the House.**"*

### ⭐⭐ The specific devices (learn each precisely — these are constantly confused)
| Device | What it is |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **Question Hour** | **"Held EVERY DAY during the sessions of Parliament, where MINISTERS HAVE TO RESPOND to searching questions raised by the members."** |
| ⭐⭐ **Zero Hour** | **"Where members are FREE TO RAISE ANY MATTER that they think is important"** — ⭐ **"(though the MINISTERS ARE NOT BOUND TO REPLY)."** ← *the key distinction from Question Hour* |
| **Half-an-hour discussion** | **On matters of public importance** |
| **Adjournment motion** | Another instrument of exercising control |

### ⭐⭐ Question Hour — the most effective instrument
- ⭐ **"Perhaps the QUESTION HOUR is the MOST EFFECTIVE method of keeping vigil on the executive and the administrative agencies of the government."**
- ⭐ **"Members of Parliament have shown great interest in question hour and MAXIMUM ATTENDANCE IS RECORDED DURING THIS TIME."**
- **What is asked:** *"Most of the questions aim at **eliciting information from the government on issues of public interest** such as **price rise, availability of food grains, atrocities on weaker sections of the society, riots, black-marketing**, etc."*
- **What it achieves:** *"This gives the members an opportunity to **criticise the government** and **represent the problems of their constituencies.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The balanced verdict on disruption (a superb nuanced point):** *"The discussions during the question hour are so heated that it is not uncommon to see members **raise their voice, walk to the well of the house, or walk out in protest** to make their point. **This results in considerable loss of legislative time. AT THE SAME TIME, we must remember that many of these actions are POLITICAL TECHNIQUES to gain concessions from government and in the process FORCE EXECUTIVE ACCOUNTABILITY.**"*

> *The chapter's own quip:* *"It must be difficult to be a minister. **This is like giving an examination almost every day!**"*

## ➋ Approval and ratification of laws
- **"A bill can become a law ONLY with the approval of the Parliament."**
- **"A government that has the support of a disciplined majority may not find it difficult to get the approval of the Legislature. Such approvals however, CANNOT BE TAKEN FOR GRANTED. They are the products of INTENSE BARGAINING AND NEGOTIATIONS** amongst the members of the ruling party or coalition, **and even between government and opposition.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The critical scenario — majority in one House but not the other:** *"If the government has majority in the Lok Sabha but **NOT in the Rajya Sabha** — as has happened during the **Janata Party rule in 1977** and **N.D.A. rule in 2000** — **the government will be forced to make substantial concessions to gain the approval of BOTH Houses.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **Two real examples of bills that failed:**
  - **"Many bills, such as the LOK PAL BILL, have failed enactment."**
  - **"PREVENTION OF TERRORISM BILL (2002) was REJECTED BY THE RAJYA SABHA."**

## ➌ Financial control
- **"Financial resources to implement the programmes of the government are granted through the BUDGET."**
- ⭐ **"Preparation and presentation of the budget for the approval of the legislature is a CONSTITUTIONAL OBLIGATION of the government."** *"This obligation allows the legislature to exercise control over **the purse strings** of the government."*
- ⭐ **"The legislature MAY REFUSE to grant resources to the government. This SELDOM happens because the government ordinarily enjoys support of the majority in the parliamentary system."**
- **"Nevertheless, before granting money the Lok Sabha can discuss the reasons for which the government requires money."**
- ⭐⭐ **The audit machinery:** *"It can **enquire into cases of misuse of funds** on the basis of the report of the **COMPTROLLER AND AUDITOR GENERAL** and **PUBLIC ACCOUNTS COMMITTEES.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **The deeper point:** *"But the legislative control is **not only aimed at financial propriety.** The legislature is concerned about **the POLICIES of the government that are reflected in the budget.** **Through financial control, the legislature controls the POLICY of the government.**"*

## ➍ No Confidence Motion
- ⭐⭐ **"The MOST POWERFUL WEAPON that enables the Parliament to ensure executive accountability is the NO-CONFIDENCE MOTION."**
- ⭐ **The honest qualification:** *"As long as the government has the support of its party or coalition of parties that have a majority in the Lok Sabha, **the power of the House to dismiss the government is FICTIONAL rather than REAL.**"*
- ⭐⭐ **But after 1989 it became real:** *"**However, after 1989, SEVERAL GOVERNMENTS HAVE BEEN FORCED TO RESIGN due to lack of confidence of the house.** Each of these governments lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha **because they failed to retain the support of their COALITION PARTNERS.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Three conditions for effective parliamentary control — and how India is failing them
*"Thus, the Parliament CAN effectively control the executive and ensure a more responsive government. **It is however important for this purpose, that:**"*
1. **"There is ADEQUATE TIME at the disposal of the House"**
2. **"The MEMBERS ARE INTERESTED in discussion and PARTICIPATE EFFECTIVELY"**
3. **"There is WILLINGNESS TO COMPROMISE amongst the government and the opposition"**

⭐⭐ **The diagnosis of decline (learn these four symptoms):**
- **"In the last two decades, there has been a GRADUAL DECLINE IN SESSIONS of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies"**
- **"...and [a decline in] TIME SPENT ON DEBATES"**
- **"Moreover, the Houses of the Parliament have been plagued by ABSENCE OF QUORUM"**
- **"...[and] BOYCOTT OF SESSIONS BY MEMBERS OF OPPOSITION — which DEPRIVE the house of the power to control the executive through discussion."**

## ⭐⭐ PART 8 — THE COMMITTEES OF PARLIAMENT

### Why committees are necessary
*"A significant feature of the legislative process is the appointment of committees for various legislative purposes. These committees play a vital role **not merely in law making, but also in the day-to-day business of the House.**"*

⭐ **The reasons:**
1. **"Since the Parliament meets only during SESSIONS, it has VERY LIMITED TIME at its disposal."**
2. **"The making of law requires IN-DEPTH STUDY of the issue under consideration. This in turn demands more attention and time."**
3. **Other important functions also need committees:**
   - **"Studying the DEMANDS FOR GRANTS made by various ministries"**
   - **"Looking into EXPENDITURE incurred by various departments"**
   - **"Investigating cases of CORRUPTION"**

### ⭐⭐ Standing Committees
- ⭐⭐ **"SINCE 1983, India has developed a system of parliamentary STANDING COMMITTEES."**
- ⭐ **"There are OVER TWENTY such DEPARTMENTALLY RELATED committees."**
- ⭐⭐ **What they supervise (four things):** *"Standing Committees supervise **(i) the work of various departments, (ii) their BUDGET, (iii) their EXPENDITURE, and (iv) BILLS that come up in the house relating to the department.**"*

### ⭐ Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs)
- **"Apart from standing committees, the JOINT PARLIAMENTARY COMMITTEES have occupied a position of EMINENCE in our country."**
- ⭐ **Two purposes:** *"JPCs can be set up **(i) for the purpose of DISCUSSING A PARTICULAR BILL**, or **(ii) for the purpose of INVESTIGATING FINANCIAL IRREGULARITIES.**"*
- ⭐ **"Members of these committees are selected from BOTH Houses."**

### ⭐⭐ The effect of the committee system — and its constitutional limit
- **"The committee system has REDUCED THE BURDEN on the Parliament."**
- **"Many important bills have been referred to committees."**
- ⭐⭐ **"The Parliament has merely APPROVED the work done in the committees with FEW OCCASIONAL ALTERATIONS."**
- ⭐ **The legal position remains intact:** *"Of course legally speaking, **no bill can become law, and no budget will be sanctioned, unless approved by the Parliament.** **But the Parliament RARELY REJECTS the suggestions made by the committees.**"*

> ### ⭐ N.V. Gadgil on legislative sovereignty
> *"The nature of the legislature is such that **there are restrictions only so far as PROCEDURE is concerned. But in SUBSTANCE there is NO restriction, NO limitation on the sovereignty of the legislature or Parliament...**"*
> — **N.V. Gadgil, CAD, Vol. XI, p.659, 18 November 1949**
>
> *(Worth noting: this view was later qualified by the **basic structure doctrine** — see Chapters 2 and 9.)*

## ⭐⭐ PART 9 — HOW DOES THE PARLIAMENT REGULATE ITSELF?

### The presiding officer
*"Parliament is a **debating forum.** It is through debates that the parliament performs all its vital functions. **Such discussions must be MEANINGFUL and ORDERLY** so that the functions of the Parliament are carried out smoothly and **its dignity is intact.** **The Constitution itself has made certain provisions to ensure smooth conduct of business.**"*

⭐ **"The PRESIDING OFFICER of the legislature is the FINAL AUTHORITY in matters of regulating the business of the legislature."**

### ⭐⭐ The Anti-Defection Law
**The problem:** *"Most of the members of the legislatures are elected on the ticket of some political party. **What would happen if they decide to LEAVE the party after getting elected?** For many years after independence, this issue was unresolved."*

**The solution:** *"Finally there was an agreement among the parties that **a legislator who is elected on one party's ticket must be restricted from 'DEFECTING' to another party.**"*

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| ⭐⭐ **The amendment** | **The 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 — known as the ANTI-DEFECTION AMENDMENT** |
| ⭐⭐ **Later modified by** | **The 91st Amendment** *(the same amendment that capped the Council of Ministers at 15% — Ch. 4)* |
| ⭐⭐ **Who decides** | **"The PRESIDING OFFICER of the House is the authority who takes FINAL DECISIONS on all such cases."** |
| ⭐⭐ **Consequence of defection** | **"If it is proved that a member has 'defected', then such member LOSES THE MEMBERSHIP of the House. Besides, such a person is ALSO DISQUALIFIED FROM HOLDING ANY POLITICAL OFFICE like ministership, etc."** |

### ⭐⭐ What counts as "defection"? (three grounds — memorise all three)
*"If a member:"*
1. **"Remains ABSENT in the House when asked by the party leadership to remain present"**
2. **"VOTES AGAINST the instructions of the party"**
3. **"VOLUNTARILY LEAVES the membership of the party"**

**"...it is deemed as DEFECTION."**

### ⭐⭐ The verdict on the anti-defection law (a crucial critical point)
> **"Experience of the past twenty years shows that the anti-defection amendment HAS NOT BEEN ABLE TO CURB DEFECTIONS, BUT IT HAS GIVEN ADDITIONAL POWERS TO THE PARTY LEADERSHIP AND THE PRESIDING OFFICERS OF THE LEGISLATURES OVER THE MEMBERS."**

*(Note the irony worth flagging in an answer: a law meant to protect the integrity of the legislature ended up **strengthening party bosses against individual legislators** — which sits uneasily with the "freedom to speak without fear" that parliamentary privilege is meant to guarantee.)*

> *The chapter's cartoon captions capture the mood:* *"**We are not walking out, we have been ordered out**," said the MPs.* And: *"Walkout is a frequently adopted measure by the opposition to register their protest against the government. **Has there been an overuse of that weapon?**"*

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Parliament** | The **national** legislature; the legislature of a State is called the **State legislature** |
| **Bicameral legislature** | A legislature with **two houses**; India's are the **Rajya Sabha** (Council of States) and **Lok Sabha** (House of the People) |
| **Unicameral** | A single-chamber legislature; **States may choose either** — only **six** are bicameral |
| **Symmetrical representation** | Equal representation to all parts irrespective of size or population — the **US Senate** model; **not** India's |
| **Fourth Schedule** | Fixes the **number of Rajya Sabha members to be elected from each State** |
| **Permanent House** | The Rajya Sabha — **never fully dissolved**, because **one-third of members retire every two years** |
| **Nominated members** | **Twelve** members nominated by the **President** from **literature, science, art and social service** |
| ***Bundestag* / *Bundesrat*** | Germany's Federal Assembly (elected, 4-year term) and Federal Council (**69 seats**, **16 states**, members are **appointed state ministers** who must **vote as a bloc**, with a **veto** over state-relevant legislation) |
| **Bill** | A **draft** of the proposed law |
| **Government Bill** | A bill proposed by a **minister** |
| **Private Member's Bill** | A bill proposed by a **non-minister** |
| **Money Bill** | A bill that can be introduced **only in the Lok Sabha** (**Article 109**); the Rajya Sabha may approve or suggest changes but **cannot reject**, and is **deemed to have passed it after 14 days** of inaction |
| **Joint Session** | The mechanism to resolve deadlock between the Houses on a bill; **the decision has always gone in favour of the Lok Sabha** |
| **Miniature legislatures** | Committees — so called because **a large part of the discussion on bills happens there** |
| **Standing Committees** | Departmentally related committees, developed **since 1983**, **over twenty** in number, supervising a department's **work, budget, expenditure and bills** |
| **Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC)** | A committee with members **from both Houses**, set up to **discuss a particular bill** or **investigate financial irregularities** |
| **Question Hour** | Held **every day** during sessions; **ministers must respond** — the **most effective** instrument of vigil; **maximum attendance** |
| **Zero Hour** | Members may raise **any matter** they consider important — but **ministers are not bound to reply** |
| **Parliamentary privilege** | **No action can be taken against a member for anything said in the legislature**; the **presiding officer** decides breach of privilege |
| **Cabinet dictatorship** | The danger that a majority-backed Cabinet **leads while the House merely follows** |
| **No-confidence motion** | The **most powerful weapon** of parliamentary control; **fictional** while the government has a stable majority, but **real after 1989** |
| **Comptroller and Auditor General** | Whose reports (with the **Public Accounts Committee**) let the Lok Sabha enquire into **misuse of funds** |
| **Anti-defection law** | **52nd Amendment, 1985**, modified by the **91st Amendment**; the **presiding officer** decides; a defector **loses membership and is barred from political office** |
| **Defection** | **Being absent when the party directs presence**, **voting against party instructions**, or **voluntarily leaving the party** |

## Quick Recap (16 points)
1. **The legislature is the centre of all democratic political process, not merely a law-making body.** Executives have grown stronger everywhere — in India **the Cabinet initiates policy, sets the agenda and carries it through** — but **even strong cabinets must retain a majority**, and Parliament remains the **most representative** organ and the only one that can **choose and dismiss the government.**
2. ⭐ **Walkouts and protests are instruments, not merely noise:** **Yashwant Sinha rolled back the urea hike (June 1998)** and **Jaswant Singh rolled back fertilizer price increases (11 March 2002)** under opposition pressure; the Lok Sabha **suspended official business for the Assam debate (22 Feb 1983)**.
3. **India's Parliament is bicameral; States may choose. Only SIX States are bicameral: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh.**
4. **Bicameralism is justified by** representation of **all sections**, representation of **all regions**, and — decisively — **reconsideration**: every bill is **discussed twice**, so a hasty decision in one House can be corrected in the other. **Purnima Banerji: the Upper House is a "revising body" whose "views may count but not its votes."**
5. **Rajya Sabha represents the States and is indirectly elected by elected MLAs.** India uses **population-based**, not **symmetrical (US Senate)**, representation — seats are fixed by the **Fourth Schedule**. **UP (population 1998.12 lakhs) sends 31 members; Sikkim (6.10 lakhs) sends 1.**
6. **Rajya Sabha: six-year terms, re-election allowed, one-third retire every two years — so it is NEVER fully dissolved and is called the PERMANENT HOUSE**, which lets urgent business proceed even when the Lok Sabha stands dissolved. Plus **twelve members nominated by the President** from **literature, science, art and social service.**
7. **Germany's Bundesrat is the sharper federal model:** **69 seats, 16 states, banded by population, members are appointed state ministers who must vote as a bloc on state instructions, with a veto over legislation in areas of concurrent state responsibility.** By contrast the chapter admits **India's Rajya Sabha members "represent their parties more than their States."**
8. **Lok Sabha is directly elected from territorial constituencies of roughly equal population by universal adult suffrage with equal vote value; 543 constituencies, UNCHANGED SINCE THE 1971 CENSUS; five-year maximum term**, cut short if no party can form a government or if the PM advises dissolution.
9. **Eight functions — L-C-F-R-D-C-E-J:** Legislative (though drafting is by the bureaucracy and **substance and timing are decided by the Cabinet**); Control of executive (**the most vital**); Financial (**any new tax needs Lok Sabha approval**); Representation; Debating (**highest forum, no limitation on discussion, members speak without fear**); Constituent (**both Houses' powers are similar; amendments need a special majority in both**); Electoral (**elects President and Vice President**); Judicial (**removal of President, VP, and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts**).
10. **Only the Lok Sabha can:** initiate, reject or amend **money bills**, and **remove the government** (the Council of Ministers is responsible to it alone — the Rajya Sabha **can criticise but cannot remove**). ⭐ **The reason: the Rajya Sabha is elected by MLAs, not directly by the people, and "the people are the final authority."** **Co-equal in:** non-money bills, constitutional amendments, impeaching the President, removing the Vice President.
11. **Rajya Sabha's special powers:** its **approval is necessary to move a subject from the State List to the Union or Concurrent List**; it can **empower Parliament to legislate on State List matters**; and it **alone can initiate the removal of the Vice President.**
12. **Law-making:** bills are **Government** or **Private Member's**; Government bills split into **Money** and **Non-Money**, and non-money into **Ordinary** and **Constitution Amendment.** ⭐ **"Law making is not merely a legal procedure but also a political course of action"** — parties, interest groups, media and citizens' forums push; the **Cabinet weighs resources, likely opposition, electoral impact and coalition acceptability** before deciding. Drafting is by the **concerned ministry**. Three stages: **introduction** (money bills **only in the Lok Sabha** — Art. 109), **committee** (hence **"miniature legislatures"**), and **voting.** Deadlock → **joint session**, where **the outcome has always favoured the Lok Sabha.** Money bills: the Rajya Sabha **cannot reject**, and inaction for **14 days** means **deemed passed**; its amendments **may or may not be accepted.** Finally, the **President's assent** enacts it.
13. **The danger is "Cabinet dictatorship — the Cabinet leads and the House merely follows."** Control rests on **parliamentary privilege** (no action for anything said in the House; the **presiding officer** decides breach).
14. **Four instruments of control:** ⓵ **Deliberation** — **Question Hour** (**daily; ministers must reply; the most effective device; maximum attendance**) vs **Zero Hour** (**any matter, but ministers need not reply**), plus **half-an-hour discussions** and **adjournment motions**; noisy protest is partly a **political technique to force accountability.** ⓶ **Approval of laws** — real when the government lacks a Rajya Sabha majority (**Janata Party 1977, NDA 2000**); the **Lok Pal Bill failed** and the **Prevention of Terrorism Bill (2002) was rejected by the Rajya Sabha.** ⓷ **Financial control** — the budget is a **constitutional obligation**; misuse is probed via the **CAG** and **Public Accounts Committee**; and **through financial control the legislature controls policy.** ⓸ **No-confidence motion** — the **most powerful weapon**, "fictional rather than real" with a stable majority, but **after 1989 several governments fell** when coalition partners withdrew.
15. **Effective control needs adequate time, interested members and a willingness to compromise** — all under strain: **fewer sessions, less time on debate, absence of quorum, and opposition boycotts.**
16. **Committees:** **standing committees since 1983, over twenty, departmentally related**, supervising a department's **work, budget, expenditure and bills**; **JPCs** (members from **both** Houses) to **discuss a bill or investigate financial irregularities.** They have reduced Parliament's burden, and **Parliament rarely rejects their suggestions** — though legally nothing becomes law without Parliament's approval. **Self-regulation** runs through the **presiding officer** and the **anti-defection law (52nd Amendment, 1985; modified by the 91st)** — defection means **absence against a party direction, voting against instructions, or voluntarily leaving the party**, and costs the member **their seat and any political office.** ⭐ **But it "has not been able to curb defections; it has given additional powers to the party leadership and the presiding officers over the members."**

## 60-Second Revision

RS alone initiates VP's removal; RS consent needed to shift a State List subject.** **Money bill: LS only (Art 109), RS can't reject, 14 days = deemed passed.** **Joint session → always favours LS.** **Committees = "miniature legislatures"; standing committees since 1983, 20+; JPCs from both Houses.** **Cabinet dictatorship is the danger; privilege is the shield.** **Question Hour = daily, ministers MUST reply, most effective; Zero Hour = any matter, ministers NEED NOT reply.** **Janata 1977 / NDA 2000 = no RS majority; Lok Pal Bill failed; POTA Bill 2002 rejected by RS.** **CAG + PAC; financial control = policy control.** **No-confidence = most powerful; fictional pre-1989, real after.** **Decline: fewer sessions, less debate, no quorum, boycotts.** **Anti-defection: 52nd Amendment 1985, modified by 91st; presiding officer decides; absence/voting against/leaving = defection; loses seat + office; BUT it empowered party bosses instead of curbing defection.**

### NCERT Exercises (with answers/pointers)
1. **Alok thinks that if we simply elected our Prime Minister and Ministers and left the task of government to them, we would not need a legislature. Do you agree?** → **No.** *Use: the legislature makes law, controls the purse, ensures accountability (Question Hour, no-confidence), represents divergent groups, debates, amends the Constitution, and elects/removes the President and judges. Without it, "parliamentary democracy may slip into **Cabinet dictatorship**." Efficiency is not the only value — **accountability** is the point.*
2. **The bicameralism debate:**
   - **Neha:** *"Bicameral legislature does not serve any purpose."* → **Disagree** — it provides representation to **all sections and regions** and, above all, ensures **every decision is reconsidered** ("a double check"). Note that **POTA (2002) was rejected by the Rajya Sabha** and governments without a Rajya Sabha majority (1977, 2000) had to make **substantial concessions.**
   - **Shama:** *"Experts should be nominated in the second chamber."* → **Largely agree** — this is exactly the logic of the **twelve nominated members** from **literature, science, art and social service**, and of Purnima Banerji's argument that those who cannot enter "the rough and tumble of active politics" could still advise. **But** the counter is fair too: *whom do they represent?*
   - **Tridib:** *"If a country is not a federation, then there is no need to have a second chamber."* → **Disagree** — federal representation is only **one** of the three justifications. **Reconsideration/revision** and **representation of diverse social sections** apply to unitary states too (the UK's House of Lords is the obvious example).
3. **Why can the Lok Sabha control the executive more effectively than the Rajya Sabha can?** → Because **the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha alone**, so only the Lok Sabha can pass a **no-confidence motion** and dismiss the government; and because **money bills and taxation require Lok Sabha approval**, giving it control of the purse — and *"through financial control, the legislature controls the policy of the government."* The underlying reason is democratic: the Lok Sabha is **directly elected**, and **"the people are the final authority."**
4. **"Rather than effective control of the executive, the Lok Sabha is a platform for the expression of popular sentiments and people's expectations." Do you agree?** *(Balanced answer: **For** — decline in sessions and debate time, absence of quorum, boycotts, and control being "fictional rather than real" when the government has a disciplined majority. **Against** — two Finance Ministers were forced into rollbacks; POTA was rejected; the Lok Pal Bill failed; and **after 1989 several governments were actually forced to resign.**)*
5. **Three proposals for making Parliament more effective:**
   | Proposal | Assessment |
   |---|---|
   | **Parliament should work for longer periods** | ✅ Directly addresses the chapter's first condition — "adequate time at the disposal of the House" — and the documented **decline in sessions.** More sitting days mean more Question Hours and fuller committee scrutiny. |
   | **Attendance should be made compulsory for MPs** | ✅ Would fix the **"absence of quorum"** problem. ⚠️ But it cannot compel *effective participation*, and would sit oddly with **boycott as a legitimate form of protest.** |
   | **Speakers should be empowered to penalise members for interrupting proceedings** | ⚠️ **Double-edged.** It would save legislative time, **but** the chapter warns that disruption is often *"a political technique to gain concessions... and force executive accountability"* — and that the **anti-defection law already gave presiding officers excessive power over members.** Since the Speaker usually belongs to the ruling party, this could **weaken** opposition control of the executive. |
6. **Arif asks: if ministers propose most important bills and the majority party gets them passed, what is Parliament's role in law-making?** → *Parliament's role is **scrutiny, amendment and legitimation**, not authorship: committees (**"miniature legislatures"**) do detailed clause-by-clause work and their suggestions are **rarely rejected**; approvals are **"products of intense bargaining and negotiations"**; a government lacking a Rajya Sabha majority must **concede substantially** (1977, 2000); and bills **do fail** (Lok Pal Bill; POTA 2002). Above all, **no bill becomes law without Parliament's approval.***
7. **Which statement do you agree with most?**
   - *"Legislators must be free to join any party they want."* → Respects the member's conscience and the privilege of speaking "without fear," **but** invites the horse-trading the 52nd Amendment was meant to stop.
   - ✅ *"Anti-defection law has contributed to the domination of the party leaders over the legislators."* → **This is the chapter's own verdict**, and therefore the best-supported answer: the law *"has not been able to curb defections, but it has given additional powers to the party leadership and the presiding officers."*
   - *"Defection is always for selfish purposes and therefore a legislator who wants to join another party must be disqualified from being a minister for the next two years."* → **"Always" is too strong**; and note the law already disqualifies a defector from **any political office**, not just for two years.
8. **Dolly vs Sudha on the efficiency of Parliament.** **Dolly:** decline is evident in **less time on debate and more disturbance and walkouts.** **Sudha:** the **fall of governments on the floor of the Lok Sabha proves its vibrancy.** *(Extra arguments for **Dolly**: fewer sessions, absence of quorum, opposition boycotts, bills passed without discussion, drafting controlled by the Cabinet. Extra arguments for **Sudha**: two forced budget rollbacks, POTA's rejection, the strengthened standing committee system since 1983, high Question Hour attendance, and — post-1989 — genuine no-confidence outcomes. Best answer: **the metrics of decline and the metrics of vitality are measuring different things** — procedural time versus real accountability outcomes.)*
9. ⭐ **Arrange the stages of passing a bill in correct sequence:**
   1. **The concerned minister proposes the need for a bill**
   2. **Legislative department in the Ministry of Law drafts a bill**
   3. **A resolution is passed to admit the bill for discussion**
   4. **The bill is referred to the subcommittee — the committee makes some changes and sends it back to the house for discussion**
   5. **The bill is read clause by clause and each is voted upon**
   6. **The bill is passed in the house in which it was proposed**
   7. **The bill is referred to the other House and is passed**
   8. **The bill is referred to the President of India**
   — ⭐ **What happens if the President does not sign it?** *The President may **return** a non-money bill (not a Money Bill) to Parliament for reconsideration; **if Parliament passes it again, the President must give assent.** But since the Constitution sets **no time limit**, the President may keep it pending indefinitely — the **'pocket veto'**, as with the **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986.***
10. **How has the system of parliamentary committees affected the overseeing and appraisal of legislation by Parliament?** → *Since **1983**, **over twenty departmentally related standing committees** supervise each department's **work, budget, expenditure and bills**; **JPCs** examine particular bills and financial irregularities. Effects: **in-depth scrutiny despite limited sitting time**, a **reduced burden** on the full House, and committees becoming the real site of deliberation — hence **"miniature legislatures."** The trade-off: **Parliament "has merely approved the work done in the committees with few occasional alterations"** and **"rarely rejects"** their suggestions, so effective appraisal has migrated from the floor to the committee room.*
