# Chapter 3: Election and Representation

## The Big Idea
**The chess-and-cricket analogy the chapter opens with:** What if the black knight suddenly started moving straight instead of two-and-a-half squares? What if a cricket match had no umpires? **In any sport we need rules, and an impartial umpire whose decision all players accept — and both must be agreed upon *before* the game begins.**

**What is true of a game is true of elections.** There are different **rules or systems** of conducting elections, and **the outcome of an election depends on the rules adopted.** We also need **machinery to conduct elections impartially.** Since both decisions must be taken *before* the game of electoral politics begins, **they cannot be left to any government** — *that is why these basic decisions about elections are written down in the constitution of a democratic country.*

**Two halves of the chapter:** (i) **fair representation** — the method of election chosen (FPTP vs PR, reservation of constituencies); (ii) **free and fair elections** — universal franchise, right to contest, and an independent Election Commission. Plus the **debate on electoral reforms.**

---

## PART 1 — ELECTIONS AND DEMOCRACY: two framing questions

### Q1: Can we have democracy without holding elections?
This reminds us of the **necessity of representation in a large democracy.** **All citizens cannot take direct part in making every decision**, so representatives are elected by the people. *"Elections have today become the most visible symbol of the democratic process."*

#### Direct vs Indirect democracy
| **Direct democracy** | **Indirect (representative) democracy** |
|---|---|
| Citizens **directly participate in day-to-day decision-making and in running the government** | Citizens **choose representatives** who are actively involved in governing and administering the country |
| Examples: the **ancient city-states in Greece**; and **local governments, especially gram sabhas**, are considered the closest modern examples | Citizens have a **limited role** in major decisions and administration; they are **not very actively involved in making policies** — they are involved **only indirectly**, through elected representatives |
| **Cannot be practised when a decision has to be taken by lakhs and crores of people** | *"Rule by the people usually means rule by people's representatives."* |

**The consequence:** in an arrangement where **all major decisions are taken by elected representatives**, *"the method by which people elect their representatives becomes very important."*

### Q2: Can we hold elections without having democracy?
**Yes — and this is the crucial insight.** *"Not all elections are democratic. A large number of non-democratic countries also hold elections."* In fact **non-democratic rulers are very keen to present themselves as democratic** — they do so by **holding elections in such a way that it does not threaten their rule.**

**This is where the constitution comes in.** The constitution of a democratic country lays down **some basic rules about elections**; the **details are usually left to be worked out by laws passed by the legislatures.**

### ⭐ The five basic questions every democratic constitution must answer
| # | Question | Which aspect it secures |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who is eligible to vote?** | **Free and fair elections** |
| 2 | **Who is eligible to contest?** | **Free and fair elections** |
| 3 | **Who is to supervise elections?** | **Free and fair elections** |
| 4 | **How do the voters choose their representatives?** | **Fair representation** |
| 5 | **How are the votes to be counted and representatives elected?** | **Fair representation** |

*"Like most democratic constitutions, the Constitution of India answers all these questions."* **First three = free and fair; last two = fair representation.**

## PART 2 — THE ELECTION SYSTEM IN INDIA

### Why the counting method is not "obvious"
*"People go and vote. The candidate who gets highest votes gets elected. That is what elections are all over the world. Why do we need to think about it?"*

**Because it is not that simple.** *"We have got so used to our system of elections that we think that there cannot be any other way."* In a democratic election people vote and their preference decides the winner — **but there can be very different ways in which people make their choices, and very different ways in which their preferences can be counted.**

⭐ **These different rules of the game can make a difference to who the winner will be:**
- **Some rules can favour bigger parties; some rules can help the smaller players.**
- **Some rules can favour the majority community; others can protect the minorities.**

---

## FIRST PAST THE POST (FPTP) SYSTEM

### ⭐⭐ The dramatic instance: Lok Sabha 1984 — *"Less than 50 per cent votes and more than 80 per cent seats!"*
- In the **Lok Sabha elections of 1984**, the **Congress party came to power winning 415 of the 543 Lok Sabha seats — more than 80% of the seats.** *"Such a victory was never achieved by any party in the Lok Sabha."*
- **Does winning four-fifths of the seats mean four out of five Indian voters voted Congress? *Actually not.*** **The Congress got only 48% of the votes** — yet won **more than 80% of the seats.**
- **The mirror image:** the **BJP got 7.4 per cent of votes but less than one per cent of seats.**

#### Votes and seats won by major parties, Lok Sabha 1984
| Party | Votes (%) | Seats |
|---|---|---|
| **Congress** | **48.0** | **415** |
| **BJP** | **7.4** | **2** |
| Janata | 6.7 | 10 |
| Lok Dal | 5.7 | 3 |
| CPI (M) | 5.7 | 22 |
| Telugu Desam | 4.1 | 30 |
| DMK | 2.3 | 2 |
| AIADMK | 1.6 | 12 |
| Akali Dal | 1.0 | — |
| AGP | 1.0 | — |

*(Read the pattern, not just the numbers: Congress got ~6.5× BJP's vote share but ~200× its seats. Telugu Desam with 4.1% got 30 seats while Janata with 6.7% got only 10 — because TDP's votes were **geographically concentrated** in one state while Janata's were **thinly spread**.)*

### ⭐ How FPTP works — the three defining rules
1. **The entire country is divided into 543 constituencies.**
2. **Each constituency elects one representative.**
3. **The candidate who secures the highest number of votes in that constituency is declared elected.**

> **⚠️ The single most important point:** *"Whoever has **more votes than all other candidates** is declared elected. **The winning candidate need not secure a majority of the votes.**"*

**The names:** *"In the electoral race, the candidate who is ahead of others, who crosses the winning post first of all, is the winner."* This is called the **First Past the Post (FPTP)** system, **also called the Plurality System.** **This is the method of election prescribed by the Constitution.**

### ⭐ Why the 1984 distortion happened — the "wasted votes" logic
- Congress won a **greater share of seats than its share of votes** because **in many constituencies where its candidates won, they secured less than 50% of the votes.**
- **If there are several candidates, the winning candidate often gets much less than 50% of the votes.**
- **The votes that go to all the losing candidates go 'waste'** — those candidates or parties **get no seat from those votes.**
- **The extreme illustration:** *"Suppose a party gets **only 25 per cent of the votes in every constituency**, but everyone else gets even less votes. **In that case, the party could win ALL the seats with only 25 per cent votes or even less.**"*

## PROPORTIONAL REPRESENTATION (PR) SYSTEM

### How it works
Compare Israel. **Once the votes are counted, each party is allotted the share of seats in the parliament in proportion to its share of votes.** Each party then **fills its quota of seats by picking that many of its nominees from a preference list declared before the elections.**

**Definition:** *In the PR system **a party gets the same proportion of seats as its proportion of votes.***

### ⭐ Two variations of PR
| Variation | How constituencies are drawn | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **(i) Whole country = one constituency** | Seats allocated to each party according to its share of votes in the **national** election | **Israel, Netherlands** |
| **(ii) Country divided into several multi-member constituencies** | Each party prepares a **list of candidates for each constituency**, depending on how many are to be elected from it | **Argentina, Portugal** |

**In both variations:**
- **Voters exercise their preference for a PARTY, not a candidate.**
- **Seats in a constituency are distributed on the basis of votes polled by a party.**
- **Therefore representatives from one constituency would and do belong to different parties.**

> **The chapter voices the natural objection:** *"This is very confusing! How do I know who is my MP or my MLA in this system? Who would I go to if I have any work?"* — this is precisely FPTP's advantage (see below).

### ⭐ Box: Proportional Representation in Israel (learn the specifics)
- Israel follows the PR system. Elections to the legislature — **the Knesset** — take place **every four years.**
- **Every party declares a list of its candidates, but voters vote for the party and not for the candidates.**
- A party gets seats **in proportion to the votes polled by it.** *"This allows even smaller parties with very small support base to get representation in the legislature."*
- ⭐ **Threshold: a party must get a minimum of 3.25% votes to be eligible to get seats.**
- ⭐ **Consequence: "This often leads to a multi-party coalition government."**

**2015 Knesset election result (total 120 seats):**
| Party / List | % of votes | Seats |
|---|---|---|
| Likud | 23.40 | 30 |
| Zionist Camp | 18.67 | 24 |
| Joint List (Hadash, National Democratic Assembly, Arab Movement for Renewal, United Arab List) | 10.61 | 13 |
| Yesh Atid | 8.82 | 11 |
| Kulanu | 7.49 | 10 |
| Habayit Hayehudi | 6.74 | 8 |
| Shas | 5.74 | 7 |
| Yisrael Beitenu | 5.10 | 6 |
| United Torah Judaism | 4.99 | 6 |
| Israel's Left | 3.93 | 5 |

| **Total** | **100** | **120** |

*(Note the near-exact match between vote % and seat % — 23.40% → 30/120 = 25%. Compare with 1984 India: 48% → 76% of seats. That gap **is** the difference between the two systems.)*

### ⭐⭐ Comparison of FPTP and PR (a guaranteed exam table)
| **FPTP** | **PR** |
|---|---|
| The country is divided into **small geographical units** called **constituencies** or districts | **Large geographical areas** are demarcated as constituencies; **the entire country may be a single constituency** |
| **Every constituency elects one representative** | **More than one representative may be elected** from one constituency |
| **Voter votes for a CANDIDATE** | **Voter votes for the PARTY** |
| **A party may get more seats than votes** in the legislature | **Every party gets seats in proportion to the percentage of votes it gets** |
| The candidate who wins **may not get majority (50%+1) votes** | The candidate who wins **gets majority of votes** |
| **Examples: U.K., India** | **Examples: Israel, Netherlands** |

### ⭐ Where India *does* use PR
*"In India, we have adopted PR system **on a limited scale for indirect elections.**"* **The Constitution prescribes a third and complex variation of the PR system for the election of:**
- the **President**
- the **Vice-President**
- the **Rajya Sabha**
- the **Vidhan Parishads** (State Legislative Councils)

### ⭐⭐ Box: How PR works in Rajya Sabha elections — Single Transferable Vote (STV)
*A third variant of PR, the **Single Transferable Vote system (STV)**, is followed for Rajya Sabha elections.*
- **Every State has a specific quota of seats in the Rajya Sabha.**
- **The members are elected by the respective State Legislative Assemblies** — **the voters are the MLAs in that State.**
- **Every voter is required to rank candidates according to her or his preference.**
- To be declared the winner, **a candidate must secure a minimum quota of votes**, determined by a formula:

> ### The quota formula
> $$\text{Quota} = \frac{\text{Total votes polled}}{\text{Total number of candidates to be elected} + 1} + 1$$

**Worked example from the chapter:** *If **4** Rajya Sabha members have to be elected by the **200** MLAs in Rajasthan, the winner would require* **(200 / (4+1)) + 1 = 40 + 1 = 41 votes.**

**The counting procedure:**
1. Counting is done **on the basis of first preference votes** secured by each candidate.
2. **If, after counting all first-preference votes, the required number of candidates fail to fulfil the quota**, then **the candidate who secured the lowest number of first-preference votes is eliminated**, and **his/her votes are transferred to those mentioned as second preference on those ballot papers.**
3. **This process continues till the required number of candidates are declared elected.**

---

### ⭐⭐ Why did India adopt FPTP? (five reasons — a certain exam question)
1. **Simplicity.** The STV/PR method *"is a complicated system which may work in a small country, but would be difficult to work in a sub-continental country like India."* The FPTP system is *"extremely simple to understand even for common voters who may have no specialised knowledge about politics and elections."*
2. **A clear choice is presented to voters.** Voters **simply endorse a candidate or a party.** Depending on the nature of actual politics, voters may **give greater importance to the party, or to the candidate, or balance the two** — FPTP *"offers voters a choice not simply between parties but specific candidates."*
3. **Local accountability.** In PR systems voters are asked to choose a party and representatives are elected from **party lists** — so *"there is **no one representative who represents and is responsible for one locality**."* In a constituency-based system like FPTP, **"the voters know who their own representative is and can hold him or her accountable."**
4. ⭐ **Stability in a parliamentary system.** The makers of the Constitution felt **PR-based election may not be suitable for giving a stable government in a parliamentary system**, which *requires that the executive has a majority in the legislature.* **PR may not produce a clear majority**, because seats would be divided on the basis of vote share. **FPTP generally gives the largest party or coalition some extra bonus seats, more than their share of votes would allow** — thus *"makes it possible for parliamentary government to function smoothly and effectively by facilitating the formation of a stable government."*
5. ⭐ **It encourages cross-community coalitions at the local level.** **FPTP encourages voters from different social groups to come together to win an election in a locality.** In a diverse country like India, *"a **PR system would encourage each community to form its own nation-wide party**."* This may also have been at the back of the constitution makers' minds.

### ⭐ Has the expectation been borne out? — the verdict on FPTP in India
*"The experience of the working of the Constitution has confirmed the expectation of the constitution makers."*
- The FPTP system has proved **simple and familiar to ordinary voters.**
- It has **helped larger parties win clear majorities at the Centre and the State level.**
- ⭐ It **has discouraged political parties that get all their votes only from one caste or community.**

**But India departs from the textbook prediction.** *"Normally, the working of the FPTP system results in a **two-party system**"* — two major competitors for power, sharing it alternately, with **new or third parties finding it difficult to enter.** In India:
- **After independence**, though we adopted FPTP, **there emerged a one-party dominance, alongside many smaller parties.**
- ⭐ **After 1989, India has been witnessing the functioning of multi-party coalitions.**
- **At the same time, gradually, in many States a two-party competition is emerging.**
- ⭐ **The distinguishing feature of India's party system: "the rise of coalitions has made it possible for new and smaller parties to enter into electoral competition IN SPITE OF the FPTP system."**

## PART 3 — RESERVATION OF CONSTITUENCIES

### The problem FPTP creates for small social groups
In FPTP, the candidate with the highest votes in a constituency is elected — and *"this often works to the disadvantage of the smaller social groups."* **This is even more significant in the Indian social context**, given our **history of caste-based discrimination**: in such a system **the dominant social groups and castes can win everywhere and the oppressed social groups may continue to remain unrepresented.**

**The constitution makers were aware of this and of the need to ensure fair and just representation to oppressed social groups.**

### ⭐⭐ Separate electorates vs Reserved constituencies (the key distinction — Exercise Q5)
| **Separate electorates** (introduced by the British; **rejected**) | **Reserved constituencies** (**adopted**) |
|---|---|
| For electing a representative from a particular community, **ONLY those voters would be eligible who belong to that community** | **ALL voters in a constituency are eligible to vote** |
| — | **But the candidates must belong to only a particular community or social section for which the seat is reserved** |
| Effect: **divides the electorate**, so a representative answers only to their own community | Effect: **keeps the electorate united**, while guaranteeing that the elected member comes from the reserved group |

*"This issue was debated even before independence and the British government had introduced 'separate electorates'... In the Constituent Assembly, many members expressed a fear that this will not suit our purposes. **Therefore, it was decided to adopt the system of reserved constituencies.**"*

> ### ⭐ Constituent Assembly voices — memorise these two
> **Against separate electorates:**
> *"**Separate electorates have been a curse to India**, have done incalculable harm to this country... Separate electorates have barred our progress... We (Muslims) want to merge in the nation. ...for God's sake keep your hands off reservation for the Muslim community."*
> — **Tajamul Husain, CAD, Vol. VIII, p. 333, 26 May 1949**
>
> **For reserved seats (but not separate electorates):**
> *"...But I have come to say a few words on behalf of the Adivasis of India.... In the past, thanks to the major political parties, thanks to the British Government and thanks to every enlightened Indian citizen, **we have been isolated and kept, as it were, in a zoo.** ...**We are willing to mix with you**, and it is for that reason ... that **we have insisted on a reservation of seats** as far as the Legislatures are concerned. **We have NOT asked ...(for) separate electorates**; ...Under the **1935 Act**, throughout the Legislatures in India, **there were altogether only 24 Adivasi MLAs out of a total of 1585**, ...and **not a single representative at the Centre.**"*
> — **Jaipal Singh, CAD, Vol. V, pp. 209–210, 27 August 1947**

### Why reservation is needed for dispersed groups
*"There are certain social groups which may be spread across the country. In a particular constituency, their numbers may not be sufficient to be able to influence a victory of a candidate. However, taken across the country they are a significantly sizeable group. To ensure their proper representation, a system of reservation becomes necessary."*

### ⭐ Hard facts on reservation of seats
| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Where reserved | **Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies**, for **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes** |
| Original duration | Provided **initially for a period of 10 years** |
| Extended | By **successive constitutional amendments, extended up to 2030** |
| Further extension | **Parliament can decide to further extend it** when the period expires |
| Basis of numbers | **In proportion to their share in the population of India** |
| **SC seats in Lok Sabha** | **84** (of the 543 elected seats) — *as on 26 January 2019* |
| **ST seats in Lok Sabha** | **47** — *as on 26 January 2019* |

### ⭐ The Delimitation Commission — who decides which constituency is reserved
| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | An **independent body** |
| **Appointed by** | **The President of India** |
| **Works with** | **In collaboration with the Election Commission of India** |
| **Purpose** | **Drawing up the boundaries of constituencies all over the country** |
| **Quota per State** | A quota of constituencies to be reserved in each State is **fixed depending on the proportion of SC or ST in that State** |

**How it picks the reserved seats (note the deliberate asymmetry between ST and SC):**
- **For ST:** after drawing boundaries, the Commission looks at the composition of population in each constituency; **those constituencies with the *highest proportion* of Scheduled Tribe population are reserved for ST.**
- **For SC — two things:** (i) it picks constituencies with a **higher proportion of Scheduled Caste population**; **but** (ii) it also **spreads these constituencies across different regions of the State.** ⭐ **Why? "This is done because the Scheduled Caste population is generally spread evenly throughout the country."**
- ⭐ **These reserved constituencies can be rotated each time the delimitation exercise is undertaken.**

### ⭐ Women's reservation — the current position
- **The Constitution does not make similar reservation for other disadvantaged groups.**
- **Of late there has been a strong demand for reservation of seats in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for women.** Given that **very few women are elected to representative bodies**, the demand for **reserving one-third of seats for women was articulated for many years.**
- **Reservation of seats for women has already been provided for rural and urban local bodies** *(see the chapter on Local Governments)*.
- ⭐⭐ **A similar provision for Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabhas has been provided through the *Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam* (Women's Reservation Act, 2023).**

### Box: "Check your progress" — the under-representation of Muslims
*The proportion of Muslims in India's population is about **14.2 per cent**. But the number of Muslim MPs in the Lok Sabha has usually been **less than 6 per cent** — **less than half their share in the population.** A similar situation prevails in most State Assemblies.*

## PART 4 — FREE AND FAIR ELECTIONS

*"The true test of any election system is its ability to ensure a free and fair electoral process."* For democracy to be translated into reality, the system must be **impartial and transparent**, and must **allow the aspirations of the voter to find legitimate expression through the electoral results.**

### Universal adult franchise
- Democratic elections require that **all adult citizens of the country must be eligible to vote.** This is **universal adult franchise.**
- *"In many countries, citizens had to fight long battles with the rulers to get this right. In many countries, **women could get this right very late and only after struggle.**"*
- **One of the important decisions of the framers of the Indian Constitution was to guarantee every adult citizen in India the right to vote.**
- ⭐⭐ **Till 1989, an adult Indian meant an Indian citizen above the age of 21. An amendment to the Constitution in 1989 reduced the eligibility age to 18.** *(This is the 61st Amendment.)*
- **Adult franchise ensures that all citizens are able to participate in selecting their representative** — *"consistent with the principle of equality and non-discrimination"* studied in the chapter on rights.
- ⭐ **Many people thought — and many think so today — that giving the right to vote to everyone irrespective of educational qualification was not right.** *But our Constitution makers had a firm belief in the ability and worth of all adult citizens as equals in the matter of deciding what is good for the society, the country and for their own constituencies.*

> *The chapter puts the counter-question in the student's voice:* *"Am I or am I not an adult? I am mature enough to choose my future career, old enough to get a driving licence, but not old enough to vote! **If laws can apply to me, why can't I decide on who makes those laws?**"*
>
> *And a Shankar cartoon (21 October 1951) compares **universal adult franchise to an elephant** — is it unmanageable, or is it like the story where everyone describes the elephant only by its parts?*

### ⭐ Right to contest — eligibility and disqualification
- **All citizens have the right to stand for election and become the representative of the people.**
- **Minimum age:** *"in order to stand for **Lok Sabha or Assembly election, a candidate must be at least 25 years old**."* **(Different offices have different minimum ages.)**
- ⭐ **Disqualification:** *"there is a legal provision that a person who has undergone **imprisonment for two or more years** for some offence is **disqualified** from contesting elections."*
- ⭐⭐ **The open-access principle:** *"But there are **no restrictions of income, education or class or gender** on the right to contest elections. In this sense, our system of election is open to all citizens."*

## PART 5 — THE INDEPENDENT ELECTION COMMISSION

**The most important of the efforts to ensure free and fair elections is the creation of an independent Election Commission to 'supervise and conduct' elections.** *"Do you know that in many countries, there is an absence of an independent mechanism for conducting elections?"*

> ### ⭐⭐ Bare text to memorise — **Article 324(1)**
> *"The **superintendence, direction and control** of the preparation of the **electoral rolls** for, and the **conduct of, all elections to Parliament and to the Legislature of every State** and of **elections to the offices of President and Vice-President** held under this Constitution **shall be vested in a Commission** (referred to in this Constitution as the Election Commission)."*

**Why the wording matters:** *"These words in the Constitution are very important, for they give the Election Commission a **decisive role in virtually everything to do with elections.** **The Supreme Court has agreed with this interpretation** of the Constitution."*

### Structure and jurisdiction
- **To assist the Election Commission of India there is a Chief Electoral Officer in every State.**
- ⭐⭐ **The Election Commission is NOT responsible for the conduct of local body elections.** **The State Election Commissioners work independently of the Election Commission of India, and each has its own sphere of operation.** *(See the chapter on Local Government.)*

### ⭐ Single-member or multi-member? — the full timeline
*"The Election Commission of India can either be a single member or a multi-member body."*
| Period | Composition |
|---|---|
| **Till 1989** | **Single member** |
| **Just before the 1989 general elections** | **Two Election Commissioners were appointed, making the body multi-member** |
| **Soon after the 1989 elections** | **Reverted to single-member status** |
| **In 1993** | **Two Election Commissioners were once again appointed** — the Commission became multi-member **and has remained multi-member since then** |

**The controversy:** *"Initially there were many apprehensions about a multi-member Commission. There was a **sharp difference of opinion between the then Chief Election Commissioner and the other Commissioners about who had how much power. The matter had to be settled by the Supreme Court.**"*

⭐ **The settled consensus:** *"Now there is a general consensus that a **multi-member Election Commission is more appropriate as power is shared and there is greater accountability.**"*

### ⭐⭐ The CEC's position — a classic trap
- **The Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) presides over the Election Commission, BUT does NOT have more powers than the other Election Commissioners.**
- **The CEC and the two Election Commissioners have EQUAL powers to take all decisions relating to elections as a collective body.**

### Appointment — and the criticism of it
- **They are appointed by the President of India on the advice of the Council of Ministers.**
- ⭐ **The problem:** *"It is therefore possible for **a ruling party to appoint a partisan person** to the Commission who might favour them in the elections. This fear has led many to suggest that this procedure should be changed."*
- ⭐ **The reform suggested:** *"Many persons have suggested that a different method should be followed that makes **consultation with the Leader of the Opposition and the Chief Justice of India necessary** for the appointment of the CEC and Election Commissioners."*

### ⭐⭐ Security of tenure and removal — note the asymmetry
| | **Chief Election Commissioner** | **Election Commissioners** |
|---|---|---|
| **Term** | **Six-year term, or till the age of 65, whichever is earlier** | **Six-year term, or till the age of 65, whichever is earlier** |
| **Removal** | Can be removed before expiry of the term **by the President ONLY IF BOTH Houses of Parliament make such a recommendation with a SPECIAL MAJORITY** | **Can be removed by the President of India** *(i.e. no such parliamentary protection)* |

⭐ **Why the CEC's protection exists:** *"This is done to ensure that **a ruling party cannot remove a CEC who refuses to favour it in elections.**"*

### ⭐ Box: What is a "special majority"? (learn the two-part definition)
**Special majority means:**
1. **Two-thirds majority of those present and voting**, **AND**
2. **Simple majority of the total membership of the House.**

**The chapter's worked puzzle:** *A class has a total strength of **57**. On the day of voting only **51 students are present** and **50 participated in voting.** When has the resolution passed with special majority?* → It needs **two-thirds of those voting = 2/3 × 50 ≈ 34**, **and** **a simple majority of total membership = more than 28.5, i.e. at least 29.** **So at least 34 votes in favour** satisfies both conditions.

⭐ *"In this book you will find mention of 'special majority' in at least three other chapters. One is in the next chapter on Executive, where we discuss the **impeachment of the President of India**."* **The other places to look: the removal of judges (Chapter 6, Judiciary) and the amendment of the Constitution (Chapter 9, Constitution as a Living Document).**

### ⭐⭐ Functions of the Election Commission of India
**1. Electoral rolls.** *"It supervises the preparation of an up-to-date voters' list. It makes every effort to ensure that the voters' list is free of errors like non-existence of names of registered voters or existence of names of those non-eligible or non-existent."*

**2. Timing and schedule.** It **determines the timing of elections and prepares the election schedule**, which includes:
- **notification** of elections
- **date from which nominations can be filed**
- **last date for filing nominations**
- **last date of scrutiny**
- **last date of withdrawal**
- **date of polling**
- **date of counting and declaration of results**

**3. Powers to ensure a free and fair poll.** During this entire process the EC can:
- ⭐ **Postpone or cancel the election in the entire country, or a specific State or constituency**, on the grounds that **the atmosphere is vitiated and a free and fair election may not be possible**
- **Implement a model code of conduct for parties and candidates**
- **Order a re-poll in a specific constituency**
- **Order a recount of votes** when it feels the counting process has not been fully fair and just

**4. Recognition and symbols.** *"The Election Commission accords recognition to political parties and allots symbols to each of them."*

### ⭐ Control over the administrative machinery
- **The Election Commission has very limited staff of its own** — it **conducts elections with the help of the administrative machinery.**
- ⭐ **However, once the election process has begun, the Commission has control over the administration as far as election-related work is concerned.**
- During the election process, **administrative officers of the State and central governments are assigned election-related duty**, and in this respect the **EC has full control over them:**
  - **It can transfer the officers, or stop their transfers**
  - **It can take action against them for failing to act in a non-partisan manner**

### ⭐⭐ The most important insight in this chapter: powers unchanged, use transformed
*"Over the years, the Election Commission of India has emerged as an independent authority which has asserted its powers to ensure fairness in the election process..."*

> **"The record of the Election Commission also shows that **every improvement in the functioning of institutions does not require legal or constitutional change.** It is widely agreed that the Election Commission is **more independent and assertive now than it was till 25 years ago.** **This is NOT because the powers and constitutional protection of the Election Commission have increased. The Election Commission has started using more effectively the powers it always had in the Constitution.**"**

*(This is one of the most quotable lines in the whole book — use it in any Mains answer on institutional reform.)*

### ⭐ The EC's record — the difficult situations it has handled
- **Since 1951-52, seventeen Lok Sabha elections have been held**, plus many more State assembly elections and by-elections.
- **Holding elections in militancy-affected areas** like **Assam, Punjab, and Jammu and Kashmir.**
- ⭐ **1991: it had to postpone the election process mid-way when former Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated during campaigning.**
- ⭐⭐ **2002 — Gujarat:** the **Gujarat Assembly was dissolved** and elections had to be conducted. **But the EC found that unprecedented violence in that State had made it impossible to hold free and fair elections immediately. The EC decided to postpone elections to the State Assembly by a few months — and the Supreme Court upheld this decision of the Election Commission.**

*(An R.K. Laxman cartoon captures it: "The Netaji is afraid of EC." **Why do leaders fear the Election Commission? Is this good for democracy?**)*

### Box: "Check your progress" — why the EC needs each power
| Power / privilege | Why it exists | What could have happened without it |
|---|---|---|
| The Commission can **issue orders to government employees engaged in election-related duty** | The EC has almost no staff of its own and must borrow the state machinery | Officials would answer to the ruling party, not the EC — partisan conduct of the poll itself |
| **The government cannot remove the Chief Election Commissioner** | Security of tenure (special majority of both Houses) | A ruling party could sack any CEC who refused to favour it |
| The Commission can **cancel an election if it thinks it was not fair** | The ultimate deterrent against rigging and a vitiated atmosphere | Rigging would go unremedied; malpractice would pay |

## PART 6 — ELECTORAL REFORMS

*"**No system of election can ever be perfect.** And in actual election process, there are bound to be many flaws and limitations. Any democratic society has to keep searching for mechanisms to make elections free and fair to the maximum."*

**What India already has:** the acceptance of **adult suffrage**, **freedom to contest elections**, and the establishment of an **independent Election Commission.** But the experience of the past **72 years** has produced many suggestions for reform, from **the Election Commission, political parties, various independent groups, and many scholars.**

### ⭐⭐ The six reform proposals listed (learn all six)
1. **Change the system of elections from FPTP to some variant of the PR system**, so that parties get seats, as far as possible, **in proportion to the votes they get.**
2. **A special provision to ensure that at least one-third women are elected to Parliament and the assemblies.**
3. **Stricter provisions to control the role of money in electoral politics** — *"the election expenses should be paid by the government out of a special fund"* **(state funding of elections).**
4. ⭐ **Candidates with any criminal case should be barred from contesting elections, *even if their appeal is pending before a court.***
5. **A complete ban on the use of caste and religious appeals in the campaign.**
6. **A law to regulate the functioning of political parties**, and to ensure they **function in a transparent and democratic manner.**

### ⭐ The honest caveat — the limits of law
*"These are but a few suggestions. **There is no consensus about these suggestions.** Even if there was a consensus, **there are limits to what the laws and formal provisions can do.** Free and fair elections can be held only if **the candidates, the parties and those involved in the election process agree to abide by the spirit of democratic competition.**"*

**Two non-legal routes to better elections:**
1. **People themselves have to be more vigilant, more actively involved in political activities** — *"But there are limits to the extent to which ordinary people can engage in politics on a regular basis."*
2. **Therefore it is necessary that various political institutions and voluntary organisations are developed and are active in functioning as watchdogs for ensuring free and fair elections.**

## Conclusion — five measures of the success of India's election system
*"In countries where representative democracy is practised, elections and the representative character of those elections are crucial factors in making democracy effective and trustworthy."*

1. **Peaceful change of government.** Our election system has allowed voters not only to **freely choose representatives, but also to change governments peacefully both at the State and national level.**
2. **Sustained participation.** **Voters have consistently taken a keen interest in the election process and participated in it.** *"The number of candidates and parties that contest elections is on the rise."*
3. **Accommodative and inclusive.** **The social composition of our representatives has changed gradually** — representatives now come from many different social sections — **"though the number of women legislators has not increased satisfactorily."**
4. **Malpractice does not usually decide outcomes.** *"The election outcome in most parts of the country does not reflect electoral malpractices and rigging. Of course, **many attempts at rigging do take place** — violence, voters' names disappearing from the voters' list, intimidation. **Yet such instances rarely directly affect the outcome of the election.**"*
5. ⭐ **Elections are now part and parcel of our democratic life.** *"**No one can imagine a situation where a government would disrespect the verdict of an election.** Similarly, no one can imagine that a government would be formed without holding elections. In fact, **regularity and periodicity of elections has earned fame for India as a great democratic experiment.**"*

*"The voter in India has gained confidence. The legitimacy of the Election Commission has increased in the eyes of the people. This vindicates the basic decisions taken by our Constitution makers."*

> *"We, the citizens of India, having abiding faith in democracy, hereby pledge to uphold the democratic traditions of our country and the dignity of free, fair and peaceful elections, and to vote in every election fearlessly and without being influenced by considerations of **religion, race, caste, community, language or any inducement.**"*

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Direct democracy** | Citizens directly participate in day-to-day decision-making; ancient Greek city-states; **gram sabhas** are the closest modern example |
| **Indirect / representative democracy** | Citizens choose representatives who govern; citizens are involved only indirectly |
| **First Past the Post (FPTP)** | Country divided into constituencies, each electing **one** representative; the candidate with **the highest votes wins — a majority is not required.** Also called the **Plurality System.** Prescribed by the Indian Constitution |
| **Plurality** | More votes than any other candidate, but not necessarily more than half |
| **Wasted votes** | Votes cast for all losing candidates, which yield no seat — the mechanism behind FPTP's vote–seat distortion |
| **Proportional Representation (PR)** | Each party gets seats **in proportion to its share of votes**; voters vote for a **party**, not a candidate |
| **Knesset** | The legislature of **Israel**; elected every **four years** by PR with a **3.25% threshold** |
| **Single Transferable Vote (STV)** | The third PR variant used for **Rajya Sabha** elections; MLAs rank candidates; a quota must be met; lowest candidate eliminated and votes transferred to second preferences |
| **Quota (STV)** | (Total votes polled ÷ (seats to be filled + 1)) + 1 |
| **Separate electorates** | British-introduced system where **only voters of a community could elect that community's representative** — **rejected** by the Constituent Assembly |
| **Reserved constituencies** | **All voters vote**, but **only candidates from the specified community may contest** — the system **adopted** |
| **Delimitation Commission** | Independent body **appointed by the President**, working with the ECI, to **draw constituency boundaries** and decide which are reserved |
| **Universal adult franchise** | All adult citizens eligible to vote; age lowered from **21 to 18 by a 1989 amendment** |
| **Article 324** | Vests **superintendence, direction and control** of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections in the **Election Commission** |
| **Chief Electoral Officer** | Officer in every State who assists the Election Commission of India |
| **State Election Commissioner** | Conducts **local body** elections; works **independently** of the ECI |
| **Special majority** | **Two-thirds of those present and voting** *and* **a simple majority of the total membership** of the House |
| **Model code of conduct** | Rules for parties and candidates during elections, implemented by the EC |
| ***Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*** | The **Women's Reservation Act, 2023**, providing reservation for women in the **Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabhas** |
| **SVEEP** | Systematic Voters' Education and Electoral Participation — the ECI's voter-awareness programme |

## Quick Recap (15 points)
1. **Rules and umpire must be fixed before the game** — so the basic decisions about elections **cannot be left to any government** and are **written into the Constitution.**
2. **Direct democracy** = ancient Greek city-states, and **gram sabhas** today; impossible for lakhs and crores. **Indirect democracy** makes the *method* of electing representatives decisive.
3. **Elections without democracy are possible** — **non-democratic rulers hold elections designed not to threaten their rule.** Hence constitutional guarantees.
4. **Five constitutional questions:** who may vote, who may contest, who supervises *(= free and fair)*; how voters choose, how votes are counted *(= fair representation)*.
5. **FPTP:** **543 constituencies, one representative each, highest votes wins — no majority needed.** Also called the **Plurality System.** **Prescribed by the Constitution.**
6. **1984 Lok Sabha is the set-piece example: Congress 48% votes → 415 of 543 seats (>80%); BJP 7.4% votes → 2 seats.** Cause: winners taking under 50% in many seats, and **all votes for losers going 'waste'.** A party with **25% in every constituency could win every seat.**
7. **PR:** seats proportional to votes; **voters vote for a party**; two variants — **whole country as one constituency (Israel, Netherlands)** and **multi-member constituencies (Argentina, Portugal)**. Israel's **Knesset**, 4-year terms, **3.25% threshold**, tends to **multi-party coalitions.**
8. **India uses PR only for indirect elections:** **President, Vice-President, Rajya Sabha, Vidhan Parishads** — via **STV**, quota = (votes ÷ (seats+1)) + 1 *(e.g. 200 MLAs, 4 seats → 41)*, with elimination and transfer of preferences.
9. **Five reasons India chose FPTP:** simplicity; clear choice of a **specific candidate**; a **known, locally accountable representative**; **stable majority government** for a parliamentary system (FPTP gives the largest party **bonus seats**); and it **pushes social groups to coalesce locally** rather than each community forming a nationwide party.
10. **Result in practice:** FPTP normally produces a **two-party system**; **India instead had one-party dominance, then multi-party coalitions after 1989**, with two-party competition emerging in many States — and **coalitions have let small parties in *despite* FPTP.** It has also **discouraged single-caste/community parties.**
11. **Separate electorates (only that community votes) were REJECTED; reserved constituencies (everyone votes, only that community contests) were ADOPTED.** Remember **Tajamul Husain** ("a curse to India") and **Jaipal Singh** ("kept, as it were, in a zoo"; only **24 Adivasi MLAs out of 1585** under the **1935 Act**).
12. **Reservation:** SC/ST seats in **Lok Sabha and State Assemblies**, in proportion to population; originally for **10 years**, now extended to **2030**; **84 SC and 47 ST** of 543 (as on 26 Jan 2019). The **Delimitation Commission** (appointed by the **President**, working with the ECI) draws boundaries; **ST seats go to the highest-ST constituencies, SC seats are also *spread across regions* because the SC population is evenly distributed**; reserved seats **rotate** at each delimitation. Women's reservation for Lok Sabha/Vidhan Sabhas came via the ***Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam* (2023)**.
13. **Franchise and candidature:** voting age **21 → 18 by the 1989 amendment**; minimum age to contest Lok Sabha/Assembly is **25**; **imprisonment of two or more years disqualifies**; but **no bar of income, education, class or gender.**
14. **Election Commission (Article 324):** superintendence, direction and control of rolls and of elections to Parliament, State legislatures, **President and Vice-President** — **but NOT local bodies** (that is the **State Election Commissioner**). **Single-member till 1989 → multi-member just before the 1989 polls → single again → multi-member from 1993 onwards.** **CEC presides but has no more power than the other Commissioners** — all decide **collectively with equal powers.** Appointed by the **President on the advice of the Council of Ministers** (criticised; reform suggests consulting the **Leader of the Opposition and the CJI**). **Six-year term or age 65, whichever is earlier**; **the CEC can be removed only on a special-majority recommendation of both Houses**, while **other Commissioners can be removed by the President.** It can **postpone or cancel polls, order re-polls and recounts, enforce the model code, recognise parties and allot symbols**, and — once the process starts — **transfer or block transfers of officials and act against partisan conduct.**
15. **The EC's assertiveness came from using powers it always had, not from new powers** — proof that *"every improvement in the functioning of institutions does not require legal or constitutional change."* Landmarks: **17 Lok Sabha elections since 1951-52**; polls in **Assam, Punjab, J&K**; **mid-way postponement in 1991 after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination**; **postponement of the 2002 Gujarat Assembly election, upheld by the Supreme Court.** **Six reform proposals:** move toward PR; one-third women; **state funding** to curb money power; bar candidates with **any criminal case even if appeal is pending**; ban **caste and religious appeals**; and a law to make **parties transparent and democratic.**

## 60-Second Revision
**FPTP = 543 seats, one each, plurality wins, no majority needed, prescribed by the Constitution. 1984: Congress 48% → 415 seats; BJP 7.4% → 2.** **Wasted votes explain it.** **PR = seats ∝ votes, vote for the party; Israel/Netherlands (one constituency) vs Argentina/Portugal (multi-member); Knesset, 4 yrs, 3.25% threshold → coalitions.** **India uses PR/STV only for President, VP, Rajya Sabha, Vidhan Parishads; quota = votes/(seats+1) + 1 → 200 MLAs, 4 seats = 41.** **Why FPTP: simple, candidate-specific, locally accountable, stable majorities (bonus seats), discourages community-only parties.** **Separate electorates rejected; reserved constituencies adopted. 84 SC + 47 ST of 543; extended to 2030; Delimitation Commission appointed by President; ST = highest concentration, SC = also spread out (evenly distributed population); seats rotate. Women: Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam 2023.** **Vote at 18 since the 1989 amendment (was 21); contest at 25; 2 years' imprisonment disqualifies; no income/education/class/gender bar.** **Article 324 = superintendence, direction, control — NOT local bodies.** **EC: single till 1989 → multi (1989) → single → multi from 1993. CEC presides but has equal power; 6 years or 65; CEC removable only by special majority of both Houses, others by the President.** **Special majority = 2/3 present-and-voting + simple majority of total membership.** **1991 Rajiv Gandhi postponement; 2002 Gujarat postponement upheld by SC.** **The EC grew strong by using powers it always had.** **Six reforms: PR, 1/3 women, state funding, bar the criminally accused, ban caste/religious appeals, regulate parties.**
