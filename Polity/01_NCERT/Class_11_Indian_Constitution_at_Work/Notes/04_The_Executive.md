# Chapter 4: Executive

## The Big Idea
**Legislature, executive and judiciary are the three organs of government.** Together they perform the functions of government, maintain law and order, and look after the welfare of the people. **The Constitution ensures that they work in coordination with each other and maintain a balance among themselves.**

⭐ **The defining feature of a parliamentary system:** *"executive and the legislature are **interdependent**: the legislature controls the executive, and, in turn, is controlled by the executive."*

**This chapter covers four things:** the distinction between **parliamentary and presidential** executives; the **constitutional position of the President of India**; the **composition and functioning of the Council of Ministers and the importance of the Prime Minister**; and the **administrative machinery** (the permanent executive).

---

## PART 1 — WHAT IS AN EXECUTIVE?

### The concept
In any organisation, some office holder has to take decisions and implement them — we call this activity **administration or management.** But administration requires **a body at the top that will take policy decisions or the big decisions and supervise and coordinate the routine administrative functioning.**

Every formal group has a body functioning as its **chief administrators or executives**. *"Some office holders decide the policies and rules and regulations and then some office holders implement those decisions in the actual day-to-day functioning."*

**Definition:** *"The word **executive** means a body of persons that looks after the **implementation of rules and regulations in actual practice.**"*

**In government:** one body may take policy decisions and decide rules and regulations, while another is in charge of implementing them. ⭐ ***"The organ of government that primarily looks after the function of implementation and administration is called the executive."***

### Principal functions
**The executive is the branch of government responsible for the implementation of laws and policies adopted by the legislature.** ⭐ **But note:** *"The executive is often involved in the framing of policy"* too — it is not purely an implementing body.

**Designations vary from country to country:** some countries have **presidents**, others have **chancellors**.

### ⭐⭐ Political executive vs Permanent executive (a core distinction)
*"The executive branch is not just about presidents, prime ministers and ministers. **It also extends to the administrative machinery (civil servants).**"*

| **Political Executive** | **Permanent Executive** |
|---|---|
| **The heads of government and their ministers** | **Those responsible for day-to-day administration** |
| **Saddled with the overall responsibility of government policy** | The **bureaucracy / civil service** |
| Changes with elections | **Permanent employees of the government** |

> *The chapter's own aside worth thinking about:* *"the executive in a democracy is accountable to people. Is that also true of executives in big companies? Aren't they called CEOs? **Who are they accountable to?**"*

## PART 2 — TYPES OF EXECUTIVE ACROSS THE WORLD

**The puzzle the chapter sets:** *The powers of the **President of the USA** are very different from those of the **President of India**. The powers of the **Queen of England** differ from those of the **King of Bhutan**. Both **India and France** have prime ministers, **but their roles are different.* Why?*

### ⭐⭐ Country-by-country table (very high-yield — learn every row)
| Country | System | Head of State | Head of Government |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USA** | **Presidential** | **President** | **President** (same person) |
| **Brazil & most of Latin America** | **Presidential** | President | President |
| **Canada** | **Parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarchy** | **Queen Elizabeth II** (formal chief of state) | **Prime Minister** |
| **United Kingdom** | **Parliamentary** | Monarch | Prime Minister |
| **Japan** | **Parliamentary** | **The Emperor** | **Prime Minister** |
| **Italy** | **Parliamentary** | **President** (formal head) | **Prime Minister** |
| **Germany** | **Parliamentary** | **President** (**ceremonial** head of state) | ⭐ **The Chancellor** |
| **Portugal** | **Parliamentary** | President | Prime Minister |
| **France** | **Semi-presidential** | **President** | **Prime Minister** — ⭐ *the President **appoints** the PM and the ministers **but cannot dismiss them**, as they are **responsible to the parliament*** |
| **Russia** | **Semi-presidential** | **President** | **Prime Minister, appointed by the President** |
| **Sri Lanka** | **Semi-presidential** | President | Prime Minister |

### ⭐ The three types defined
| Type | Definition | Key feature | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Presidential** | The **president is Head of State AS WELL AS head of government** | *"The office of president is very powerful, both in theory and practice"* | **United States, Brazil, most of Latin America** |
| **Parliamentary** | **The prime minister is the head of government**; most such systems have **a president or a monarch who is the nominal Head of State** | The role of president/monarch is **primarily ceremonial**; the **PM along with the cabinet wields effective power** | **Germany, Italy, Japan, United Kingdom, Portugal** (and **India**) |
| **Semi-presidential** | Has **both a president and a prime minister**, but **unlike the parliamentary system the president may possess significant day-to-day powers** | ⭐ **The president and PM may belong to the same party, or to two different parties and thus be opposed to each other** | **France, Russia, Sri Lanka** |

> ### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — Neha's mistake
> **Neha says:** *"It is really very simple. A country having a president has a presidential executive and one with a prime minister has parliamentary executive."*
>
> **Why she is wrong:** **India, Italy and Germany all have Presidents but are parliamentary.** **France, Russia and Sri Lanka have both a President and a PM but are semi-presidential.** **Canada and Japan have no president at all (a monarch/emperor) yet are parliamentary.** ⭐ **The test is not the *title* of the office but *where effective executive power lies* and *to whom the executive is responsible.***

### ⭐⭐ Box: Semi-Presidential Executive in Sri Lanka (learn this box in full — it is the standard comparison)
- **In 1978 the constitution of Sri Lanka was amended and the system of Executive Presidency was introduced.**
- ⭐ **Under Executive Presidency, people DIRECTLY elect the President.** *(Contrast: India's President is **indirectly** elected.)*
- **It may happen that both the President and the Prime Minister belong to the same political party or to different political parties.**
- **The President has vast powers under the constitution:**
  - **The President chooses the Prime Minister from the party that has a majority in Parliament.**
  - **Though ministers must be members of Parliament, the President has the power to REMOVE the Prime Minister, or ministers.** *(Contrast France, where the President appoints but **cannot dismiss**.)*
  - **Apart from being the elected Head of State and the Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces, the President is ALSO the Head of the Government.**
- ⭐ **Term and removal:** **Elected for a term of six years**, the President **cannot be removed except by a resolution in Parliament passed by at least two-thirds of the total number of MPs.** **If it is passed by not less than one-half of the total number of MPs and the Speaker is satisfied that such allegations merit inquiry, then the Speaker can report the matter to the Supreme Court.**

> **⭐ The two comparison questions the chapter asks (be ready to answer both):**
> **(a) How is the position of the President and Prime Minister in Sri Lanka different from India?** → In Sri Lanka the President is **directly elected**, is **both Head of State and Head of Government**, is **Commander-in-Chief**, **chooses** the PM and **can remove** the PM and ministers. In India the President is **indirectly elected**, is **only the formal Head of State**, exercises powers **on the binding advice of the Council of Ministers**, and **cannot remove** the PM — the PM leaves office only on losing the Lok Sabha's confidence.
> **(b) Compare the role of the Supreme Court in the impeachment of the President in India and Sri Lanka.** → In **Sri Lanka the Speaker can refer allegations to the Supreme Court** for inquiry. In **India impeachment is entirely a parliamentary process** — a **special majority in both Houses**, on the **sole ground of violation of the Constitution** — with **no adjudicating role for the Supreme Court.**

## PART 3 — THE PARLIAMENTARY EXECUTIVE IN INDIA

### ⭐⭐ Why India chose the parliamentary system (a certain exam question)
1. ⭐ **Prior experience.** *"When the Constitution of India was written, India **already had some experience of running the parliamentary system under the Acts of 1919 and 1935.**"* **This experience had shown that in the parliamentary system, the executive can be effectively controlled by the representatives of the people.**
2. **Responsiveness and accountability.** *"The makers of the Indian Constitution wanted to ensure that the government would be **sensitive to public expectations** and would be **responsible and accountable.**"*
3. ⭐⭐ **Fear of the personality cult.** The alternative was the presidential form — *"but the presidential executive puts much emphasis on the president as the chief executive and as source of all executive power. **There is always the danger of personality cult in a presidential executive.**"*
4. **A strong executive with safeguards.** The makers *"wanted a government that would have a **strong executive branch**, but at the same time, **enough safeguards should be there to check against the personality cult.**"*
5. **Mechanisms of answerability.** *"In the parliamentary form there are many mechanisms that ensure that the executive will be **answerable to and controlled by the legislature** or people's representatives."*

⭐ **Therefore the Constitution adopted the parliamentary system of executive for governments BOTH at the national AND at the State levels.**

> *The chapter's own honest worry:* *"Haven't we had very strong Prime Ministers? Does it mean that **even the parliamentary system is not fool-proof against personality cult**? That means people and the legislatures have to be **constantly vigilant**!"*

### The structure at the two levels
| Level | Formal Head | Real executive |
|---|---|---|
| **Union / national** | **The President** — formal Head of the State of India | **The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers** |
| **State** | **The Governor** | **The Chief Minister and Council of Ministers** |

## PART 4 — THE PRESIDENT OF INDIA

### ⭐ Election, term and removal
| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Where executive power formally vests** | *"The Constitution of India vests the executive power of the Union **formally in the President**."* |
| **How it is really exercised** | *"In reality, the President exercises these powers **through the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister.**"* |
| **Term** | **Five years** |
| **Mode of election** | ⭐ **INDIRECT — "there is no direct election by the people for the office of President."** *"The President is elected not by the ordinary citizens but by the **elected MLAs and MPs**."* |
| **Electoral method** | ⭐ **Proportional representation with single transferable vote** *(the PR/STV system from Chapter 3)* |
| **Removal** | **Only by Parliament**, following the procedure for **impeachment**, which requires a **special majority** *(2/3 of those present and voting **plus** a simple majority of total membership — see Ch. 3)* |
| **⭐ Sole ground for impeachment** | ⭐⭐ **"The only ground for impeachment is VIOLATION OF THE CONSTITUTION."** |

### ⭐⭐ Power and position of the President — Article 74(1)
> ### Bare text to memorise — **Article 74(1)**
> *"There shall be a **Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head** to **aid and advise the President** who **shall**, in the exercise of his functions, **act in accordance with such advice.***
>
> *Provided that the President **may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider** such advice....., and **the President shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration.**"*

### ⭐ The significance of the word "shall" — and the two amendments
*"Do you know what the word **shall** means here? **It indicates that the advice is BINDING on the President.**"*
1. ⭐ **First amendment:** *"In view of the controversy about the scope of the President's powers, a **specific mention was made in the Constitution by an amendment that the advice of the Council of Ministers will be binding on the President.**"* *(This is the **42nd Amendment, 1976**.)*
2. ⭐ **Second amendment:** *"By another amendment made later, it was decided that **the President can ask the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice but has to accept the reconsidered advice** of the Council of Ministers."* *(This is the **44th Amendment, 1978** — remember it as the "one-time send-back" rule.)*

### The formal breadth of the office
*"We have already seen that the President is the formal head of the government. In this formal sense, the President has wide-ranging **executive, legislative, judicial and emergency powers.** In a parliamentary system, **these powers are in reality used by the President only on the advice of the Council of Ministers.** The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers have support of the majority in the Lok Sabha and **they are the real executive.**"*

> ### ⭐⭐ Nehru on the President's position — the definitive quotation
> *"**We did not give him any real power but we have made his position one of authority and dignity.** The constitution wants to create **neither a real executive nor a mere figurehead**, but **a head that neither reigns nor governs; it wants to create a great figurehead**..."*
> — **Jawaharlal Nehru, CAD, Vol. VI, p. 734**

**The phrase to remember: *"neither reigns nor governs"* — a "great figurehead" of authority and dignity, not of power.**

## ⭐⭐ PART 5 — DISCRETIONARY POWERS OF THE PRESIDENT

*"Can we infer that the President has no discretionary power under any circumstances? **This will be an incorrect assessment.**"*

### First, the constitutional right to be informed
- ⭐ *"Constitutionally, the President has **a right to be informed of all important matters and deliberations of the Council of Ministers.**"*
- ⭐ *"**The Prime Minister is obliged to furnish all the information that the President may call for.**"*
- *"The President often **writes to the Prime Minister and expresses his views** on matters confronting the country."*

### ⭐⭐ The three situations of genuine discretion

#### Discretion 1 — Sending back the advice for reconsideration
- The President **can send back the advice given by the Council of Ministers and ask the Council to reconsider the decision.** *"In doing this, the President acts on his (or her) own discretion."*
- **When?** *"When the President thinks that the advice has **certain flaws or legal lacunae**, or that it is **not in the best interests of the country.**"*
- **The limit:** *"Although the Council can still send back the same advice and the President would then be bound by that advice, **such a request by the President to reconsider the decision would naturally carry a lot of weight.**"*

#### Discretion 2 — Veto power, and the 'pocket veto'
- ⭐ **The President has veto power by which he can withhold or refuse to give assent to Bills — *other than a Money Bill* — passed by Parliament.**
- **Every bill passed by Parliament goes to the President for his assent before it becomes a law.** The President **can send the bill back to Parliament asking it to reconsider.**
- ⭐ **Why the veto is limited:** *"if the **Parliament passes the same bill again and sends it back to the President, then the President HAS TO give assent** to that bill."*
- ⭐⭐ **The loophole that creates real power:** *"**There is no mention in the Constitution about the TIME LIMIT within which the President must send the bill back for reconsideration.** This means that **the President can just keep the bill pending with him without any time limit.** This gives the President an **informal power to use the veto in a very effective manner.** This is sometimes referred to as the **'POCKET VETO'.**"*

> ### ⭐⭐ Box: The real incident — the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986 (memorise this sequence)
> - **In 1986, Parliament passed a bill known as the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill.**
> - **This bill was widely criticised by many, for it sought to CURTAIL THE FREEDOM OF THE PRESS.**
> - **The then President, Giani Zail Singh, did not take any decision on this bill.**
> - **After his term was over, the next President, R. Venkataraman, finally sent the bill back to Parliament for reconsideration.**
> - **By that time the government that had brought the bill had changed, and a new government was elected in 1989.** This government **belonged to a different coalition and did not bring the bill back before Parliament.**
> - ⭐ **"Thus, Zail Singh's decision to POSTPONE giving assent to the bill effectively meant that the bill could NEVER become a law!"**
>
> **This is the textbook illustration of the pocket veto actually working.**

#### Discretion 3 — Appointing the Prime Minister in a hung House
- **Formally, the President appoints the Prime Minister.**
- **Normally** a leader with majority support in the Lok Sabha is appointed and **no question of discretion arises.**
- ⭐ **But:** *"imagine a situation when after an election, **no leader has a clear majority** in the Lok Sabha... after attempts to forge alliances, **two or three leaders are claiming that they have the support of the majority.** Now, the President has to decide whom to appoint as the Prime Minister."* In such a situation the President must **use his own discretion in judging who really may have the support of the majority, or who can actually form and run the government.**

### ⭐⭐ Why presidential discretion grew after 1989
*"Since 1989 major political changes have considerably increased the importance of the presidential office. **In the four parliamentary elections held from 1989 to 1998, no single party or coalition attained a majority in the Lok Sabha.**"*

These situations demanded presidential intervention either:
- **to constitute governments**, or
- **to grant a request for dissolution of the Lok Sabha by a Prime Minister who could not prove a majority in the House.**

> ⭐ **The rule to remember:** *"**Presidential discretion is related to political conditions. There is greater scope for presidential assertiveness when governments are not stable and coalitions occupy power.**"*

### ⭐⭐ Box: The real incident — President Narayanan and the March 1998 government formation
- **No political party or coalition secured a majority in the elections held in March 1998.**
- **The BJP and its allies secured 251 seats — 21 short of a majority.**
- ⭐ **President K.R. Narayanan adopted an elaborate procedure:**
  1. He asked the leader of the alliance, **Atal Behari Vajpayee, "to furnish documents in support of his claim from concerned political parties."**
  2. **Not stopping at this, the President also advised Vajpayee to secure a vote of confidence within ten days of being sworn in.**
- *(Context given: "After 1977, party politics in India became more competitive and there have been many instances when no party had a clear majority in the Lok Sabha.")*

### ⭐ So why do we need a President at all?
*"For the most part, the President is a formal power holder and a ceremonial head of the nation. You may wonder why then do we need a President?"*

**The answer (a very tidy argument to reproduce):**
- In a parliamentary system the **Council of Ministers is dependent on the support of the majority in the legislature.**
- **This means the Council of Ministers may be removed at any time and a new Council will have to be put in place.**
- ⭐ **"Such a situation requires a Head of the State who has a FIXED TERM, who may be empowered to APPOINT the Prime Minister, and who may SYMBOLICALLY REPRESENT the entire country."** *This is exactly the role of the President in ordinary circumstances.*
- **Besides, when no party has a clear majority, the President has the additional responsibility of making a choice and appointing the Prime Minister to run the government.**

### ⭐⭐ Box: The Vice President of India (learn every line — frequently examined)
| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Term** | **Elected for five years** |
| **Method of election** | **Similar to that of the President** — ⭐ **the only difference being that MEMBERS OF STATE LEGISLATURES ARE NOT PART OF THE ELECTORAL COLLEGE** |
| **Removal** | ⭐ **By a resolution of the RAJYA SABHA passed by a majority and AGREED TO BY THE LOK SABHA** *(note: initiated only in the Rajya Sabha)* |
| **Standing office** | **Acts as the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Rajya Sabha** |
| **Succession** | **Takes over the office of the President when there is a vacancy by reason of death, resignation, removal by impeachment or otherwise** |
| ⭐ **Crucial limit** | **"The Vice President acts as the President ONLY UNTIL A NEW PRESIDENT IS ELECTED."** |
| ⭐ **The precedent** | **B.D. Jatti acted as President on the death of Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, until a new President was elected.** |

### ⭐ Box: "Check your progress" — the President vs the PM on President's Rule
*Suppose the Prime Minister wants to impose 'President's Rule' in a State because the State government has failed to curb atrocities against Dalits. **The President disagrees**, holding that the provision should be used only sparingly. Which courses are open to the President?*

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| a. **Tell the PM he will not sign the order** promulgating President's Rule | ❌ **Not open** — the advice of the Council of Ministers is **binding** (Art. 74(1)) |
| b. **Dismiss the Prime Minister** | ❌ **Not open** — the President has no such power; the PM's tenure depends on the **Lok Sabha's** confidence |
| c. **Ask the PM to send CRPF to that State** | ❌ **Not open** — the President cannot direct policy |
| d. **Make a press statement about how the PM is wrong** | ❌ **Not open / improper** — the President does not publicly campaign against the government |
| e. ✅ **Discuss the matter with the PM and try to dissuade him, but if he insists, agree to sign the order** | ✅ **The correct course** — persuasion plus the one-time power to require reconsideration, followed by compliance |

**This exercise is the practical meaning of Nehru's "neither reigns nor governs."**

## PART 6 — PRIME MINISTER AND COUNCIL OF MINISTERS

### Why the PM is the most important functionary
*"No discussion of government or politics in India would normally take place without mentioning one office: the Prime Minister of India."*

**The chain of reasoning:** the President exercises his powers **only on the advice of the Council of Ministers** → the Council of Ministers is **headed by the Prime Minister** → **therefore, as head of the Council of Ministers, the Prime Minister becomes the most important functionary of the government in our country.**

### ⭐ The majority requirement
- **In the parliamentary form of executive it is essential that the Prime Minister has the support of the majority in the Lok Sabha.**
- **This support by the majority also makes the Prime Minister very powerful.**
- ⭐ **"The moment this support of the majority is lost, the Prime Minister loses the office."**

**The two political eras:**
- **For many years after independence, the Congress party had the majority in the Lok Sabha and its leader would become the Prime Minister.**
- ⭐ **"Since 1989, there have been many occasions when no party had a majority in the Lok Sabha."** Various political parties have come together and formed a coalition with a majority. **"In such situations, a leader who is acceptable to most partners of the coalition becomes the Prime Minister."**
- **Formally, a leader who has the support of the majority is appointed by the President as Prime Minister.**

### ⭐ How the ministry is formed
- **The Prime Minister then decides who will be the ministers in the Council of Ministers.**
- **The Prime Minister allocates ranks and portfolios to the ministers.**
- ⭐ **The three ranks — "depending upon the seniority and political importance": Cabinet Minister, Minister of State, Deputy Minister.**
- **In the same manner, Chief Ministers of the States choose ministers from their own party or coalition.**
- ⭐⭐ **"The Prime Minister and all the ministers have to be members of the Parliament. If someone becomes a minister or Prime Minister without being an MP, such a person has to get elected to the Parliament WITHIN SIX MONTHS."**

> ### ⭐ Box: The dissenting view in the Constituent Assembly — ministers *elected*, not *selected*
> Some members felt ministers should be **elected by the legislature**, not selected by the PM or CM:
> *"**Swiss system** under which the **legislature elects the executive** for a certain period ...is to my mind the best form of government for the provinces... **The system of the single transferable vote is ...the best system that can be adopted for the appointment of the executive** because in that **all interests will be represented and no party in the legislature will have any occasion to feel that it is not represented.**"*
> — **Begum Aizaz Rasul, CAD, Vol. IV, p. 631, 17 July 1947**

### ⭐⭐ Box: Size of the Council of Ministers — the 91st Amendment (2003)
- **Before the 91st Amendment Act (2003)**, the size of the Council of Ministers **was determined according to the exigencies of time and requirements of the situation.**
- ⭐ **Two problems this created:**
  1. **It led to a very large size of the Council of Ministers.**
  2. ⭐ **"When no party had a clear majority, there was a temptation to win over the support of the members of Parliament by giving them ministerial positions, as there was no restriction on the number of members of the Council of Ministers."** **This was happening in many States also.**
- ⭐⭐ **The fix:** *"An amendment was made that **the Council of Ministers shall not exceed 15 per cent of the total number of members of the House of the People** (or **Assembly**, in the case of the States)."*

### ⭐⭐ Collective responsibility — the heart of the parliamentary system
*"The most important feature of the parliamentary executive is that **the executive is routinely under the control and supervision of the legislature.**"*

**The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.** What this means, point by point:
1. ⭐ **"A Ministry which loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha is obliged to resign."**
2. **The principle indicates that "the ministry is an **executive committee of the Parliament** and it collectively governs **on behalf of** the Parliament."**
3. ⭐ **"Collective responsibility is based on the principle of the SOLIDARITY OF THE CABINET."**
4. ⭐⭐ **"It implies that a vote of no confidence EVEN AGAINST A SINGLE MINISTER leads to the resignation of the ENTIRE Council of Ministers."**
5. ⭐ **"If a minister does not agree with a policy or decision of the cabinet, he or she must EITHER ACCEPT THE DECISION OR RESIGN."**
6. **"It is binding on all ministers to pursue or agree to a policy for which there is collective responsibility."**

### ⭐⭐ The pre-eminence of the Prime Minister
*"In India, the Prime Minister enjoys a pre-eminent place in the government."*
- **The Council of Ministers cannot exist without the Prime Minister.**
- ⭐ **"The Council comes into existence ONLY AFTER the Prime Minister has taken the oath of office."**
- ⭐⭐ **The asymmetry to remember:** *"The **death or resignation of the Prime Minister automatically brings about the DISSOLUTION of the Council of Ministers**, but the **demise, dismissal or resignation of a minister only creates a MINISTERIAL VACANCY.**"*
- **The PM acts as a link between the Council of Ministers on the one hand and the President as well as the Parliament on the other.**
- ⭐⭐ **"It is this role of the Prime Minister which led Pt. Nehru to describe him as 'THE LINCHPIN OF GOVERNMENT'."**
- ⭐ **A constitutional obligation:** *"It is also the constitutional obligation of the Prime Minister to **communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation.**"*
- **The PM is involved in all crucial decisions of the government and decides on the policies of the government.**

### ⭐⭐ The seven sources of the Prime Minister's power (a ready-made answer)
*"Thus, the power wielded by the Prime Minister flows from various sources:"*
1. **Control over the Council of Ministers**
2. **Leadership of the Lok Sabha**
3. **Command over the bureaucratic machine**
4. **Access to media**
5. **Projection of personalities during elections**
6. **Projection as national leader during international summitry**
7. **Foreign visits**

### ⭐⭐ How coalition politics since 1989 changed the parliamentary executive (four effects)
*"The power which the Prime Minister wields and actually puts into use **depends upon the prevailing political conditions.** The position of the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers has been **unassailable whenever a single political party has secured a majority** in the Lok Sabha. **However, this has not been the case when governments have been led by coalitions.**"*

**Since 1989 we have witnessed many coalition governments. Many could not remain in power for the full term of the Lok Sabha — they were either removed or resigned due to loss of majority support.** Four consequences:

| # | Effect on the parliamentary executive |
|---|---|
| **1** | ⭐ **"A growing DISCRETIONARY ROLE OF THE PRESIDENT in the selection of Prime Ministers."** |
| **2** | ⭐ **"The coalitional nature of Indian politics in this period has necessitated much more consultation between political partners, leading to EROSION OF PRIME MINISTERIAL AUTHORITY."** |
| **3** | ⭐ **"Restrictions on various prerogatives of the Prime Minister like CHOOSING THE MINISTERS and DECIDING THEIR RANKS AND PORTFOLIOS."** |
| **4** | ⭐ **"Even the POLICIES AND PROGRAMMES of the government cannot be decided by the Prime Minister alone."** Parties of different ideologies come together as **pre-poll and post-poll allies**; *"policies are framed after a lot of negotiations and compromises among the allies."* |

> ⭐⭐ **The conclusion to quote:** *"In this entire process, **the Prime Minister has to act more as a NEGOTIATOR than as leader of the government.**"*

### ⭐ The parliamentary executive at the State level
*"At the State level, a similar parliamentary executive exists, though with some variations."*
- ⭐ **The most important variation: "there is a Governor of the State appointed by the President (on the advice of the central government)."** *(Note: **appointed**, not elected — unlike the President.)*
- **Though the Chief Minister, like the Prime Minister, is the leader of the majority party in the Assembly, ⭐ the GOVERNOR HAS MORE DISCRETIONARY POWERS** *(than the President).*
- **However, the main principles of the parliamentary system operate at the State level too.**

### Box: "Check your progress" — how does a PM actually pick a Council of Ministers?
The options offered: (a) experts in the various subjects; (b) only those from his/her own party; (c) those personally loyal and dependable; (d) supporters of the government; (e) **take into account the political weight of the various aspirants and choose from among them.**

⭐ **In practice (e) dominates** — and the chapter's own account of coalition politics explains why: portfolios are a currency of alliance management. Note how this connects to the **91st Amendment** box: before 2003, ministerial berths were used precisely to buy support.

## PART 7 — THE PERMANENT EXECUTIVE: BUREAUCRACY

### What it is
The executive organ includes the **Prime Minister, the ministers, and a large organisation called the bureaucracy or the administrative machinery.** ⭐ *"To underline the difference between this machinery and the **military service**, it is described as the **civil service.**"*

**Definition of the role:** *"**Trained and skilled officers who work as permanent employees of the government** are assigned the task of **assisting the ministers in formulating policies AND implementing these policies.**"* *(Note both halves — bureaucrats help make policy, not only execute it.)*

### ⭐⭐ Democratic control and political neutrality
- **In a democracy, the elected representatives and the ministers are in charge of government, and the administration is under their control and supervision.**
- **In the parliamentary system, the legislature also exercises control over the administration.**
- ⭐ **"The administrative officers cannot act in violation of the policies adopted by the legislature."**
- **"It is the responsibility of the ministers to retain political control over the administration."**
- ⭐ **"India has established professional administrative machinery. At the same time, this machinery is made politically accountable."**
- ⭐⭐ **Political neutrality defined:** *"The bureaucracy is also expected to be **politically neutral. This means that the bureaucracy will not take any political position on policy matters.**"*
- ⭐ **Why neutrality matters — the test case:** *"In a democracy, it is always possible that a party is defeated in elections and the new government wants to opt for new policies in place of the policies of the previous government. **In such a situation, it is the responsibility of the administrative machinery to faithfully and efficiently participate in drafting the policy and in its implementation.**"*

### ⭐ The composition of Indian bureaucracy
*"The Indian bureaucracy today is an enormously complex system. It consists of:"*
1. **The All-India Services**
2. **State Services**
3. **Employees of the local governments**
4. **Technical and managerial staff running public sector undertakings**

### ⭐⭐ Recruitment — the Public Service Commissions
- **The makers of our Constitution were aware of the importance of a non-partisan and professional bureaucracy.** They wanted members of the civil services **to be impartially selected on the basis of merit.**
- ⭐ **"So, the Union Public Service Commission has been entrusted with the task of conducting the process of recruitment of the civil servants for the Government of India."**
- ⭐ **"Similar public service commissions are provided for the States also."**
- ⭐⭐ **Their independence:** *"Members of the Public Service Commissions are **appointed for a fixed term**. Their **removal or suspension is subject to a thorough enquiry made by a JUDGE OF THE SUPREME COURT.**"*

### ⭐ Representativeness — reservation in the bureaucracy
*"While efficiency and merit are the norms for recruitment, the Constitution also ensures that all sections of the society including the weaker sections have an opportunity to be part of the public bureaucracy."*
- **The Constitution has provided reservation in jobs for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.**
- ⭐ **"Subsequently, reservations have also been provided for women, Other Backward Classes (OBC) and Economically Weaker Sections (EWS)."**
- **Purpose:** *"These provisions ensure that the bureaucracy would be more representative and social inequalities will not come in the way of recruitment to the civil service."*

### ⭐⭐ The IAS/IPS and the federal twist (an extremely important point — links to Chapter 7)
- **Persons selected by the UPSC for the Indian Administrative Service and Indian Police Service constitute the backbone of the higher-level bureaucracy in the States.**
- ⭐ **"The collector of a district is the most important officer of the government at the district level."** The collector is **normally an IAS officer**, and is **governed by the service conditions laid down by the CENTRAL government.**
- ⭐⭐ **The double control — learn this precisely:**
  - **An IAS or IPS officer is assigned to a particular State, where he or she works under the supervision of the STATE government.**
  - **BUT the IAS or IPS officers are APPOINTED BY THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT.**
  - **They can go back into the service of the central government.**
  - ⭐ **"And most importantly, ONLY THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT CAN TAKE DISCIPLINARY ACTION AGAINST THEM."**
- ⭐⭐ **The conclusion:** *"This means that **the key administrative officers of the States are under the supervision and control of the central government.**"*
- **Apart from IAS and IPS officers appointed by the UPSC, the administration of the State is looked after by officers appointed through the State Public Service Commissions.**
- ⭐⭐ **"As we shall study later in the chapter on federalism, this feature of the bureaucracy STRENGTHENS THE CONTROL OF THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT OVER THE ADMINISTRATION OF THE STATES."** *(This is a standard argument for India's federalism being "quasi-federal".)*

### ⭐⭐ The critique of the bureaucracy — and the dilemma
- **"The bureaucracy is an instrument through which welfare policies of the government must reach the people. But most often, it is so powerful that people are afraid of approaching a government officer."**
- **"It is a common experience of the people that bureaucracy is insensitive to the demands and expectations of the ordinary citizen."**
- ⭐⭐ **The two-horned dilemma (the key analytical point):**
  - **"Only if the democratically elected government controls the bureaucracy can some of these problems be effectively handled."**
  - **"On the other hand, too much political interference turns the bureaucracy into an instrument in the hands of the politician."**
- ⭐ **Two gaps identified:**
  1. **"Though the Constitution has created independent machinery for recruitment, many people think that there is NO PROVISION FOR PROTECTING THE CIVIL SERVANTS FROM POLITICAL INTERFERENCE in the performance of their duties."**
  2. **"It is also felt that enough provisions are not there to ensure the ACCOUNTABILITY OF THE BUREAUCRACY TO THE CITIZEN."**
- ⭐ **The hope:** *"There is an expectation that measures like the **RIGHT TO INFORMATION** may make the bureaucracy a little more responsive and accountable."*

> *The chapter's own blunt observation:* *"Yes, I know that the officers are there to serve the people. But **people are always afraid of these officers. And officers also behave as if they were the masters!**"*

## Conclusion
- **"The modern executive is a very powerful institution of government. The executive enjoys greater powers compared to other organs of the government."**
- ⭐ **"This generates a greater need to have democratic control over the executive."**
- **"The makers of our Constitution thought with foresight that the executive must be put firmly under regular supervision and control. Thus, a parliamentary executive was chosen."**
- ⭐ **"Periodic elections, constitutional limits over the exercise of powers and democratic politics have ensured that the executive organ cannot become unresponsive."**

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Executive** | The organ of government that primarily looks after **implementation and administration**; also often involved in **framing policy** |
| **Political executive** | The **head of government and ministers**, with overall responsibility for government policy |
| **Permanent executive** | Those responsible for **day-to-day administration** — the bureaucracy / civil service |
| **Presidential system** | The **President is Head of State AND head of government**; a very powerful office (USA, Brazil, most of Latin America) |
| **Parliamentary system** | The **PM is head of government**; a president or monarch is the **nominal, ceremonial Head of State**; PM and cabinet wield effective power (UK, Germany, Italy, Japan, Portugal, India) |
| **Semi-presidential system** | Both a president and a PM, but the **president may possess significant day-to-day powers**; the two may belong to different parties (France, Russia, Sri Lanka) |
| **Executive Presidency** | The Sri Lankan system introduced by the **1978** constitutional amendment: **directly elected** President who is Head of State, Head of Government and Commander-in-Chief, and **can remove the PM and ministers** |
| **Chancellor** | The head of government in **Germany** |
| **Impeachment** | The parliamentary procedure to remove the President; needs a **special majority**; **the only ground is violation of the Constitution** |
| **Article 74(1)** | There **shall** be a Council of Ministers with the PM at the head to aid and advise the President, who **shall act in accordance with such advice** — with **one power to require reconsideration** |
| **Veto power** | The President's power to withhold or refuse assent to Bills **other than a Money Bill**; limited, because if Parliament passes the bill again the President **must** assent |
| **Pocket veto** | Because the Constitution sets **no time limit** for the President to return a bill, he can **keep it pending indefinitely** — as with the **Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986** |
| **Council of Ministers** | The body of ministers headed by the PM; **cannot exist without the PM**; capped at **15% of the House** by the **91st Amendment (2003)** |
| **Cabinet Minister / Minister of State / Deputy Minister** | The three **ranks** allocated by the PM on the basis of **seniority and political importance** |
| **Collective responsibility** | The Council of Ministers is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**; based on the **solidarity of the cabinet**; a no-confidence vote **even against a single minister** brings down the **whole** Council; a dissenting minister must **accept the decision or resign** |
| **Linchpin of Government** | **Nehru's description of the Prime Minister** as the link between the Council of Ministers, the President and Parliament |
| **Governor** | The formal head of a State, **appointed by the President on the advice of the central government**; has **more discretionary powers than the President** |
| **Civil service** | The bureaucracy, so named to distinguish it from the **military service** |
| **Political neutrality** | The expectation that the bureaucracy **will not take any political position on policy matters**, and will serve any elected government faithfully |
| **UPSC / State PSCs** | Bodies that recruit civil servants on **merit**; members serve a **fixed term** and can be removed or suspended only after **enquiry by a judge of the Supreme Court** |
| **All-India Services** | Services (notably **IAS and IPS**) whose officers serve in the States but are **appointed by, and disciplinable only by, the central government** |
| **Right to Information** | A measure expected to make the bureaucracy **more responsive and accountable** to citizens |

## Quick Recap (15 points)
1. **Executive = implementation and administration**, though it is **often involved in framing policy** too. It splits into the **political executive** (PM + ministers) and the **permanent executive** (bureaucracy).
2. **Three types:** **presidential** (president is Head of State *and* government — USA, Brazil, Latin America); **parliamentary** (PM heads government, president/monarch is ceremonial — UK, Germany, Italy, Japan, Portugal, India); **semi-presidential** (both offices, president has real day-to-day power, the two may be from different parties — France, Russia, Sri Lanka).
3. **Country specifics:** **Germany's head of government is the Chancellor**; **Japan's head of state is the Emperor**; **Canada's is Queen Elizabeth II**; **in France the President appoints the PM and ministers but cannot dismiss them.**
4. **Sri Lanka's Executive Presidency (1978 amendment):** **directly elected** President, **six-year term**, Head of State + Head of Government + Commander-in-Chief, **chooses the PM and can remove the PM and ministers**; removal needs **two-thirds of all MPs**, and with **one-half** the **Speaker can refer the matter to the Supreme Court.**
5. **India chose the parliamentary system** because of **prior experience under the Acts of 1919 and 1935**, a desire for a **responsible and accountable** government, and — decisively — the **fear of a personality cult** in a presidential executive. It applies at **both the Union and the State level.**
6. **The President:** executive power of the Union **formally** vests in him; **five-year term**; **indirectly elected by elected MPs and MLAs** through **proportional representation with single transferable vote**; removable **only by impeachment** with a **special majority**, on the **sole ground of violation of the Constitution.**
7. **Article 74(1):** the President **shall** act in accordance with the advice of the Council of Ministers — **"shall" makes it binding**, confirmed by one amendment (**42nd, 1976**), while a later amendment (**44th, 1978**) allows **one request for reconsideration**, after which the reconsidered advice **must** be accepted. **Nehru: "neither reigns nor governs... a great figurehead."**
8. **Presidential discretion is real, in three forms:** (i) **requiring reconsideration** of advice; (ii) the **veto**, made potent by the absence of any time limit — the **'pocket veto'**, illustrated by **Giani Zail Singh sitting on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986** (anti-press-freedom), which **R. Venkataraman** later returned and which **never became law**; (iii) **choosing a PM in a hung Lok Sabha.** Plus the standing **right to be informed** of all important matters, which the **PM is obliged** to satisfy.
9. ⭐ **"Presidential discretion is related to political conditions."** **No single party or coalition won a majority in the four elections from 1989 to 1998.** **March 1998: BJP + allies got 251 seats, 21 short; President K.R. Narayanan demanded documentary proof of support from Vajpayee and ordered a confidence vote within ten days.**
10. **Why have a President?** Because the Council of Ministers can fall at any moment, the system needs a **Head of State with a fixed term**, able to **appoint the PM** and **symbolically represent the whole country.**
11. **Vice President:** **five years**; elected like the President **except that State legislators are excluded** from the electoral college; removed by a **Rajya Sabha resolution agreed to by the Lok Sabha**; ***ex-officio* Chairman of the Rajya Sabha**; **acts as President only until a new President is elected** — precedent: **B.D. Jatti after Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed's death.**
12. **The PM:** needs **Lok Sabha majority** — *"the moment this support is lost, the PM loses the office."* The PM **chooses ministers and allocates ranks (Cabinet Minister / Minister of State / Deputy Minister) and portfolios**; **all ministers must be MPs, or get elected within six months.** The **91st Amendment (2003) capped the Council at 15% of the House**, because unlimited size had bloated ministries and let hung Parliaments buy MPs with berths.
13. **Collective responsibility:** the Council is **collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha**, is *"an executive committee of the Parliament"*, rests on **cabinet solidarity**, means a **no-confidence vote against even one minister topples the whole Council**, and requires a dissenting minister to **accept or resign.**
14. **PM's pre-eminence:** the Council **comes into existence only after the PM is sworn in**; the **PM's death or resignation dissolves the whole Council**, while a minister's departure creates **only a vacancy**; the PM must **communicate all Council decisions to the President**; **Nehru called the PM "the linchpin of Government."** Power flows from **control of the Council, leadership of the Lok Sabha, command of the bureaucracy, media access, personality projection in elections, international summitry and foreign visits.** **But coalitions since 1989 produced four changes: greater presidential discretion, erosion of prime ministerial authority through constant consultation, curbs on choosing ministers and portfolios, and policies made by negotiation — "the PM has to act more as a negotiator than as leader of the government."** At the State level the pattern repeats, except that the **Governor is appointed by the President on central advice and has more discretionary power than the President.**
15. **Bureaucracy:** called the **civil service** to distinguish it from the military; consists of **All-India Services, State Services, local government employees, and PSU technical/managerial staff**; recruited on **merit** by the **UPSC** and **State PSCs**, whose members hold a **fixed term** and can be removed only after **enquiry by a Supreme Court judge**; **reservation for SC/ST**, later **women, OBCs and EWS**, makes it representative; expected to be **politically neutral.** ⭐ **The federal sting:** IAS/IPS officers serve **under State supervision** but are **appointed by the Centre**, may **return to central service**, and **only the Centre can discipline them** — so *"the key administrative officers of the States are under the supervision and control of the central government"*, which **strengthens central control over State administration.** The standing problems: **insensitivity to citizens**, the twin dangers of **too little and too much political control**, **no protection from political interference**, and **weak accountability to citizens** — with the **Right to Information** the main hope.

## 60-Second Revision
**Political vs permanent executive.** **Presidential (USA/Brazil) | Parliamentary (UK/Germany-Chancellor/Japan-Emperor/Italy/Portugal/India) | Semi-presidential (France—appoints but can't dismiss; Russia; Sri Lanka—1978, directly elected, 6 yrs, also Head of Govt + C-in-C, CAN remove PM, Speaker may refer to SC).** **India chose parliamentary: 1919 & 1935 experience + accountability + fear of personality cult.** **President: 5 yrs, indirect, PR-STV by elected MPs+MLAs, impeachment by special majority, sole ground = violation of the Constitution.** **Art 74(1): "shall" = binding; 42nd made it explicit, 44th gave one reconsideration.** **Nehru: "neither reigns nor governs — a great figurehead."** **Three discretions: reconsideration, veto/pocket veto (Zail Singh + Post Office Bill 1986 → Venkataraman → never law), PM choice in a hung House.** **1989–98: four elections, no majority; March 1998 Narayanan → Vajpayee 251 seats, documents + 10-day confidence vote.** **VP: 5 yrs, no State legislators in the college, removed by RS resolution agreed by LS, ex-officio RS Chairman, acts only till a new President is elected — B.D. Jatti after Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed.** **Ministers must be MPs or elected within 6 months; ranks = Cabinet/MoS/Deputy; 91st Amendment 2003 = 15% cap.** **Collective responsibility = cabinet solidarity; no-confidence in one = all resign; accept or resign.** **PM = "linchpin of Government"; Council exists only after PM's oath; PM's exit dissolves the Council, a minister's only creates a vacancy.** **Coalitions post-1989: more presidential discretion, eroded PM authority, curbs on portfolios, negotiated policy → "negotiator, not leader."** **Governor appointed by President on central advice; more discretion than the President.** **Bureaucracy = civil service; UPSC/State PSCs, fixed term, removal only after SC-judge enquiry; SC/ST + women/OBC/EWS reservation; politically neutral.** **IAS/IPS: State supervision but central appointment and central discipline only → strengthens central control (→ Ch. 7 federalism).** **Problems: insensitivity, too little/too much political control, no protection from interference, weak citizen accountability → RTI.**

2. **Which argument do you agree with?**
   - **Amit:** *"Looking at the constitutional provisions, it seems that the President is only a rubber stamp."* → **Partly right, largely wrong.** Art. 74(1) does bind the President, but he has the **right to be informed**, the **power to require reconsideration**, the **pocket veto** (Zail Singh, 1986), and **real discretion in a hung House** (Narayanan, 1998).
   - **Shama:** *"The President appoints the Prime Minister. So, he must have the powers to remove the Prime Minister as well."* → **Wrong** — this would convert India into a semi-presidential system like Sri Lanka. In a parliamentary system the PM is removed by **losing the Lok Sabha's confidence**, not by the Head of State.
   - **Rajesh:** *"We don't need a President. After the election, the Parliament can meet and elect a leader to be the Prime Minister."* → **Wrong** — the chapter's own answer: because the Council of Ministers **can fall at any time**, the system needs a Head of State with a **fixed term** who can appoint the PM and **symbolically represent the nation.**
