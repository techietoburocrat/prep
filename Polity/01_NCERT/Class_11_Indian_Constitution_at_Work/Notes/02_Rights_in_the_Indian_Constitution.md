# Chapter 2: Rights in the Indian Constitution

## 🎯 Chapter Outcome — what you should walk away with

> **The one thing this chapter exists to teach:** a right is only as real as the machinery that enforces it. The chapter's two opening stories carry the whole argument — the Asian Games workers whose right *was* enforced, and Machal Lalung, whose right was never available in practice.

**After reading it, here is what you now understand:**

1. **That the demand for a bill of rights is older than independence** — the Motilal Nehru Committee asked for one in **1928** — and what the six Fundamental Rights cover (E-F-E-R-C-C).
2. **That equality permits reservation.** Article 16(4) means differential treatment for the backward is *not* a violation of the equality right. This is the constitutional half of an argument you meet again in Political Theory Ch 3 and 4.
3. **How Article 21 grew.** It began as a narrow protection and was expanded by judicial interpretation into dignity, shelter and livelihood — your first concrete example of the Constitution as a *living* document (Ch 9).
4. **The five writs (HM-PQC) and what each actually does** — and why Ambedkar called Article 32 "the heart and soul" of the Constitution: a right without a remedy is not a right.
5. **That the DPSPs are non-justiciable but not decorative.** No court can order their implementation, yet zamindari abolition, bank nationalisation, minimum wages, panchayati raj, the right to education and the mid-day meal scheme all came from them.
6. **The whole journey of the right to property** — Fundamental Right → *Kesavananda* holds it is **not** basic structure → 44th Amendment 1978 → an ordinary legal right under **Article 300A**. This is the single best worked example of the Parliament–judiciary conflict.
7. **That the Fundamental Duties inserted by the 42nd Amendment did *not* make rights conditional** — the Constitution says nothing about how the duties are to be enforced, and the enjoyment of rights was never made dependent on performing them.

2. Why is reservation not a violation of the right to equality?
3. The NHRC investigated the Punjab disappearances and the Gujarat riots. What is the one thing it cannot do?

**Where this leads:** Ch 6 gives you the courts that enforce all this, and Ch 9 gives you the amendments that were fought over it.

## The Big Idea
A constitution is not only about the composition of the organs of government. It is also **a document that sets limits on the power of government and ensures a democratic system in which all persons enjoy certain rights.** **Part III** of the Constitution of India lists the **Fundamental Rights** — and also **mentions the limits on these rights.** Over the past six decades the scope of rights has **changed and, in some respects, expanded** — largely through judicial interpretation.

**What this chapter delivers:** (i) the various Fundamental Rights, (ii) how they are protected, (iii) the judiciary's role in protecting and interpreting them, and (iv) the difference between **Fundamental Rights** and the **Directive Principles of State Policy**.

## PART 1 — THE IMPORTANCE OF RIGHTS

### ⭐ Real incident 1: The Asian Games construction workers (1982)
- In **1982**, during construction work for the **Asian Games**, the government engaged a few **contractors**.
- These contractors employed a large number of very poor construction workers from different parts of the country to build the **flyovers and stadiums**.
- The workers were kept in **poor working conditions** and paid **less than the minimum wages** fixed by the government.
- A team of **social scientists** studied their condition and **petitioned the Supreme Court**.
- **Their legal argument (learn this move):** employing a person to work for **less than the minimum prescribed wage amounts to *begar* or forced labour**, which violates the **Fundamental Right against Exploitation**.
- **The court accepted this plea** and directed the government to ensure that thousands of workers got the prescribed wages.
- **Lesson:** here rights were violated **but were challenged in court** — and so the workers got what was due to them. The constitutional guarantee of the **right against exploitation ensured justice.**

### ⭐ Real incident 2: Machal Lalung — 54 years in custody without a hearing
- **Machal Lalung was 23 when arrested.** A resident of **Chuburi village, Morigaon district, Assam**, he was charged with **causing grievous injuries**.
- He was found **mentally too unstable to stand trial** and was sent as an **undertrial** to **Lok Priya Gopinath Bordoloi Mental Hospital, Tejpur**, for treatment.
- Machal was **treated successfully**, and doctors wrote to jail authorities **twice — in 1967 and in 1996** — that he was **fit to stand trial**. **But no one paid any attention.** He remained in *"judicial custody."*
- **Machal Lalung was released in July 2005. He was 77.** He had spent **54 years in custody, during which his case never came up for hearing.**
- He was freed when a team appointed by the **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)** intervened after an inspection of undertrials in the State.
- **Machal's entire life was wasted because a proper trial never took place.** The Constitution gives every citizen the right to **'life and liberty'** — which means every citizen must also have the **right to a fair and speedy trial.**
- **Lesson:** Machal's case shows what happens **when rights granted by the Constitution are not available in practice.**

> **The chapter's own probing question (worth thinking through):** *What if Machal had been a rich and powerful man? What if those working with the construction contractor had been engineers? Would their rights have been violated?*

**Contrast the two cases:** Both show the importance of **having rights** *and* of the **actual implementation** of those rights. In case 1 the violation was **challenged** and remedied; in case 2 it went **unchallenged for 54 years**.

### Bill of Rights
A democracy must ensure that individuals have certain rights and that **the government will always recognise these rights.** Hence the common practice in most democratic countries of **listing the rights of citizens in the constitution itself.**

**Definition:** *A list of rights mentioned and protected by the constitution is called the **'bill of rights'**.*

A bill of rights does two things:
1. **Prohibits government from acting against the rights of individuals**, and
2. **Ensures a remedy in case those rights are violated.**

### From *whom* does a constitution protect rights? (a two-sided answer — remember both)
1. **From other persons or private organisations** — a person's rights may be threatened by **another person or a private organisation**; here the individual needs the **protection of the government**. So government is **bound to protect** the rights of the individual.
2. **From the government itself** — the organs of government (**the legislature, executive, bureaucracy, or even the judiciary**) may, in the course of their functioning, **violate the rights of the person.**

## PART 2 — FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS IN THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION

### The freedom-struggle inheritance
- During the freedom struggle, leaders realised the importance of rights and **demanded that the British rulers respect the rights of the people.**
- ⭐ **The Motilal Nehru Committee had demanded a bill of rights as far back as 1928.** *(The Nehru Report — a very high-yield date.)*
- So when India became independent and the Constitution was being prepared, **there were no two opinions** on the inclusion and protection of rights. The Constitution listed the rights that would be **specially protected** and called them **'fundamental rights'.**

### Why "fundamental"?
The word *fundamental* suggests these rights are **so important that the Constitution has separately listed them and made special provisions for their protection.** The Constitution **itself** ensures they are not violated by the government.

### ⭐ Fundamental Rights vs Ordinary Legal Rights (a classic distinction)
| Ordinary legal rights | Fundamental Rights |
|---|---|
| Protected and enforced by **ordinary law** | Protected and **guaranteed by the Constitution** |
| May be **changed by the legislature through the ordinary process of law-making** | May **only be changed by amending the Constitution itself** |
| — | **No organ of the government can act in a manner that violates them** |
| — | **Judiciary has the power and responsibility to protect them** from violation by government action |

**Judicial consequence:** **Executive as well as legislative actions can be declared illegal by the judiciary** if they violate the fundamental rights or **restrict them in an unreasonable manner.**

> **⚠️ Trap to remember:** **Fundamental Rights are NOT absolute or unlimited.** *Government can put **reasonable restrictions** on the exercise of our fundamental rights.* (Exercise Q2 turns on exactly this — the correct description of FRs is "the rights given and protected by the Constitution," **not** "rights that can never be restricted.")

### Box: The Bill of Rights in the South African Constitution (comparison material)
- The South African Constitution was **inaugurated in December 1996.**
- Its creation and promulgation took place at a time when South Africa **still faced the threat of civil war** after the dissolution of the **Apartheid** government.
- It declares that its **"Bill of Rights is a cornerstone of democracy in South Africa."**
- It **forbids discrimination** on the grounds of *"race, gender, pregnancy, marital status, ethnic or social origin, colour, age, disability, religion, conscience, belief, culture, language and birth."*
- It grants **perhaps the most extensive range of rights** to citizens, and **a special constitutional court enforces** them.

**Rights included in the South African Constitution:**
- Right to **Dignity**
- Right to **Privacy**
- Right to **fair labour practices**
- Right to a **healthy environment** and right to protection of the environment
- Right to **adequate housing**
- Right to **health care, food, water and social security**
- **Children's rights**
- Right to **basic and higher education**
- Right of **cultural, religious and linguistic communities**
- **Right to information**

> **The key analytical point (this is the chapter's "Check your progress" and a superb Mains angle):** Several items South Africa put in its **enforceable Bill of Rights** — housing, health care, food, water, social security, environment, fair labour practices — appear in India **only as non-justiciable Directive Principles**, or are **implicit** (read into Article 21 by the Supreme Court). India's Right to Education became a Fundamental Right only later; privacy and dignity were **read in** by judicial interpretation rather than written down.

## PART 3 — THE SIX FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (the master map)

| # | Right | Contents as listed in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Right to Equality** | Equality before law — equal protection of laws; prohibition of discrimination on grounds of **religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth** — equal access to shops, hotels, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads etc.; **equality of opportunity in public employment**; **abolition of untouchability**; **abolition of titles** |
| **2** | **Right to Freedom** | Protection of the right to — **freedom of speech and expression; assemble peacefully; form associations/unions; move freely throughout the territory of India; reside and settle in any part of India; practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business.** Also: **protection in respect of conviction for offences**; **right to life and personal liberty**; **right to education**; **protection against arrest and detention in certain cases** |
| **3** | **Right against Exploitation** | **Prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour**; **prohibition of employment of children in hazardous jobs** |
| **4** | **Right to Freedom of Religion** | **Freedom of conscience** and free **profession, practice and propagation** of religion; **freedom to manage religious affairs**; freedom as regards **payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion**; **freedom to attend religious instruction or worship in certain educational institutions** |
| **5** | **Cultural and Educational Rights** | **Protection of language and culture of minorities**; **right of minorities to establish educational institutions** |
| **6** | **Right to Constitutional Remedies** | **Right to move the courts to issue directions / orders / writs for the enforcement of rights** |

**Mnemonic for the six: "E-F-E-R-C-C"** → **E**quality, **F**reedom, **E**xploitation (against), **R**eligion (freedom of), **C**ultural & Educational, **C**onstitutional Remedies.

## 1. RIGHT TO EQUALITY

### The two illustrative situations the chapter opens with
- **Caste discrimination:** *Swadesh Kumar visits his village with a friend. They stop for tea at a roadside hotel. The shopkeeper knows Swadesh, but asks his friend's name in order to learn his caste. He then serves Swadesh tea in **a nice mug** while the friend is given tea in **an earthen cup**, because he is **Dalit**.*
- **Gender discrimination:** *An order is served on four newsreaders of a television channel that they will no longer read news on screen. **All four are women.** The reason given is that they are **above the age of forty-five**. **Two male newsreaders above the same age are not barred.***

**Both are examples of clear discrimination** — one based on **caste**, the other on **gender**.

### What the right to equality provides
- **Equal access to public places** — shops, hotels, places of entertainment, **wells, bathing ghats** and places of worship.
- **No discrimination in that access on the grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.**
- It also **prohibits discrimination in public employment** on any of the above grounds.
- **Why this matters so much:** *"This right is very important because our society did not practise equal access in the past."*
- **Abolition of untouchability** — *"the practice of untouchability is one of the crudest manifestations of inequality"* — abolished under the right to equality.
- **Abolition of titles** — the state shall **confer no title on a person except on those who excel themselves in the military or academic field.**
- **Aim:** to make India a **true democracy** by ensuring **a sense of equality of dignity and status among all its citizens.**

### Equality in the Preamble — and how reservation fits in
The **Preamble mentions two things about equality: equality of status and equality of opportunity.**

**Equality of opportunity** means all sections of society enjoy equal opportunities. But in a society with **various kinds of social inequalities**, what does equal opportunity mean?

**The Constitution's answer:** the government **can implement special schemes and measures for improving the conditions of certain sections of society** — **children, women, and the socially and educationally backward classes.**

> ### ⭐ Bare text to memorise — **Article 16(4)**
> *"Nothing in this article shall prevent the State from making any provision for the reservation of appointments or posts in favour of any backward class of citizens which, **in the opinion of the State**, is **not adequately represented** in the services under the State."*

**The reasoning to reproduce in an answer:** Article 16(4) **explicitly clarifies that a policy like reservation will NOT be seen as a violation of the right to equality.** Read in the spirit of the Constitution, **reservation is *required for the fulfilment of* the right to equality of opportunity** — not an exception to it.

### Box: "You are the judge" — the Puri (Odisha) case
*A postcard from **Hadibandhu**, who identifies himself as a "member of the Dalit community" in **Puri district, Odisha**. Men from this community **refused to follow a custom requiring them to wash the feet of the groom and 'upper caste' guests** during marriage ceremonies. **In revenge, four women from the community were beaten up and another was paraded naked.** The writer says: "Our children are educated and they are not willing to do the customary job of washing the feet of upper caste men, clear the left-overs after the marriage feast and wash the utensils."*

**Rights involved:** Right to Equality (**abolition of untouchability**; non-discrimination on grounds of caste), **Right against Exploitation** (forced/customary unpaid labour = *begar*), **Right to life and personal liberty with dignity** (Art. 21), plus criminal law remedies.

## 2. RIGHT TO FREEDOM

### Freedom and equality — inseparable
*"Equality and freedom or liberty are the two rights that are most essential to a democracy. It is not possible to think of the one without thinking of the other."*

**Liberty** means **freedom of thought, expression and action.** But it does **not** mean freedom to do anything one desires or likes — *if that were permitted, a large number of people would not be able to enjoy their freedom.* Hence freedoms are defined so that **every person enjoys her freedom without threatening the freedom of others and without endangering the law and order situation.**

### Right to life and personal liberty — the foremost right
> ### ⭐ Bare text to memorise — **Article 21**
> *"**Protection of life and personal liberty** — No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty **except according to procedure established by law.**"*

**Procedural protections that flow from it (a checklist worth memorising):**
- No citizen can be **denied life except by procedure laid down under law**; likewise **no one can be denied personal liberty.**
- **No one can be arrested without being told the grounds for the arrest.**
- If arrested, the person has the **right to defend himself by a lawyer of his choice.**
- **It is mandatory for the police to take that person to the nearest magistrate within 24 hours.**
- **The magistrate, who is not part of the police, decides whether the arrest is justified.**

### ⭐ How the Supreme Court expanded Article 21 (very high-yield)
The right is **not just a guarantee against the taking away of life** — it has **wider application**, and **various judgments of the Supreme Court have expanded its scope.** The Court has ruled that Article 21 also includes:
- The **right to live with human dignity, free from exploitation**
- The **right to shelter**
- The **right to livelihood** — *"because no person can live without the means of living, that is, the means of livelihood."*

### Preventive detention — the built-in tension
- **Ordinarily** a person is arrested **after** having reportedly committed an offence. **But there are exceptions.**
- **Definition:** Sometimes a person can be arrested **simply out of an apprehension that he or she is likely to engage in unlawful activity**, and imprisoned for some time **without following the above procedure.** This is **preventive detention.**
- **Trigger:** if the government feels a person **can be a threat to law and order or to the peace and security of the nation**, it can detain or arrest that person.
- ⭐ **Time limit:** preventive detention **can be extended only for three months.** **After three months, such a case is brought before an advisory board for review.**
- **Critique:** on the face of it, it looks like an effective tool against **anti-social elements or subversives** — *"But this provision has often been misused by the government."* Many people think there must be **greater safeguards** so it is not misused for unjustified reasons.
- **The chapter's own conclusion:** *"There is a clear tension between right to life and personal liberty and the provision for preventive detention."*

### The other freedoms — and their restrictions
These rights are **not absolute**; each is **subject to restrictions imposed by the government.**
- **Freedom of speech and expression** is subject to restrictions such as **public order, peace and morality.**
- **Freedom to assemble** must be exercised **peacefully and without arms.** The government may impose restrictions in certain areas, **declaring the assembly of five or more persons unlawful.**
- **The danger acknowledged frankly:** *"Such powers can be easily misused by the administration. The genuine protest against an act or policy of government by the people may be denied permission."* **However**, if people are **aware and vigilant** about their rights and **choose to protest** against such acts, **such misuse becomes rare.**
- Even **in the Constituent Assembly itself, some members expressed dissatisfaction about restrictions on rights.**

> ### ⭐ Somnath Lahiri's critique (a set-piece quotation — Exercise Q9 is built on it)
> *"I feel that many of these fundamental rights have been framed **from the point of view of a police constable**... you will find that very minimum rights have been conceded and are almost invariably followed by a proviso. **Almost every article is followed by a proviso which takes away the right almost completely**... What should be our conception of fundamental rights? ...We want to incorporate every one of those rights which our people want to get."*
> — **Somnath Lahiri, CAD, Vol. III, p. 404, 29 April 1947**

### Rights of the accused (three specific protections)
*"We often tend to believe that anyone who is charged with some offence is guilty. However, **no one is guilty unless the court has found that person guilty**."* A person accused of any crime must get **adequate opportunity to defend** himself or herself. To ensure a **fair trial**, the Constitution provides **three rights**:
1. **No person would be punished for the same offence more than once** *(double jeopardy)*
2. **No law shall declare any action as illegal from a backdate** *(no ex post facto criminal law / retrospective punishment)*
3. **No person shall be asked to give evidence against himself or herself** *(no self-incrimination)*

### Box: "Check your progress" — do these demand restrictions on freedom?
| Situation | How to reason |
|---|---|
| a. People assembled for a **peace march** after communal riots | **No restriction warranted** — peaceful, unarmed assembly; a legitimate exercise of the right |
| b. Dalits denied temple entry; a march is organised to **forcibly** enter the temple | Right to equality is clearly being violated by the denial — but **"forcibly"** raises a law-and-order question; the remedy should be **legal enforcement of temple entry**, not force |
| c. Hundreds of Adivasis **blocked the road** demanding return of land taken for an industry | The grievance is legitimate (livelihood, Art. 21); but **road-blocking affects others' freedom of movement** — the classic "my freedom must not destroy yours" balance |

A private body has no power to punish |

## 3. RIGHT AGAINST EXPLOITATION

In our country there are **millions of underprivileged and deprived people** who may be subjected to **exploitation by their fellow human beings.**

**Two forms prohibited by the Constitution:**
1. ***Begar* or forced labour without payment.**
2. **Buying and selling of human beings and using them as slaves** (traffic in human beings).

**On forced labour:**
- It was imposed by **landlords, moneylenders and other wealthy persons** in the past.
- ⭐ **Some form of bonded labour still continues in the country, especially in brick kiln work.**
- **It has now been declared a crime and is punishable.**

**On child labour:**
- The Constitution **forbids employment of children below the age of 14 years in dangerous jobs like factories and mines.**
- **With child labour being made illegal and the right to education becoming a fundamental right for children, this right against exploitation has become more meaningful.**

## 4. RIGHT TO FREEDOM OF RELIGION

### Why this is a hallmark of democracy
Everyone enjoys the right to follow the religion of his or her choice. **Historically, there were rulers and emperors in different parts of the world who did not allow residents to enjoy religious freedom** — persons following a religion different from the ruler's were **either persecuted or forced to convert** to the official religion. **Therefore democracy has always incorporated the freedom to follow the religion of one's choice as one of its basic principles.**

### Freedom of faith and worship — the full content
- Everyone is **free to choose a religion and practise that religion.**
- It includes **freedom of conscience** — meaning *a person may choose any religion **or may choose not to follow any religion.***
- It includes the freedom to **profess, follow and propagate** any religion.

### The limitations (and why they exist)
- Freedom of religion is **subject to certain limitations.** Government can impose restrictions to protect **public order, morality and health.** **So freedom of religion is NOT an unlimited right.**
- ⭐ **Government can interfere in religious matters for rooting out certain social evils.** *Examples given: in the past the government has taken steps **banning practices like sati, bigamy or human sacrifice.*** **Such restrictions cannot be opposed in the name of interference in the right to freedom of religion.**
- **The tension is acknowledged:** these limitations *"always produce tensions between followers of various religions and the government."* When government seeks to restrict some activity of a religious group, **people of that religion feel this is interference in their religion.**

### ⭐ The conversion controversy (a frequently-asked area)
- The Constitution has guaranteed the **right to propagate one's religion.** This **includes persuading people to convert from one religion to another.**
- **However, some people resent conversions on the ground that these are based on intimidation or inducement.**
- **The settled position:** *"The Constitution does **not** allow **forcible** conversions. It only gives us the right to **spread information** about our religion and thus **attract** others to it."*

### Equality of all religions — the secularism link
Being home to several religions, it is necessary that **government extend equal treatment to different religions.**
- **Negatively**, this means **government will not favour any particular religion.**
- ⭐ **India does not have any official religion.**
- **We do not have to belong to any particular religion to be a Prime Minister, President, judge or any other public official.**
- Under the right to equality there is a guarantee that **government will not discriminate on the basis of religion in giving employment.**
- **Institutions run by the state will not preach any religion or give religious education, nor will they favour persons of any religion.**
- **The objective of all these provisions is to sustain and nurture the principle of secularism.**

## 5. CULTURAL AND EDUCATIONAL RIGHTS

**The starting premise:** *"India is not made up of a monolithic society. We are a society that has vast diversity."* In such a society there will be **social sections small in number** compared to others. **If a group is in minority, must it adopt the culture of the majority?**

**The Constitution's answer: "diversity is our strength."** Therefore one of the fundamental rights is **the right of minorities to maintain their culture.**

### ⭐ Who counts as a minority (a precise definition worth memorising)
- **Minority status is NOT dependent only upon religion** — **linguistic and cultural minorities are also included** in this provision.
- **Definition:** *Minorities are groups that have a **common language or religion** and, **in a particular part of the country or in the country as a whole**, they are **outnumbered by some other social section.***
- Such communities **have a culture, language and a script of their own, and have the right to conserve and develop these.**

> **The chapter's own sharp aside (worth internalising):** *"I am in minority in my locality but majority in the town, minority if you look at my language but majority if you go by my religion... Aren't we all minorities?"* — minority status is **relative to the unit of comparison.**

### Educational institutions
- **All minorities, religious or linguistic, can set up their own educational institutions.** By doing so they can **preserve and develop their own culture.**
- ⭐ **The government will not, while granting aid to educational institutions, discriminate against any educational institution on the basis that it is under the management of a minority community.**

> **⚠️ Exercise Q4 trap — what these rights do NOT mean:** They do **not** mean that only children of that minority may study there; nor that government schools must introduce minority children to their belief and culture; nor that minorities may demand their children study *only* in institutions managed by their own community. **The correct reading: linguistic and religious minorities can open schools for their children** *(and may keep admission for their community)* — the right is one of **establishment and administration**, not a mandate on others.

## 6. RIGHT TO CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDIES

*"Our Constitution contains a very impressive list of Fundamental Rights. **But merely writing down a list of rights is not enough.** There has to be a way through which they could be realised in practice and defended against any attack."*

> ### ⭐ **Dr. Ambedkar called the Right to Constitutional Remedies the "HEART AND SOUL of the Constitution."**

**What it does:** It gives a citizen the right to **approach a High Court or the Supreme Court** to get **any of the fundamental rights restored** in case of their violation. **The Supreme Court and the High Courts can issue orders and give directives to the government for the enforcement of rights.**

### ⭐⭐ The Five Writs (guaranteed exam material — learn each precisely)
| Writ | Literal sense | When the court issues it |
|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | *"you may have the body"* | The court orders that **the arrested person be presented before it.** It can also order to **set free an arrested person if the manner or grounds of arrest are not lawful or satisfactory.** |
| **Mandamus** | *"we command"* | Issued when the court finds that **a particular office holder is not doing his legal duty** and is thereby **infringing on the right of an individual.** |
| **Prohibition** | *"forbid"* | Issued by a **higher court (High Court or Supreme Court)** when **a lower court has considered a case going beyond its jurisdiction.** |
| **Quo Warranto** | *"by what authority"* | If the court finds a person is **holding an office but is not entitled to hold it**, it issues this writ and **restricts that person from acting as an office holder.** |
| **Certiorari** | *"to be certified"* | The court orders a **lower court or another authority to transfer a matter pending before it** to the higher authority or court. |

**Mnemonic: "HM-PQC"** → **H**abeas corpus, **M**andamus, **P**rohibition, **Q**uo warranto, **C**ertiorari.
**Memory hook for the confusable pair:** **Prohibition** stops a lower court **while the case is still going on**; **Certiorari** **transfers/moves the case up** to the higher court.

### Other rights-protection mechanisms (beyond the judiciary)
Many other mechanisms have been created in later years:
- **National Commission on Minorities**
- **National Commission on Women**
- **National Commission on Scheduled Castes**
- **National Human Rights Commission** — established **by law** to protect fundamental and other kinds of rights

### ⭐ Box: National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) — learn this box in full
*"The real test of the rights given by any constitution is in their actual implementation. The poor, illiterate and the deprived sections of society must be able to exercise their rights."*

- **Independent watchdogs against rights violations:** the **People's Union for Civil Liberties (PUCL)** and the **People's Union for Democratic Rights (PUDR)**.
- ⭐ **The government established the NHRC in 1993.**
- **Composition (five members):**
  1. A **former Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of India**
  2. A **former judge of the Supreme Court**
  3. A **former Chief Justice of a High Court**
  4. & 5. **Two other members** who have **knowledge and practical experience in matters relating to human rights**
- **Functions:**
  - **Inquiry at its own initiative** *(suo motu)* **or on a petition presented to it by a victim** into a complaint of violation of human rights
  - **Visits to jails** to study the condition of the inmates
  - **Undertaking and promoting research** in the field of human rights
- **Complaints received in thousands every year**, relating to: **custodial death, custodial rape, disappearances, police excesses, failure in taking action, indignity to women.**
- ⭐ **Its most significant interventions:** the **disappeared youth in Punjab**, and the **investigation and trial of the Gujarat riot cases** — where its intervention **proved effective.** *(Also: it freed **Machal Lalung** — see Part 1.)*
- ⭐⭐ **Its key limitation:** **The Commission does NOT have the power of prosecution.** It **can merely make recommendations to the government, or recommend to the courts to initiate proceedings** based on the inquiry it conducts.

## PART 4 — DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY (DPSP)

### Why they exist and why they are *not* enforceable
The makers of the Constitution knew independent India would face many challenges — **foremost among them the challenge of bringing about equality and the well-being of all citizens.** They thought **certain policy direction was required.** **At the same time, the Constitution did not want future governments to be bound by certain policy decisions.**

So some guidelines were incorporated **but were not made legally enforceable**: if a government does not implement a particular guideline, **we cannot go to court asking it to instruct the government to implement that policy.**

⭐ **These guidelines are 'non-justiciable'** — *i.e.*, **parts of the Constitution that cannot be enforced by the judiciary.**

**Two things the framers relied on instead:**
1. They thought **the moral force behind these guidelines** would ensure governments take them seriously.
2. They expected **the people would hold governments responsible** for implementing these directives.

### What the Directive Principles contain — three categories
| Category | Contents given in the chapter |
|---|---|
| **Goals and objectives** society should adopt | **Welfare of the people**; **social, economic and political justice**; **raising the standard of living**; **equitable distribution of resources**; **promotion of international peace** |
| **Non-justiciable rights** individuals should enjoy (apart from FRs) | **Adequate livelihood**; **equal pay for equal work for men and women**; **right against economic exploitation**; **right to work**; **early childhood care and education to children below the age of six years** |
| **Policies** government should adopt | **Uniform civil code**; **prohibition of consumption of alcoholic liquor**; **promotion of cottage industries**; **prevention of slaughter of useful cattle**; **promotion of village panchayats** |

### ⭐ How governments have actually given effect to DPSPs (a ready-made list of real measures)
- Passed several **zamindari abolition bills**
- **Nationalised banks**
- Enacted numerous **factory laws**
- **Fixed minimum wages**
- Promoted **cottage and small industries**
- Made provisions for **reservation for the uplift of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes**
- The **right to education**
- **Formation of panchayati raj institutions** all over the country
- **Partial right to work** under the **employment guarantee programme**
- The **mid-day meal scheme**

### ⭐ Box: Fundamental Duties of citizens
- **In 1976, the 42nd Amendment to the Constitution was passed.** Among other things, this amendment **inserted a list of Fundamental Duties of Citizens.**
- **In all, ten duties were enumerated.**
- **However, the Constitution does not say anything about enforcing these duties.**
- As citizens we must **abide by the Constitution, defend our country, promote harmony among all citizens, protect the environment.**
- ⭐⭐ **Crucial caveat:** *"Our Constitution does **NOT** make the enjoyment of rights dependent or conditional upon fulfilment of duties. In this sense, the inclusion of fundamental duties has **not changed the status** of our fundamental rights."*

## PART 5 — RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS AND DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES

### They are complementary — the core contrast table
| Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|
| **Restrain the government** from doing certain things | **Exhort the government to do** certain things |
| Mainly **protect the rights of individuals** | Ensure the **well-being of the entire society** |
| **Justiciable** — enforceable in court | **Non-justiciable** — cannot be enforced by the judiciary |
| Guaranteed by the Constitution; changeable only by amendment | Guidelines backed by **moral force** and popular pressure |

**One-line summary to reproduce:** *Fundamental Rights are **negative** (limits on government); Directive Principles are **positive** (duties for government) — and they are **complementary**, not opposed.*

### ⭐⭐ The conflict, and how it played out (the single most important narrative in this chapter)

**The trigger — zamindari abolition.** When the government sought to pass laws to **abolish the zamindari system**, these measures were **opposed on the ground that they violated the right to property.** Keeping in mind **societal needs greater than individual interests**, the government **amended the Constitution to give effect to the Directive Principles.** *"This led to a long legal battle."*

**The two positions:**
| The government (executive) said | The court said |
|---|---|
| **Rights can be abridged for giving effect to Directive Principles** — an argument that **assumed rights were a hindrance to the welfare of the people** | **Fundamental Rights are so important and sacred that they cannot be limited even for purposes of implementing Directive Principles** |
| **Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution** | **Parliament cannot make an amendment that violates Fundamental Rights** |

**The resolution:** *"This controversy was settled by an important decision of the Supreme Court in the **Kesavananda Bharati case**. In this case the court said that **there are certain basic features of the Constitution and these cannot be changed by Parliament.**"* *(Developed further in **Chapter 9 — Constitution as a Living Document**.)*

### ⭐⭐ Box: Right to Property — the full sequence (memorise the four steps)
Behind the whole rights-vs-DPSP controversy lay **one important reason**: the right to property.

1. **Originally**, the Constitution contained a **fundamental right to "acquire, possess and maintain" property.** But the Constitution **made it clear that property could be taken away by the government for public welfare.**
2. **Since 1950**, the government made **many laws that limited this right to property.** This right was **at the centre of the long debate** over rights vs directive principles.
3. ⭐ **In 1973**, the **Supreme Court** gave a decision that the **right to property was NOT part of the basic structure of the Constitution**, and therefore **Parliament had power to abridge this right by an amendment.**
4. ⭐ **In 1978, the 44th Amendment removed the right to property from the list of Fundamental Rights and converted it into a simple legal right under Article 300A.**

**What difference does this change of status make?** Because it is now an **ordinary legal right** rather than a Fundamental Right, **it can be altered by ordinary legislation** and **one cannot move the Supreme Court directly under Article 32** for its enforcement — precisely the FR-vs-ordinary-right distinction from Part 2.

## Conclusion
- ⭐ In the writings of **Jotirao Phuley (1827–1890)**, a **radical social reformer from Maharashtra**, we find **one of the earliest expressions of the view that rights include both freedom and equality.**
- **During the national movement, this idea of rights was further sharpened and expanded into constitutional rights.**
- Our Constitution reflected this **long tradition** and listed the fundamental rights.
- **Since 1950, the judiciary has functioned as an important protector of rights**, and **judicial interpretations have expanded the scope of rights in many respects.**
- The government and administration function **within this overall framework.** *"Rights enforce limitations on the functioning of the government and ensure democratic governance of the country."*

> *"A heavy responsibility would be cast on the majority to see that in fact the minorities feel secure. ...**the only safety for the minorities lies in a secular State.** It pays them to be nationalists ...The majority community should not boast of their national outlook. ...**They should try to place themselves in the position of the minorities and try to appreciate their fears.** All demands for safeguards ...are the products of those fears that the minorities have in their minds, ...as regards their language, their script and also about the services."*
> — **Sardar Hukam Singh, CAD, VIII, p. 322, 26 May 1949**

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bill of Rights** | A list of rights mentioned and protected by the constitution; prohibits government from acting against individual rights and ensures a remedy for violations |
| **Fundamental Rights** | Rights listed in **Part III** and specially protected by the Constitution; changeable only by constitutional amendment; enforceable through the judiciary; **not absolute** — subject to reasonable restrictions |
| ***Begar*** | Forced labour without payment; prohibited under the Right against Exploitation |
| **Bonded labour** | Labour tied to a debt; still continues in India, **especially in brick kiln work**; now a punishable crime |
| **Preventive detention** | Arrest and imprisonment merely on the **apprehension** that a person is likely to engage in unlawful activity; can be extended **only for three months**, after which the case goes to an **advisory board** |
| **Freedom of conscience** | The freedom to choose any religion **or to choose not to follow any religion** |
| **Minority** | A group with a **common language or religion** that is outnumbered by another social section, **in a part of the country or in the country as a whole** |
| **Writ** | A special order issued by the Supreme Court or a High Court for the enforcement of rights (**habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, certiorari**) |
| **Habeas corpus** | Court order that an arrested person be produced before it; can order release if the arrest is unlawful |
| **Mandamus** | Order to an office holder who is failing in a legal duty, thereby infringing an individual's right |
| **Prohibition** | Order by a higher court when a **lower court is hearing a case beyond its jurisdiction** |
| **Quo warranto** | Order restraining a person from acting in an office they are not entitled to hold |
| **Certiorari** | Order to a lower court/authority to **transfer a pending matter** to the higher court |
| **Directive Principles of State Policy** | Non-enforceable policy guidelines listing goals, non-justiciable rights and policies for government |
| **Non-justiciable** | Parts of the Constitution that **cannot be enforced by the judiciary** |
| **Fundamental Duties** | Ten duties of citizens inserted by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**; **not enforceable**, and **do not condition the enjoyment of rights** |
| **Basic structure** | Certain basic features of the Constitution that **Parliament cannot change** — laid down in the **Kesavananda Bharati case** |
| **Article 300A** | The provision under which the **right to property** now exists as an **ordinary legal right** after the **44th Amendment, 1978** |
| **NHRC** | National Human Rights Commission, established **1993**; five members; inquires, visits jails, promotes research; **has no power of prosecution** |

## Quick Recap (14 points)
1. **Part III** lists the Fundamental Rights **and their limits.** The **Motilal Nehru Committee demanded a bill of rights as early as 1928.**
2. A constitution must protect rights **both from other private persons/organisations *and* from the organs of government** (legislature, executive, bureaucracy, even judiciary).
3. **FRs vs ordinary rights:** ordinary rights are protected by ordinary law and changeable by ordinary legislation; **FRs are guaranteed by the Constitution and changeable only by amendment**, and **no organ of government may violate them.** But **FRs are not absolute** — **reasonable restrictions** are permitted.
4. **Six FRs — "E-F-E-R-C-C":** Equality; Freedom; against Exploitation; Freedom of Religion; Cultural & Educational; Constitutional Remedies.
5. **Right to Equality:** equal access to shops, hotels, wells, bathing ghats; no discrimination on grounds *only* of **religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth**; **abolition of untouchability**; **abolition of titles** (except military/academic excellence). Preamble promises **equality of status and equality of opportunity.** **Article 16(4)** makes clear **reservation is not a violation of equality** but a means of fulfilling equality of opportunity.
6. **Article 21:** no deprivation of life or personal liberty **except according to procedure established by law**; grounds of arrest must be told; lawyer of one's choice; **production before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours.** The Supreme Court has expanded it to include **living with human dignity, shelter, and livelihood.**
7. **Preventive detention** — arrest on mere apprehension; **maximum three months**, then **advisory board review**; **often misused**; in **clear tension** with Article 21.
8. **Rights of the accused (three):** no **double punishment** for the same offence; **no retrospective criminal law**; **no self-incrimination.**
9. **Right against Exploitation:** prohibits ***begar*/forced labour** and **traffic in human beings**; **bonded labour persists in brick kilns**; bars children **below 14** from **hazardous jobs like factories and mines.**
10. **Freedom of Religion** includes **freedom of conscience** (including *not* following any religion) and to **profess, practise and propagate** — but is limited by **public order, morality and health**, and the state **may interfere to root out social evils** (**sati, bigamy, human sacrifice**). **Propagation includes persuading conversion, but forcible conversion is not allowed.** **India has no official religion.**
11. **Cultural & Educational Rights:** minorities may be **religious *or* linguistic**; minority status is relative to the **unit of comparison**; minorities may **establish their own educational institutions**, and government **may not discriminate in granting aid** merely because an institution is minority-managed.
12. **Right to Constitutional Remedies — Ambedkar's "heart and soul of the Constitution."** Five writs: **HM-PQC** (Habeas corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo warranto, Certiorari). Backed by the **NHRC (1993, five members, no power of prosecution)**, PUCL and PUDR, and commissions for Minorities, Women and Scheduled Castes.
13. **DPSPs are non-justiciable** guidelines in three parts — **goals, non-justiciable rights, policies** — relied on for their **moral force** and popular pressure. Given effect through **zamindari abolition, bank nationalisation, factory laws, minimum wages, reservations, right to education, panchayati raj, employment guarantee, mid-day meals.** **Fundamental Duties (ten) were inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976**, are **not enforceable**, and **do not make rights conditional on duties.**
14. **FR vs DPSP conflict:** FRs **restrain**, DPSPs **exhort**; FRs protect **individuals**, DPSPs the **whole society.** The clash began over **zamindari abolition and the right to property**; in **1973** the Supreme Court held the **right to property was not part of the basic structure**; the **44th Amendment (1978)** moved it out of Part III into **Article 300A**; and the wider amendment controversy was settled in **Kesavananda Bharati** with the **basic structure doctrine.**

## 60-Second Revision
**Two stories: Asian Games 1982 workers (below-minimum wage = *begar* → SC enforced wages)** and **Machal Lalung (arrested at 23, freed at 77 in July 2005 after 54 years, no hearing, NHRC intervened)** — one right *enforced*, one right *never available in practice*.
**1928 Motilal Nehru Committee → bill of rights demanded.** **Six FRs: E-F-E-R-C-C.** **Art 16(4) = reservation is not anti-equality.** **Art 21 + 24 hours + expanded to dignity, shelter, livelihood.** **Preventive detention = 3 months + advisory board.** **Accused: no double punishment, no backdated law, no self-incrimination.** **Child labour <14 in hazardous jobs; bonded labour in brick kilns.** **Religion: profess-practise-propagate, limited by public order/morality/health; sati, bigamy, human sacrifice banned; no forcible conversion; no official religion.** **Minorities = religious OR linguistic.** **Writs: HM-PQC.** **NHRC 1993, 5 members, NO prosecution power; Punjab disappearances + Gujarat riots.** **DPSP = non-justiciable; Ireland-sourced; three parts (goals / rights / policies).** **42nd Amendment 1976 = 10 Fundamental Duties, unenforceable, rights NOT conditional.** **Property: FR originally → 1973 SC (not basic structure) → 44th Amendment 1978 → Article 300A.** **Kesavananda Bharati = basic structure.** **Ambedkar: Art 32 is the heart and soul.** **Jotirao Phuley (1827–1890) = earliest expression that rights include both freedom and equality.**

### NCERT Exercises (with pointers)
1. **True or False:** (a) A Bill of Rights lays down the rights enjoyed by the people of a country → **True.** (b) A Bill of Rights protects the liberties of an individual → **True.** (c) Every country of the world has a Bill of Rights → **False.** (d) The Constitution guarantees remedy against violation of Rights → **True.**
2. **Best description of Fundamental Rights?** → **(c) The rights given and protected by the Constitution.** *(Not (d) — FRs **can** be restricted reasonably.)*
3. **Which FR is used or violated?** (a) *Overweight male cabin crew get promotion but women colleagues who gain weight are penalised* → **Right to Equality violated** (discrimination on grounds of sex). (b) *A director makes a documentary criticising government policies* → **Freedom of speech and expression, being used.** (c) *People displaced by a big dam rally demanding rehabilitation* → **Freedom of assembly and expression being used**; underlying **Art. 21 right to livelihood/shelter.** (d) *Andhra society runs Telugu-medium schools outside Andhra Pradesh* → **Cultural and Educational Rights being used** (linguistic minority establishing institutions).
4. **Correct interpretation of Cultural and Educational Rights?** → **(c) Linguistic and religious minorities can open schools for their children and keep it reserved for them.**
5. **Which is a violation of Fundamental Rights, and why?** (a) **Not paying minimum wages → violation** (*begar*/forced labour, Right against Exploitation — the Asian Games ruling). (b) *Banning of a book* → **may be a violation** of freedom of speech and expression **unless** justified as a reasonable restriction on grounds of public order/morality. (c) *Banning loudspeakers after 9 pm* → **not a violation** — a reasonable restriction protecting public health/order (and others' rights). (d) *Making a speech* → **not a violation at all** — an exercise of a right.
6. **An activist says the poor don't need Fundamental Rights; what they need is for Directive Principles to be made legally binding.** Do you agree? *(Argue both sides: FRs already secure life, liberty, equality and — via Art. 21 — livelihood and shelter, and only FRs give the remedy under Art. 32. But justiciable socio-economic rights, as in South Africa's Bill of Rights, would directly address hunger, housing and health. Best answer: the two are complementary; note that FRs have in practice been the vehicle for delivering DPSP goals.)*
7. **Caste groups previously associated with scavenging are forced to continue in this job; those in authority refuse other jobs; their children are discouraged from education.** Which FRs are violated? → **Right to Equality** (caste discrimination, equality of opportunity in public employment, abolition of untouchability); **Right against Exploitation** (forced labour); **Right to Freedom** (Art. 21 dignity and livelihood, right to education; freedom to practise any profession/occupation).
8. **A human rights group petitions on starvation and hunger while over five crore tonnes of food grains lie in FCI godowns, and many ration cardholders do not know their entitlement; it asks the court to order improvement of the public distribution system.** (a) *Which rights, and how interlinked?* → **Art. 21 right to life read as right to food, livelihood and dignity**; **right to information** as the enabler; the **DPSPs** on adequate livelihood, nutrition and raising the standard of living; and the **Right to Constitutional Remedies** as the vehicle. (b) *Should these form part of the right to life?* → yes, on the chapter's own logic that **"no person can live without the means of living."**
9. **Read Somnath Lahiri's statement.** Do you agree that the rights were framed "from the point of view of a police constable" and that provisos take the rights away almost completely? *(For: preventive detention up to 3 months; power to declare assemblies of five or more unlawful; wide "public order, morality, health" limits. Against: judicial review has expanded Art. 21 enormously; Art. 32 gives real remedies; restrictions must be **reasonable** and are themselves reviewable.)*
10. **Which Fundamental Right is, in your opinion, the most important?** Summarise its provisions and argue why. *(The strongest case is the **Right to Constitutional Remedies** — Ambedkar's "heart and soul": without it, every other right is unenforceable. Alternatively **Article 21**, given how judicial interpretation has expanded it.)*
