# Chapter 1: Constitution — Why and How?

## 🎯 Chapter Outcome — what you should walk away with

> **The one thing this chapter exists to teach:** a constitution is not a rulebook the government follows — it is the document that **creates** the government in the first place, and then limits it, empowers it, and says who "we the people" are.

**After reading it, here is what you now understand:**

1. **Why a diverse group needs a constitution at all.** Rules that are publicly known give you *coordination*; rules that are legally enforceable give you *assurance*. Without assurance that others will comply, you have no reason to comply either — so knowledge alone is not enough.
2. **The five functions — C-S-L-E-I** (Coordination, Specification of decision-making power, Limitation, Enabling, Identity) — and, just as important, **what is *not* a function**: no constitution can guarantee that good people come to power. It cannot determine the character of whoever wins office.
3. **What makes a constitution *effective* rather than mere words on parchment** — the mode of promulgation, substantive provisions that give *everyone* a reason to go along, and an institutional design that fragments power. Nepal's five King-granted constitutions are the counter-case that proves it.
4. **That older constitutions only *limited* government, while twentieth-century ones also *enable* it** to act against entrenched inequality — and that the Indian Constitution is called the finest example of this, through the Preamble, Fundamental Rights and DPSP together.
5. **How our Constitution was actually made** — 299 members, 82% Congress, 166 days, 8 committees, and the fact that **universal suffrage was the only provision that went undebated** while everything else was fought over.
6. **That borrowing was selective, not imitative** (BIFUC) — and Ambedkar's reason for tying liberty, equality and fraternity together: liberty without equality gives supremacy to the few; equality without liberty kills initiative; both without fraternity are unnatural.

**Self-check — answer these three without looking:**
1. A rule is publicly known to everyone. Why is that still not enough?
2. Name the one item the NCERT exercise lists that is **not** a function of a constitution.
3. Why did Nepal's constitutions keep failing where India's did not?

**Where this leads:** every remaining chapter is one of these five functions in operation — Ch 2 is *limitation*, Ch 3–6 are the *institutions*, Ch 7–8 are the *vertical* division of power, and Ch 9–10 ask how the whole thing survives and what it is ultimately for.

## The Big Idea
Before you can study *elections, Parliament, Presidents or Prime Ministers*, you have to understand that **the entire structure of government and every principle binding its institutions originates in the Constitution.** This chapter answers four things: what a constitution *is*, what it *does* for a society, what makes a constitution **effective** (as opposed to mere words on parchment), and **how** the Indian Constitution was actually made.

**The one-line thesis:** A constitution is not just a rulebook — it is the document that *creates* the government in the first place, limits it, empowers it, and expresses who "we the people" are.

## PART 1 — WHY DO WE NEED A CONSTITUTION?

### The thought experiment the chapter builds on
Imagine you belong to a **reasonably large, deeply diverse group**: different religions (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, some with no religion), different professions, abilities, hobbies, tastes in films and books; some rich, some poor; some old, some young.

This group will inevitably **disagree** on questions like:
- How much property should one person be allowed to own?
- Should sending every child to school be compulsory, or should parents decide?
- How much should the group spend on safety and security — or should it build more parks instead?
- Should the group be allowed to discriminate against some of its members?

Every question produces a *variety* of answers. Yet for all their diversity, **this group has to live together** — they are mutually dependent and need each other's cooperation. So: what enables them to live together peacefully?

**Answer: some basic rules.** And note the two-step logic the chapter insists on:
1. The rules must be **publicly promulgated and known to all members** — otherwise every individual is insecure, because they cannot know what others may do to them or who can claim rights over what. This gives **minimal coordination**.
2. The rules must also be **enforceable**. If citizens have no *assurance* that others will follow the rules, they themselves have no reason to follow them. Making rules **legally enforceable** gives everyone the assurance that others will comply — because non-compliance is punished.

> ### FUNCTION 1 — Coordination and assurance
> **The first function of a constitution is to provide a set of basic rules that allow for minimal coordination amongst members of a society.**

---

### Specification of decision-making powers
A constitution is **a body of fundamental principles according to which a state is constituted or governed.**

But *which* rules are the fundamental ones, and what makes them fundamental? The chapter's insight is that you cannot start with *what* the rules should be. You must first settle a prior question:

**"Who gets to decide?"**

You may want rule X; others want rule Y. You think your rules are best; others think theirs are. Before you can decide *what* rules govern the group, you must decide **who has the authority to decide.**

Different constitutions answer this differently:
| Type of constitution | Who decides? |
|---|---|
| Monarchical constitution | The **monarch** decides |
| Old Soviet Union type | A **single party** was given the power to decide |
| **Democratic constitutions** | Broadly, **the people** decide |

But even "the people decide" is not simple — it raises further questions: For something to become law, must *everyone* agree? Should people vote **directly** on each matter as the **ancient Greeks** did? Or should they express preferences by **electing representatives**? If through representatives — how are they elected, and how many?

**In the Indian Constitution**, it is specified that in most instances **Parliament** gets to decide laws and policies, and that **Parliament itself must be organised in a particular manner.**

**The crucial circular-looking point (very important):** Before identifying what the law in a society *is*, you must identify **who has the authority to enact it.** If Parliament has authority to make laws, then there must be a law that *bestows* that authority on Parliament in the first place. **That is the constitution.** It is *"an authority that constitutes government in the first place."*

> ### FUNCTION 2 — Allocation of decision-making power
> **The second function of a constitution is to specify who has the power to make decisions in a society. It decides how the government will be constituted.**

---

### Limitations on the powers of government
Settling *who* decides is still not enough. Suppose the properly authorised authority then passes laws that are **patently unfair**. The chapter's own list of examples:
- It **prohibits you from practising your religion**.
- It bans **clothes of a certain colour**.
- It forbids you from **singing certain songs**.
- It decrees that people of a **particular caste or religion must always serve others** and may not retain any property.
- It allows government to **arbitrarily arrest** someone.
- It permits **only people of a certain skin colour to draw water from wells**.

Even though such laws were passed by a government that came into existence through correct procedures, there is **something obviously unjust** about a government enacting them.

> ### FUNCTION 3 — Limits on government power
> **The third function of a constitution is to set some limits on what a government can impose on its citizens. These limits are fundamental in the sense that government may never trespass them.**

**How constitutions limit government — the most common method:** specifying certain **fundamental rights** that all of us possess as citizens and which **no government can ever be allowed to violate.**
- The exact content and interpretation of rights **varies from constitution to constitution**, but most constitutions protect a **basic cluster** of rights.
- Citizens are protected from **arbitrary arrest** for no reason — one basic limitation on government power.
- Citizens normally have basic liberties: **freedom of speech, freedom of conscience, freedom of association, freedom to conduct a trade or business**, etc.
- **Crucial qualifier:** in practice these rights **can be limited during times of national emergency**, and the constitution itself **specifies the circumstances** under which they may be withdrawn.

*(The chapter voices the student's objection here through a margin comment: "So you first create a monster and then start worrying about saving yourself from it! Why create this monster called government in the first place?" — the answer being Functions 1 and 2: without government there is no coordination or assurance at all.)*

---

### Aspirations and goals of a society
**Most of the older constitutions** limited themselves largely to (a) allocating decision-making power and (b) setting limits on government power.

But **many twentieth-century constitutions — "of which the Indian Constitution is the finest example" — go further.** They provide an **enabling framework** for government to do certain *positive* things, expressing the aspirations and goals of society. **The Indian Constitution was particularly innovative in this respect.**

**The logic:** societies with **deeply entrenched inequalities** cannot merely *restrain* government. They must also **enable and empower** government to take **positive measures** to overcome inequality and deprivation.

Examples given:
- **India** aspires to be a society **free of caste discrimination** — so the government must be empowered to take all necessary steps toward that goal.
- **South Africa**, with a deep history of **racial discrimination**, needed a new constitution that *enabled* its government to end racial discrimination.
- The framers of the Indian Constitution held that **each individual should have all that is necessary to lead a life of minimal dignity and social self-respect** — minimum material well-being, education, etc.

**Where these enabling provisions live in the Indian Constitution (remember all three):**
1. The **Preamble** supports them.
2. Some are in the section on **Fundamental Rights** — and some of these welfare measures are **legally enforceable**.
3. The **Directive Principles of State Policy** enjoin government to fulfil certain aspirations of the people.

> ### FUNCTION 4 — Enabling aspirations
> **The fourth function of a constitution is to enable the government to fulfil the aspirations of a society and create conditions for a just society.**

#### Box: Enabling provisions in other constitutions
Constitutions are not only rules controlling government power — they also **give powers to government for pursuing the collective good.**
- **Constitution of South Africa** assigns many responsibilities to government: promote **conservation of nature**; protect persons or groups subjected to **unfair discrimination**; **progressively ensure adequate housing for all**, and **health care**.
- **Indonesia's Constitution**: government is enjoined to **establish and conduct a national education system**, and it ensures that **poor and destitute children will be looked after** by the government.

---

### Fundamental identity of a people
*"Finally, and perhaps even most importantly, a constitution expresses the fundamental identity of a people."*

Three layers to this (learn them as three):
1. **Political identity** — the people as a *collective entity* come into being **only through the basic constitution**. One has many identities that exist *prior* to a constitution; but by agreeing to a basic set of norms about *how* and *who* should be governed, one constitutes one's **basic political identity**.
2. **Moral identity** — constitutional norms are the **overarching framework** within which individuals pursue their own aspirations, goals and freedoms. The constitution sets **authoritative constraints** on what one may or may not do; it **defines the fundamental values we may not trespass.**
3. **Shared values across traditions** — many basic political and moral values are now **shared across different constitutional traditions.**

**How constitutions are similar and different:** Constitutions worldwide differ in the form of government they enjoin and in procedural details, but they also share a great deal — most modern constitutions create a form of government that is **democratic in some respects**, and most **claim to protect certain basic rights.**

**Where they differ most: how they embody conceptions of national identity.** Most nations are an amalgamation of complex historical traditions, and they weave diverse groups together in different ways.
- **German identity was constituted by being ethnically German** — the constitution gave expression to that identity.
- **The Indian Constitution, by contrast, does NOT make ethnic identity a criterion for citizenship.** (This is a key contrast to memorise.)
- Different nations also embody different conceptions of the relationship between a nation's **regions and its central government** — and this relationship too constitutes national identity.

### 🔑 The Five Functions of a Constitution — memory table
| # | Function | One-line statement |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Coordination & assurance** | Provides basic rules, publicly known and legally enforceable, so members can live together |
| 2 | **Specification of decision-making power** | Says *who* decides and how government is constituted |
| 3 | **Limitation on government** | Sets limits government may never trespass (chiefly Fundamental Rights) |
| 4 | **Enabling aspirations** | Empowers government to take positive measures for a just society (Preamble, FRs, DPSP) |
| 5 | **Fundamental identity of a people** | Gives a political identity, a moral identity, shared values |

**Mnemonic: "C-S-L-E-I"** → **C**oordination, **S**pecification, **L**imitation, **E**nabling, **I**dentity.

## PART 2 — THE AUTHORITY OF A CONSTITUTION

The functions above explain *why* societies have constitutions. But three further questions arise:
- **(a) What is a constitution?**
- **(b) How effective is a constitution?**
- **(c) Is a constitution just?**

### (a) What is a constitution — one document or many?
In most countries, the "Constitution" is a **compact document comprising a number of articles** about the state, specifying how the state is to be constituted and what norms it should follow.

**But not everywhere.** The **United Kingdom**, for instance, has **no single document** that can be called the Constitution. It has a **series of documents and decisions which, taken collectively, are referred to as the constitution.**

So the working definition: **a constitution is the document *or set of documents* that seeks to perform the functions described above.**

### (b) How effective is a constitution? — Three factors
Many constitutions around the world **exist only on paper — mere words on a parchment.** What makes a constitution actually impact people's lives? Three factors:

#### Factor 1: Mode of promulgation — *how it came into being*
This asks: **who crafted the constitution, and how much authority did they have?**
- In many countries constitutions **remain defunct** because they were crafted by **military leaders**, or by leaders who are **not popular** and cannot carry the people with them.
- **The most successful constitutions — India, South Africa, the United States — were created in the aftermath of popular national movements.**
- India's Constitution was formally created by a **Constituent Assembly between December 1946 and November 1949**, but it **drew upon a long history of the nationalist movement**, which had a remarkable ability to carry different sections of Indian society together.
- The Constitution's legitimacy came from being drawn up by people who (i) **enjoyed immense public credibility**, (ii) **had the capacity to negotiate and command respect** across a wide cross-section of society, and (iii) **convinced the people that the constitution was not an instrument for the aggrandisement of their personal power.** The final document reflected the **broad national consensus** of the time.
- **On referendums:** Some countries subject their constitution to a **full-fledged referendum**, where all the people vote on its desirability. **The Indian Constitution was never subjected to a referendum** — yet it carried enormous public authority, because it had the consensus and backing of leaders who were themselves popular. **The people adopted it as their own by abiding by its provisions.**

##### Box: Constitution-making in Nepal — the counter-example (learn the sequence)
Making a constitution is not always easy or smooth.
- **Since 1948, Nepal has had five constitutions: 1948, 1951, 1959, 1962 and 1990.** All of these were ***granted* by the King** — the crucial defect.
- The **1990 constitution introduced multi-party competition**, but the **King continued to hold final powers** in many respects.
- Nepal faced years of **militant agitations for restructuring** the government. The **central issue was the role of the monarchy.** Some groups wanted to **abolish monarchy and establish a republic**; others wanted a **limited monarchy** with a reduced role for the King. The King was not ready to give up powers, and **took over all powers in October 2002.**
- Many parties demanded a **new constituent assembly**; the **Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist)** was at the forefront of the struggle for a **popularly elected constituent assembly.**
- Under pressure of popular agitation the King installed a government acceptable to the agitating parties, which **stripped him of almost all powers.**
- **In 2008, Nepal emerged as a democratic republic after abolishing the monarchy.**
- **Nepal finally adopted a new constitution in 2015.**

> **Why this box matters:** it is the direct contrast to India — a *granted* constitution lacks the authority that a constitution born of a popular movement possesses.

#### Factor 2: The substantive provisions of a constitution
*"It is the hallmark of a successful constitution that it gives everyone in society some reason to go along with its provisions."*

Failures of substance:
- A constitution that **allowed permanent majorities to oppress minority groups** would give minorities **no reason** to go along with it.
- A constitution that **systematically privileged some members at the expense of others**, or **entrenched the power of small groups**, would **cease to command allegiance.**
- **If any group feels its identity is being stifled, it will have no reason to abide by the constitution.**

**The realistic standard:** *"No constitution by itself achieves perfect justice. But it has to convince people that it provides the framework for pursuing basic justice."*

**The rule of thumb:** *"The more a constitution preserves the freedom and equality of all its members, the more likely it is to succeed."*

#### Factor 3: Balanced institutional design
**Key insight — constitutions are usually subverted NOT by the people, but by small groups who wish to enhance their own power.**

Well-crafted constitutions therefore **fragment power in society intelligently, so that no single group can subvert the constitution.**

**Two dimensions of intelligent design:**

**(i) No institution gets a monopoly of power — horizontal fragmentation.**
The Indian Constitution **horizontally fragments power** across:
- the **Legislature**
- the **Executive**
- the **Judiciary**
- and even **independent statutory bodies like the Election Commission**

This ensures that **even if one institution wants to subvert the Constitution, others can check its transgressions.** *"An intelligent system of checks and balances has facilitated the success of the Indian Constitution."*

**(ii) The right balance between rigidity and flexibility.**
A constitution must treat certain values, norms and procedures as **authoritative**, while allowing **enough flexibility to adapt to changing needs and circumstances.**
- **Too rigid** a constitution is **likely to break under the weight of change.**
- **Too flexible** a constitution **gives no security, predictability or identity** to a people.
- Successful constitutions **strike the right balance between preserving core values and adapting them to new circumstances.**
- This is why the Indian Constitution is described as **"a living document"** (the subject of **Chapter 9**). By balancing the *possibility* of change against *limits* on change, it has ensured it will survive as a document respected by people — and that **no section or group can, on its own, subvert the Constitution.**

### 🔑 Three questions to test whether a constitution has authority
1. **Were the people who enacted the constitution credible?** *(answered in the rest of this chapter)*
2. **Was power intelligently organised so that no group can easily subvert it — and does the constitution give everyone some reason to go along with it?** *(answered by most of this book)*
3. **Is the constitution the locus of people's hopes and aspirations / is it just?** *(answered by the last chapter — Chapter 10, The Philosophy of the Constitution)*

*(The chapter's cartoons reinforce this: the failed attempt at a **European Constitution** (2004); the conflict between ethnic groups over the **new Iraqi constitution** after the collapse of Saddam Hussain's regime, depicted as a **"castle of cards"**; and Shankar's 4 December 1949 cartoon of **Nehru balancing different visions and ideologies** among the constitution-makers.)*

## PART 3 — HOW WAS THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION MADE?

### The Constituent Assembly — hard facts and dates (high-yield)
| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Made by | The **Constituent Assembly**, elected for **undivided India** |
| First sitting | **9 December 1946** |
| Reassembled as CA for **divided** India | **14 August 1947** |
| How members were chosen | **Indirect election** by members of the **Provincial Legislative Assemblies** established under the **Government of India Act, 1935** |
| Composed along lines suggested by | The **Cabinet Mission** plan (a committee of the British cabinet) |
| Membership reduced after Partition | To **299** (members elected from territories falling under Pakistan ceased to be members, under the plan of **3 June 1947**) |
| Constitution **adopted** | **26 November 1949** |
| Signatures appended | **284 members** present on **24 January 1950** signed the Constitution as finally passed |
| Constitution **came into force** | **26 January 1950** |
| Duration of sittings | Met for **166 days**, spread over **2 years and 11 months** |
| Sessions | **Open to the press and the public alike** |

### The Cabinet Mission formula for composition (four rules)
1. **Each Province and each Princely State or group of States were allotted seats in proportion to population — roughly in the ratio of 1 : 10,00,000** (one seat per million people).
2. As a result, the **Provinces** (under direct British rule) were to elect **292 members**, while the **Princely States** were allotted a minimum of **93 seats**.
3. Seats in each Province were distributed among the **three main communities — Muslims, Sikhs and general — in proportion to their respective populations.**
4. Members of each community in the Provincial Legislative Assembly elected their own representatives by the method of **proportional representation with single transferable vote.** For **Princely States**, the method of selection was **to be determined by consultation.**

### The Partition backdrop
The Constitution was framed **against the backdrop of the horrendous violence that Partition unleashed on the subcontinent.** It is a tribute to the framers' fortitude that they not only drafted a constitution under immense pressure, but **learnt the right lessons from that violence**: the Constitution was committed to a **new conception of citizenship**, where not only would **minorities be secure**, but **religious identity would have no bearing on citizenship rights.**

### Was the Assembly representative? (a standard exam question)
It was **not elected by universal suffrage** — but there was a **serious attempt to make it a representative body**:
- Members of **all religions** were given representation under the scheme above.
- The Assembly had **twenty-eight (28) members from the Scheduled Castes.**
- In party terms, the **Congress dominated**, occupying **as much as 82 per cent of the seats** after Partition.
- **But** the Congress was **itself such a diverse party that it managed to accommodate almost all shades of opinion within it.**

### The Principle of Deliberation — the real source of authority
The Assembly's authority came **not only** from being broadly (though imperfectly) representative. It came from **the procedures it adopted and the values its members brought to their deliberations.**
- Each member deliberated **with the interests of the whole nation in mind**, not merely as a representative of their own identity or community.
- There were often disagreements, but **few could be traced to members protecting their own interests** — they were **legitimate differences of principle.**

**The great questions actually debated:**
- Should India adopt a **centralised or decentralised** system of government?
- What should be the **relations between the States and the Centre**?
- What should be the **powers of the judiciary**?
- Should the Constitution **protect property rights**?

*"Almost every issue that lies at the foundation of a modern state was discussed with great sophistication."*

> **⭐ The single most quotable fact in this chapter:** **Only ONE provision of the Constitution was passed without virtually any debate — the introduction of UNIVERSAL SUFFRAGE** (all citizens reaching a certain age entitled to vote **irrespective of religion, caste, education, gender or income**). The members **felt no need at all to discuss who should have the right to vote**, while every other matter was seriously debated. *"Nothing can be a better testament to the democratic commitment of this Assembly."*

**Public reason.** The Constitution drew its authority from the fact that members engaged in what may be called **public reason** — they placed great emphasis on **discussion and reasoned argument**, and **did not simply advance their own interests but gave principled reasons to others** for their positions. The very act of giving reasons to others **moves you away from a narrow consideration of your own interest**, because you must persuade others to go along with your view.

The voluminous Constituent Assembly Debates, where **each clause was subjected to scrutiny and debate**, are a tribute to public reason at its best — *"equal in importance to the French and American revolutions"* as a chapter in the history of constitution-making.

#### Box: Procedures of the Assembly
- The Constituent Assembly had **eight major Committees** on different subjects.
- These were usually chaired by **Jawaharlal Nehru, Rajendra Prasad, Sardar Patel or B.R. Ambedkar.**
- **These were not men who agreed with each other.** **Ambedkar had been a bitter critic of the Congress and of Gandhi**, accusing them of not doing enough for the upliftment of the **Scheduled Castes**. **Patel and Nehru disagreed on many issues.** *Nevertheless, they all worked together.*
- Each Committee drafted particular provisions, which were then **subjected to debate by the entire Assembly.**
- An attempt was usually made to **reach a consensus**, on the belief that provisions agreed to by all would not be detrimental to any particular interest. **Some provisions were subject to the vote.**
- **In each instance, every single argument, query or concern was responded to with great care and in writing.**

| Role | Person |
|---|---|
| **President of the Constituent Assembly** | **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** |
| **Chairman of the Drafting Committee** | **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** |

### Inheritance of the nationalist movement
*"No constitution is simply a product of the Assembly that produces it."* An Assembly as diverse as India's **could not have functioned without a background consensus** on the main principles the Constitution should enshrine — and **those principles were forged during the long struggle for freedom.**

In a way, the Constituent Assembly was **giving concrete shape and form to principles it had inherited from the nationalist movement.** For decades before the Constitution, the nationalist movement had already debated: **the shape and form of government India should have, the values it should uphold, and the inequalities it should overcome.** The answers forged in those debates were given final form in the Constitution.

**The best summary of those principles: the OBJECTIVES RESOLUTION, moved by Nehru in 1946** — the resolution that defined the aims of the Assembly and encapsulated the aspirations and values behind the Constitution.

Based on the Objectives Resolution, the Constitution gave institutional expression to five fundamental commitments:
**➊ Equality ➋ Liberty ➌ Democracy ➍ Sovereignty ➎ A cosmopolitan identity**

*"Our Constitution is not merely a maze of rules and procedures, but a moral commitment to establish a government that will fulfil the many promises that the nationalist movement held before the people."*

#### Box: Main points of the Objectives Resolution (learn all eight)
1. India is an **independent, sovereign, republic**.
2. India shall be a **Union** of erstwhile **British Indian territories, Indian States**, and other parts outside British India and Indian States **as are willing to be part of the Union**.
3. Territories forming the Union shall be **autonomous units** and exercise all powers and functions of Government and administration, **except those assigned to or vested in the Union**.
4. **All powers and authority of sovereign and independent India and its constitution shall flow from the people.**
5. All people of India shall be guaranteed and secured **social, economic and political justice; equality of status and opportunities and equality before law**; and **fundamental freedoms** — of speech, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action — **subject to law and public morality**.
6. **Adequate safeguards** shall be provided for **minorities, backward and tribal areas, depressed and other backward classes**.
7. The **territorial integrity** of the Republic and its sovereign rights on **land, sea and air** shall be maintained according to justice and the law of civilized nations.
8. The land would make **full and willing contribution to the promotion of world peace and the welfare of mankind.**

> **UPSC hook:** The Objectives Resolution (moved 13 December 1946, adopted 22 January 1947) is the **basis of the Preamble.** Compare each point above with a phrase in the Preamble.

### Institutional arrangements — the third factor applied to India
The Constituent Assembly spent a great deal of time **evolving the right balance among the executive, legislature and judiciary.** The basic principle: **government must be democratic and committed to the welfare of the people.**

This led to the adoption of **two structural choices**:
1. The **parliamentary form** — distributing governmental power between the **legislature and the executive**.
2. The **federal arrangement** — distributing power between the **States and the central government**.

### Borrowing from other constitutions — and why it isn't imitation
While evolving these arrangements, the makers **did not hesitate to learn from the experiments and experiences of other countries.** They were **not averse to borrowing** — a testament to their wide learning, that they could lay hands on **any intellectual argument or historical example** necessary for the task.

**But borrowing was NOT slavish imitation.** *"Far from it. Each provision of the Constitution had to be defended on grounds that it was suited to Indian problems and aspirations."* India was lucky to have an Assembly that, instead of being **parochial**, could take the best available anywhere in the world and **make it their own.**

**Ambedkar's answer to the "borrowed constitution" charge (memorise this):**
> *"One likes to ask whether there can be anything new in a Constitution framed at this hour in the history of the world... The only new things, if there can be any, in a Constitution framed so late in the day are the variations made to remove the faults and to accommodate it to the needs of the country."*
> — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, CAD, Vol. VII, p.37, **4 November 1948**

#### ⭐ Provisions adapted from other constitutions (very high-frequency Prelims table)
| Source constitution | Provisions borrowed |
|---|---|
| **British Constitution** | **First Past the Post** electoral system; **Parliamentary form of government**; the idea of the **rule of law**; institution of the **Speaker** and her/his role; **law-making procedure** |
| **Irish Constitution** | **Directive Principles of State Policy** |
| **French Constitution** | Principles of **Liberty, Equality and Fraternity** |
| **United States Constitution** | **Charter of Fundamental Rights**; power of **Judicial Review** and **independence of the judiciary** |

**Mnemonic — "BIFUC":** **B**ritain (parliament/FPTP/rule of law/Speaker), **I**reland (DPSP), **F**rance (Liberty–Equality–Fraternity), **U**SA (Fundamental Rights + Judicial Review), **C**anada (quasi-federal + residuary powers).

### Ambedkar on social democracy — the chapter's most important quotation
> *"We must make our political democracy a social democracy as well. Political democracy cannot last unless there lies at the base of it social democracy. What does social democracy mean? It means a way of life, which recognises **liberty, equality and fraternity** as the principles of life. These principles of liberty, equality and fraternity are not to be treated as separate items in a trinity. **They form a union of trinity** in the sense that to divorce one from the other is to defeat the very purpose of democracy. Liberty cannot be divorced from equality, equality cannot be divorced from liberty. Nor can liberty and equality be divorced from fraternity. **Without equality, liberty would produce the supremacy of the few over the many. Equality without liberty would kill individual initiative. Without fraternity, liberty and equality could not become a natural course of things...*"
> — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, CAD, Vol. XI, p.979, **25 November 1949**

**The logic in three arrows (learn this — it is a ready-made Mains answer):**
- **Liberty without equality** → supremacy of the few over the many
- **Equality without liberty** → kills individual initiative
- **Neither, without fraternity** → cannot become a natural course of things

Rajendra Prasad's tribute to Ambedkar (CAD Vol. XI, p.994, **26 November 1949**): *"We could never make a decision which was or could be ever so right as when we put him on the Drafting Committee and made him its Chairman."*

---

## Conclusion
It is a tribute to the **wisdom and foresight** of the makers that they presented a document **enshrining fundamental values and the highest aspirations shared by the people.** This is one reason this intricately crafted document has **not only survived but become a living reality**, when so many other constitutions **perished with the paper they were written on.**

India's Constitution is a unique document which **in turn became an exemplar for many other constitutions — most notably South Africa.** The purpose of the almost-three-year search was **to strike the right balance**, so that the institutions created would not be **haphazard or tentative arrangements** but could **accommodate the aspirations of the people of India for a long time to come.**

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Constitution** | A body of fundamental principles according to which a state is constituted or governed; the document (or set of documents) performing the five functions |
| **Coordination and assurance** | The first function — publicly known, legally enforceable rules that let a diverse group live together, each assured others will comply |
| **Promulgation** | The act of officially bringing a law or constitution into being / publicly proclaiming it |
| **Mode of promulgation** | *How* a constitution came into being — who crafted it and with how much authority; decisive for its effectiveness |
| **Referendum** | A direct vote by all the people on a specific question, e.g. the desirability of a constitution (**India never held one**) |
| **Universal suffrage** | The right of all citizens above a certain age to vote, irrespective of religion, caste, education, gender or income |
| **Public reason** | Giving *principled* reasons to others (rather than asserting self-interest) to persuade them — the deliberative method of the Constituent Assembly |
| **Constituent Assembly** | The body that framed the Constitution; first sat 9 Dec 1946; 299 members after Partition |
| **Objectives Resolution** | The resolution moved by **Nehru in 1946** defining the aims of the Assembly; the source of the Preamble's values |
| **Cabinet Mission** | The committee of the British cabinet whose plan determined the Constituent Assembly's composition |
| **Directive Principles of State Policy** | Provisions enjoining government to fulfil certain aspirations of the people (borrowed from **Ireland**) |
| **Judicial Review** | The judiciary's power to examine the constitutionality of laws (borrowed from the **USA**) |
| **Residual / Residuary Powers** | Power to legislate on subjects not listed anywhere; belongs to the Union (idea borrowed from **Canada**) |
| **Quasi-federal** | A federal system with a strong central government (borrowed from **Canada**) |
| **Living document** | A constitution that balances the possibility of change against limits on change, and so adapts without breaking (see Ch. 9) |
| **Social democracy** | Ambedkar's idea that political democracy cannot last unless liberty, equality and fraternity are practised as a *way of life* |

## Quick Recap (12 points)
1. **The entire structure of government originates in the Constitution** — it is *"an authority that constitutes government in the first place."* Before asking what the law is, ask who had authority to enact it.
2. **Five functions: C-S-L-E-I** — **C**oordination & assurance; **S**pecification of decision-making power; **L**imitation on government; **E**nabling aspirations; **I**dentity of a people.
3. Rules need to be **both publicly known AND legally enforceable** — knowledge gives coordination, enforceability gives assurance.
4. Governments are limited chiefly through **Fundamental Rights**, but the constitution itself **specifies the emergency circumstances** in which rights may be withdrawn.
5. **Older constitutions only limited government; twentieth-century ones — "of which the Indian Constitution is the finest example" — also *enable* it** to take positive measures against entrenched inequality. In India via the **Preamble, Fundamental Rights, and DPSP.**
6. A constitution gives a people a **political identity, a moral identity, and shared values.** **German identity was ethnic; the Indian Constitution does NOT make ethnic identity a criterion for citizenship.**
7. **The UK has no single constitutional document** — a series of documents and decisions collectively form its constitution.
8. **Three factors make a constitution effective:** (i) **mode of promulgation** — India, South Africa and the USA succeeded because their constitutions followed **popular national movements**; (ii) **substantive provisions** — it must give *everyone* a reason to go along; (iii) **balanced institutional design.**
9. **Nepal is the counter-case:** five constitutions (**1948, 1951, 1959, 1962, 1990**), all *granted by the King*; King seized all power **Oct 2002**; became a **democratic republic in 2008**; new constitution **2015**.
10. **Constitutions are subverted by small groups, not by the people** — hence India **horizontally fragments power** across Legislature, Executive, Judiciary and statutory bodies like the **Election Commission**, and balances **rigidity against flexibility** (too rigid breaks; too flexible gives no security).
11. **Assembly facts:** first sitting **9 Dec 1946**; reassembled for divided India **14 Aug 1947**; members **indirectly elected** by Provincial Legislative Assemblies under the **GoI Act 1935**; composition per the **Cabinet Mission** plan (**1 seat : 10,00,000**; **292** provincial + minimum **93** princely-state seats; proportional representation with **single transferable vote**); reduced to **299** after Partition; **28** SC members; **Congress held 82%**; **adopted 26 Nov 1949**; **284 signed on 24 Jan 1950**; **in force 26 Jan 1950**; met **166 days** over **2 years 11 months**; sessions open to press and public; **8 major committees**; **Rajendra Prasad** = President, **Ambedkar** = Chairman of Drafting Committee.
12. **Universal suffrage was the ONLY provision passed without virtually any debate.** Everything else was argued through **public reason** — and the values themselves were inherited from the nationalist movement, summarised in Nehru's **Objectives Resolution (1946)**: equality, liberty, democracy, sovereignty, cosmopolitan identity.

## 60-Second Revision
**Five functions (C-S-L-E-I).** **Three effectiveness factors (promulgation, substance, institutional design).** **Three authority questions (credible framers? power fragmented? locus of hopes?).** **Borrowings: BIFUC** — Britain (parliamentary, FPTP, rule of law, Speaker, law-making), Ireland (DPSP), France (Liberty-Equality-Fraternity), USA (FRs, judicial review), Canada (quasi-federal, residuary). **Dates: 9 Dec 1946 → 14 Aug 1947 → 26 Nov 1949 (adopted) → 24 Jan 1950 (signed, 284) → 26 Jan 1950 (in force).** **Numbers: 299 members, 28 SC, 82% Congress, 292+93 seats, 1:10 lakh, 166 days, 8 committees.** **Only universal suffrage went undebated.** **Ambedkar: liberty w/o equality = supremacy of few; equality w/o liberty = kills initiative; both w/o fraternity = unnatural.**

## Try These Questions

### NCERT Exercises (with pointers)
1. **Which of these is NOT a function of the constitution?** (a) guarantees citizens' rights (b) marks out spheres of power for branches of government (c) **ensures that good people come to power** ✅ *(this is the answer — a constitution cannot guarantee the character of who wins)* (d) gives expression to shared values.
2. **Best reason that the constitution's authority is higher than Parliament's?** → **(c) The constitution specifies how Parliament is to be formed and what its powers are.** *(Not (a) chronology, not (b) eminence, and (d) is false — Parliament can amend it.)*
3. **True or False:** (a) Constitutions are written documents about formation and power of government → *partly false: not always a single written document (UK)*; (b) Constitutions exist and are required only in democratic countries → **False** *(monarchical and one-party constitutions exist)*; (c) Constitution is a legal document that does not deal with ideals and values → **False** *(Functions 4 and 5)*; (d) A constitution gives its citizens a new identity → **True.**
4. **Correct or Incorrect, with reasons:** (a) *The CA did not represent Indian people since it was not elected by all citizens* → **partly incorrect**: not elected by universal suffrage, but deliberately representative (all religions, 28 SC members, diverse Congress), and its authority rested on deliberation and the nationalist inheritance. (b) *Constitution-making involved no major decision since there was general consensus* → **Incorrect**: centralised vs decentralised, Centre–State relations, judicial powers, property rights were all fiercely debated; only universal suffrage was undebated. (c) *There was little originality since much was borrowed* → **Incorrect**: every borrowed provision had to be **defended as suited to Indian problems and aspirations** (see Ambedkar's quote).
5. **Two examples each:** (a) credible leaders → Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar, Rajendra Prasad chairing committees; leaders drawn from a mass national movement, adopted by the people without a referendum. (b) power distributed to prevent subversion → horizontal fragmentation across legislature/executive/judiciary/Election Commission; amendment procedure balancing rigidity and flexibility. (c) locus of hopes → the Objectives Resolution's guarantees; the enabling provisions in the Preamble, Fundamental Rights and DPSP.
6. Why must a country clearly demarcate powers and responsibilities in the constitution? What would happen without such demarcation?
7. Why must a constitution place limitations on the rulers? Can there be a constitution that gives no power at all to citizens?
8. The **Japanese Constitution** was made while the **US occupation army** still controlled Japan after WWII, and could contain nothing the US disliked. What is the problem with this mode of constitution-making, and how was India's experience different? *(Hint: Factor 1, mode of promulgation — compare also Nepal's *granted* constitutions.)*
9. **Rajat asks:** *"The Constitution is a fifty-year-old, outdated book. No one took my consent for implementing it. It is written in such tough language that I cannot understand it. Why should I obey it?"* — how would you answer as the teacher? *(Hint: living document; people adopted it by abiding by it; it gives everyone reason to go along; it is the locus of aspirations.)*
10. **Three positions on the working of the Constitution:** Harbans — *it has succeeded in giving us a framework of democratic government*; Neha — *it promised liberty, equality, fraternity; since that has not happened, the Constitution has failed*; **Nazima — "The Constitution has not failed us. We have failed the Constitution."** Which do you agree with, and why?
