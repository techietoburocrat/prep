## The Big Idea
Having studied *what* democracy is throughout this book, we now ask: **what does democracy actually deliver?** This chapter honestly measures democracy's real-world record against five expected outcomes — accountable government, economic growth, reduced inequality, accommodation of social diversity, and dignity/freedom — and finds a "mixed record" that is nonetheless clearly superior to the alternatives.

---

## How Do We Assess Democracy's Outcomes?
Recall from Class IX that democracy is a better form of government because it: promotes equality among citizens, enhances the dignity of the individual, improves the quality of decision-making, provides a method to resolve conflicts, and allows room to correct mistakes.

But do these expectations get **realised in practice**? When we talk to people, many support democracy over alternatives (monarchy, military rule) but are **not so satisfied with democracy in practice.** So we face a **dilemma**: democracy is seen to be good *in principle* but felt to be not so good *in its practice.*

**A crucial starting attitude:** The first step is to recognise that **democracy is just a form of government** — it can only *create conditions* for achieving something; citizens must take advantage of those conditions to actually achieve the goals. Over a hundred countries today practice some kind of democratic politics with formal constitutions, elections, parties, and rights — but these democracies are **very different from each other** in their social situations, economic achievements, and cultures. So what may be achieved will differ, but we can still ask: is there something we can expect from *every* democracy, just because it is a democracy?

---

## Outcome 1: Accountable, Responsive and Legitimate Government
The most basic outcome of democracy should be a government that is **accountable** to citizens and **responsive** to their needs.

### The efficiency debate: is democracy inefficient?
Some argue democracy produces **less efficient** government — non-democratic rulers don't have to bother about deliberation or public opinion, so they can be quick and efficient. Democracy, based on deliberation and negotiation, involves some delay. **Does that make it inefficient?**
- **Think in terms of costs:** A government that takes decisions *very fast* may take decisions that are **not accepted by the people** and may therefore face problems. A democratic government takes more time (following procedures), but because it followed procedures, its decisions are **more acceptable and more effective.** So the **cost of time democracy pays is worth it.**

### Transparency — democracy's key strength here
- Democracy ensures decision-making is based on **norms and procedures.**
- A citizen who wants to know if a decision was taken through correct procedures **has the right and the means** to examine it. This is called **transparency.**
- This factor is often **missing from non-democratic governments.**

So democracy should produce a government that follows procedures and is accountable. To *measure* a democracy on this outcome, look for: **regular, free and fair elections; open public debate on major policies; and citizens' right to information** about the government.

### The honest verdict (mixed record)
- Democracies have had **greater success in setting up regular, free elections and conditions for open public debate.**
- But most democracies **fall short** of providing a fair chance to everyone, and in subjecting every decision to public debate. Democratic governments **do not have a very good record on sharing information with citizens.**
- Democracy is **not free of corruption**, and there's nothing to show non-democracies are less corrupt.
- **BUT — the one respect where democracy is clearly better:** democratic government is a **legitimate government.** It may be slow, less efficient, not always responsive or clean — **but it is people's own government.** This is why there is **overwhelming support for the idea of democracy all over the world** (surveys show people prefer democracy and see it as suitable for their country). **Democracy's ability to generate its own support is itself an outcome that cannot be ignored.**

## Outcome 2: Economic Growth and Development
If democracies produce good governments, is it fair to expect development too? **Evidence shows many democracies did not fulfil this expectation.**
- For all democracies and dictatorships from **1950–2000**, **dictatorships have a slightly higher rate of economic growth** (3.95% for democratic regimes vs 4.42% for dictatorial regimes).
- **BUT** when we compare *only poor countries*, the difference in growth rates between dictatorships and democracies is **negligible** (4.34% under dictatorship vs 4.28% under democracy).

Economic development depends on **several factors**: population size, global situation, cooperation from other countries, economic priorities. So we **cannot say democracy guarantees economic development** — but given the negligible difference and democracy's *other* positive outcomes, **it is better to prefer democracy.**

---

## Outcome 3: Reduction of Inequality and Poverty
Perhaps more than development, we expect democracies to **reduce economic disparities.** Democracies are based on **political equality** (all individuals have equal weight in electing representatives — one person, one vote). But in actual life, we find **growing economic inequalities** alongside this political equality:
- A small number of ultra-rich enjoy a highly disproportionate share of wealth and incomes, and their share has been *increasing.*
- Those at the bottom have very little; their incomes have been *declining*; sometimes they can't meet basic needs (food, clothing, house, education, health).
- **In actual life, democracies do not appear very successful in reducing economic inequalities.**

**The paradox:** The poor constitute a large proportion of voters, and no party wants to lose their votes — yet **democratically elected governments do not appear as keen to address poverty as one would expect.** (Data — Table 2 — shows within democracies like South Africa and Brazil, the **top 20% take more than 60% of national income**, leaving less than 3% for the bottom 20%; countries like Denmark and Hungary are much more equal.)

> **Conceptual takeaway:** Political equality (equal votes) and economic equality do not automatically go together — this is one of democracy's genuine unmet challenges.

## Outcome 4: Accommodation of Social Diversity
Do democracies produce a **harmonious social life**? A fair expectation is that democracy should reduce the possibility of tensions becoming explosive or violent.
- Democracies usually develop a **procedure to conduct competition** among groups, which reduces the chance of social divisions turning into violence.
- No society can *fully and permanently* resolve conflicts among groups, but democracies can **learn to respect differences and evolve mechanisms to negotiate them.** Democracy is best suited to produce this outcome; **non-democratic regimes often suppress internal social differences.** The ability to **handle social differences, divisions and conflicts** is a definite plus point of democracy.

### But — two conditions must be met (using Sri Lanka's lesson)
1. **Democracy is not simply rule by majority opinion.** The majority always needs to **work with the minority** so government represents the general view. **Majority and minority opinions are not permanent.**
2. **Rule by majority must not become rule by *majority community*** (in terms of religion, race, or language). Rule by majority means that on every decision, *different* persons and groups may form a majority. Democracy remains democracy **only as long as every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point.** If someone is **barred from ever being in the majority on the basis of birth** (e.g., a permanent religious/racial minority), then democratic rule ceases to be accommodative for that person — and this is what Sri Lanka's majoritarianism got wrong.

## Outcome 5: Dignity and Freedom of the Citizens
Democracy is **superior to any other form of government in promoting dignity and freedom** of the individual. Every individual wants respect from fellow beings; the **passion for respect and freedom** is the basis of democracy. Democracies worldwide have recognised this, **at least in principle** — which matters, because once a principle is recognised, it becomes **easier to wage a struggle against practices that violate it.**

**Two key examples:**
- **Dignity of women:** Most societies were historically male-dominated. Long struggles by women created sensitivity that respect for and equal treatment of women are now *necessary ingredients* of a democratic society. This doesn't mean women are *always* treated with respect — but **once the principle is recognised, it becomes legally and morally easier to struggle** against unacceptable treatment. In a non-democratic setup, this unacceptability would have **no legal or moral basis.**
- **Caste inequalities:** Democracy in India has strengthened the claims of the disadvantaged and discriminated castes for **equal status and equal opportunity.** There are still instances of caste-based atrocities, **but these lack the moral and legal foundation** — it is the *recognition* of the principle of equality that makes ordinary citizens value their democratic rights.

### The most distinctive feature: democracy's "examination never gets over"
This is the chapter's most powerful, exam-favourite idea:
> As democracy passes one test, it produces another test. As people get some benefits of democracy, they **ask for more** and want to make democracy even better.

That is why, when we ask people about how democracy functions, they always have **more expectations and many complaints.** **The fact that people are complaining is itself a testimony to the success of democracy** — it shows people have developed the awareness and the ability to expect things of, and look critically at, power holders and the high and mighty.

> **A public expression of dissatisfaction with democracy shows the success of the democratic project: it transforms people from the status of a *subject* into that of a *citizen*.** Most individuals today believe their vote makes a difference to how the government is run.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Transparency | The quality of a government whose decision-making follows norms and procedures that citizens have the right and means to examine |
| Accountable government | A government that must answer to and justify its actions to citizens |
| Responsive government | A government attentive to the needs and demands of the people |
| Legitimate government | A government that is the people's own — chosen by them — and hence accepted as rightful, even if slow or imperfect |
| Political equality | The principle that all individuals have equal weight in elections (one person, one vote, one value) |
| Subject vs citizen | A "subject" merely obeys; a "citizen" actively participates in, questions, and holds government accountable — democracy transforms subjects into citizens |

## Quick Recap (8 points to remember)
1. To assess democracy's outcomes, recognise that democracy is **just a form of government** — it *creates conditions* but citizens must use them; democracies differ widely, so we ask what *every* democracy can be expected to deliver.
2. **Accountable/responsive/legitimate government:** democracy's deliberation causes some delay, but its decisions are more *acceptable and effective* — its key strength is **transparency**; its record on responsiveness and corruption is mixed, but it is uniquely **legitimate** (people's own government), enjoying overwhelming global support.
3. **Economic growth:** dictatorships had a *slightly* higher growth rate (1950–2000), but among *poor* countries the difference is **negligible** — so democracy, with its other benefits, is still preferable.
4. **Inequality and poverty:** despite political equality, democracies have **not been successful in reducing economic inequalities**; elected governments seem less keen to tackle poverty than expected.
5. **Accommodation of social diversity:** democracy handles differences better than non-democracies, but must meet two conditions — **majority must work with minority**, and **rule by majority must not become rule by one (permanent) majority community** (Sri Lanka's failure).
6. Democracy remains accommodative only as long as **every citizen has a chance of being in the majority at some point** — barring someone by birth destroys this.
7. **Dignity and freedom:** democracy is superior at promoting individual dignity and freedom; recognising the *principle* of equality (for women, for lower castes) gives struggles a **moral and legal foundation** they'd otherwise lack.
8. Democracy's **most distinctive feature is that its "examination never gets over"** — people always want more; **complaints are themselves a sign of success**, transforming people from **subjects into citizens.**
