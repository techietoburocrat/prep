## The Big Idea
Social diversity does not threaten democracy — in fact, political expression of social differences is possible and sometimes even desirable. This chapter applies that idea to three kinds of social division in India — **gender, religion, and caste** — examining how each gets expressed in politics, and asking whether such expression is healthy or harmful for democracy.

## Part 1: Gender and Politics

### The public/private division
Gender division is a form of **hierarchical social division** seen everywhere, but rarely recognised in the study of politics. It tends to be seen as "natural and unchangeable" — but it is **not based on biology; it is based on social expectations and stereotypes.**
- Most families follow a **sexual division of labour**: women do all the work inside the home (cooking, cleaning, washing, raising children), while men do the work outside. This is not because men *cannot* do housework, but because they think it is women's job.
- The result: although women are half of humanity, their role in **public life, especially politics, is minimal** in most societies.

### The women's movement and feminism
Gradually, women organised and agitated for equal rights. Movements demanding **enhanced political and legal status and improved educational and career opportunities** for women arose. More radical movements aimed at equality in **personal and family life** as well — these are called **feminist** movements. (A **feminist** is a woman *or a man* who believes in equal rights and opportunities for women and men.) These movements improved women's role in public life — we now find women working as scientists, doctors, engineers, lawyers, managers, and university teachers, roles earlier not considered "suitable" for women. In some countries (e.g., Scandinavian countries — **Sweden, Norway, Finland**), women's participation in public life is very high.

### Women's disadvantage in India (with data)
India is still a **male-dominated, patriarchal society**, where women face disadvantage, discrimination, and oppression:
- **Literacy rate:** only **54% among women** vs **76% among men.** Fewer girl students go for higher studies.
- **School results:** girls perform as well as (or better than) boys, but many **drop out because parents prefer to spend on boys' education.**
- **Equal Remuneration Act, 1976** provides that equal wages should be paid for equal work — yet in almost all fields, women are **paid less than men** even for the same work.
- A "**time use survey**" showed an average Indian **woman works about one hour more per day than an average man** — but much of her work is unpaid and therefore not valued/visible.
- **Sex-selective abortion** (aborting the girl child) led the **child sex ratio** (number of girls per 1000 boys) to decline to merely **919** (per Census 2011), falling below 850 or even 800 in some states.
- Women face harassment, exploitation, and **domestic violence**, even within their own homes; urban areas have become particularly unsafe for women.

### Women's political representation
Since women's issues are not given adequate attention, feminists argue **unless women control power, their problems won't get addressed** — one way is to have more women as elected representatives.
- The percentage of elected women members in the **Lok Sabha touched 14.36% for the first time in 2019.** Their share in state assemblies is **less than 5%.**
- India is among the **bottom group of nations** in the world on this measure, behind several developing countries of Africa and Latin America.
- **Panchayati Raj** is the exception: **one-third of seats in local government bodies** (panchayats and municipalities) are **reserved for women**, resulting in more than **10 lakh elected women representatives** in rural and urban local bodies.
- Women's organisations demanded similar **one-third reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.** A bill to this effect was pending for decades — and in **2023, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women's Reservation Act, 2023)** was passed, which will give **33% reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, and the Delhi Assembly.**

> **Key conceptual takeaway:** Gender division shows that some form of social division needs to be *expressed* in politics — and that **disadvantaged groups benefit when their unequal treatment becomes a political issue** (women made these gains precisely because the issue was raised in the political domain).

## Part 2: Religion, Communalism and Politics

### Religion in politics — not always wrong
Unlike gender, religious diversity is not universal, but is fairly widespread. Religious differences are **often expressed in the field of politics.** The chapter argues religion in politics is **not inherently wrong or dangerous:**
- **Gandhiji** said religion can never be separated from politics — but by "religion" he meant not any particular religion (Hinduism/Islam) but the **moral values that inform all religions.** He believed politics must be guided by ethics drawn from religion.
- Human rights groups have demanded the government take steps to **prevent communal riots** and protect religious minorities.
- The women's movement has argued that **family laws of all religions discriminate against women**, so the government should reform them.

All these involve a relationship between religion and politics — but they are **not wrong**, as long as they treat every religion equally. **Ideas, ideals, and values drawn from different religions can and perhaps should play a role in politics.**

### Communalism — the problem
The problem begins when **religion is seen as the basis of the nation.** **Communalism** involves thinking along these lines:
- The followers of a particular religion must belong to one community, with the same fundamental interests, and any differences among them are irrelevant.
- People who follow different religions **cannot belong to the same social community.**
- In its extreme form, communalism holds that people of different religions **cannot live as equal citizens within one nation** — either one must dominate the rest, or they must form separate nations.

This belief is **fundamentally flawed** — people of one religion do *not* have the same interests in every context; there are many voices within every community.

**Forms of communalism in politics:**
1. The most common form is in **everyday beliefs** — religious prejudices, stereotypes of religious communities, and belief in the superiority of one's own religion (so common we often fail to notice it).
2. A communal mind leads to a quest for **political dominance** of one's own religious community — **majoritarian dominance** for majority communities, or a **desire for a separate political unit** for minorities.
3. **Political mobilisation on religious lines** — using sacred symbols, religious leaders, emotional appeal, and plain fear to bring followers of one religion together in the political arena.
4. Sometimes communalism takes its ugliest form — **communal violence, riots, and massacre.** India and Pakistan suffered some of the worst communal riots at Partition.

### The Secular State — India's answer
The makers of the Constitution were aware communalism is a major threat to democracy, so they chose the model of a **secular state**, reflected in several constitutional provisions:
1. There is **no official religion** for the Indian state (unlike Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Islam in Pakistan, or Christianity in England).
2. The Constitution provides all individuals and communities **freedom to profess, practise, and propagate any religion, or to follow none.**
3. The Constitution **prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion.**
4. At the same time, the Constitution allows the **state to intervene in matters of religion to ensure equality** within religious communities — for example, it **bans untouchability.**

> **Key conceptual takeaway:** A secular constitution like ours is **necessary but not sufficient** to combat communalism. Communal prejudices need to be countered in everyday life, and religion-based mobilisation needs to be countered in politics.

## Part 3: Caste and Politics

### Caste inequalities
Unlike gender and religion, caste division is **special to India.** All societies have some inequality and division of labour, but the caste system is an **extreme form**, because here hereditary occupational division was **sanctioned by rituals.** Members of the same caste group were supposed to form a social community that practised the same occupation, **married within the group, and did not eat with other caste groups.** The caste system was based on exclusion of and discrimination against the "outcaste" groups, who suffered **untouchability.**

Social reformers like **Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar, and Periyar Ramaswami Naicker** advocated and worked to establish a society without caste inequalities.

**Caste has changed** in modern India due to efforts of reformers plus socio-economic changes — **urbanisation**, growth of literacy and education, **occupational mobility**, and the weakening of landlords' position in villages. The Constitution **prohibited caste-based discrimination.** In urban areas, it often no longer matters who is next to us on a street or eating at the next restaurant table.

**But caste has NOT disappeared:**
- Most people still **marry within their own caste or tribe.**
- **Untouchability has not ended completely**, despite constitutional prohibition.
- Effects of centuries of advantage/disadvantage persist: caste groups with access to education under the old system did well acquiring modern education; those denied it lagged behind.
- **Caste continues to be linked to economic status** — the National Sample Survey shows the average economic status of caste groups still follows the old hierarchy: the "upper" castes are best off, **Dalits and Adivasis worst off**, backward classes in between. (Per the poverty-line data, Scheduled Tribes and Scheduled Castes have the highest proportions below the poverty line.)

### Caste in politics
Casteism is rooted in the belief that caste is the *sole* basis of social community — a belief **not borne out by our experience** (caste is one aspect, not the only or most important one). Caste takes various forms in politics:
- Parties **choose candidates keeping in mind the caste composition** of the electorate, to muster support.
- Parties and candidates **make appeals to caste sentiment.** Some parties are seen as favouring certain castes.
- **Universal adult franchise and one-person-one-vote** compelled leaders to mobilise castes previously treated as inferior, bringing **new consciousness** among them.

**But caste alone does not determine elections** — this impression is far from true:
- **No parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one single caste** — so every candidate/party needs to win the confidence of more than one caste to win.
- **No party wins the votes of all voters of a caste** — when a caste is called a party's "**vote bank**," it just means a large proportion of that caste votes for it.
- Many parties may put up candidates from the same caste; voters have **strong attachment to parties** often stronger than to caste; and people's **assessment of the government's performance and leaders' popularity** is often decisive.

### Politics in caste (the reverse relationship)
It's not one-way — **politics also influences the caste system.** It is not that politics gets caste-ridden; rather **caste gets politicised:**
- Each caste group tries to become **bigger** by incorporating neighbouring castes/sub-castes previously excluded.
- Caste groups enter **coalitions** with other castes, requiring dialogue and negotiation.
- New kinds of caste groups have emerged — "**backward**" and "**forward**" caste groups.

> **Key conceptual takeaway:** Caste plays **different roles** in politics. In some situations, expressing caste differences gives **disadvantaged communities (Dalits, OBCs) space to demand their share of power** and access to decision-making. But **exclusive attention to caste can be harmful** — it can divert attention from pressing issues like poverty, development, and corruption, and in some cases lead to tension, conflict, and violence.

## Social and Religious Diversity of India (key data)
- **Religious composition (Census 2011):** Hindu 79.8%, Muslim 14.2%, Christian 2.3%, Sikh 1.7%, Buddhist 0.7%, Jain 0.4%, others/not stated ~1%.
- The **Sachar Committee** estimated the Muslim proportion is likely to increase only slightly (about 3–4%) over the next 50 years — debunking the myth that it will "overtake" others. **The population balance of religious communities is not likely to change in a big way.**
- **Caste groups (Census 2011):** Scheduled Castes (Dalits) **16.6%**, Scheduled Tribes (Adivasis) **8.6%.** The Census does not count OBCs; the NSSO (2004-05) estimated them at around **41%.** Together, SC + ST + OBC = about **two-thirds** of the country's population.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sexual division of labour | A system where all work inside the home is done by (or organised by) women |
| Feminist | A woman or man who believes in equal rights and opportunities for women and men |
| Patriarchy | Literally "rule by father"; a system that values men more and gives them power over women |
| Communalism | The belief that religion is the principal basis of a social community, and that people of different religions cannot be equal citizens of one nation |
| Secular state | A state that has no official religion, guarantees freedom of all religions, prohibits religious discrimination, and can intervene to ensure equality within religions |
| Family laws | Laws dealing with family matters (marriage, divorce, adoption, inheritance); different laws apply to different religions in India |
| Urbanisation | Shift of population from rural to urban areas |
| Occupational mobility | Shift from one occupation to another, usually when a new generation takes up different work from their ancestors |
| Caste hierarchy | A ladder-like formation ranking all caste groups from "highest" to "lowest" |

## Quick Recap (8 points to remember)
1. Social diversity does **not** threaten democracy; the political expression of social differences (gender, religion, caste) can be healthy, and disadvantaged groups benefit when their inequality becomes a political issue.
2. **Gender division** is based on social expectations, not biology; it produces a **sexual division of labour** and low female political representation.
3. India's female literacy (54% vs 76%), the declining **child sex ratio (919 in 2011)**, unequal pay (despite the **Equal Remuneration Act, 1976**), and low legislative representation (Lok Sabha 14.36% in 2019) show women's disadvantage.
4. **One-third of local body seats are reserved for women**; the **Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (2023)** will give women 33% reservation in the Lok Sabha and State/Delhi assemblies.
5. Religion in politics is **not inherently wrong** (Gandhiji's ethics-based view), but **communalism** — treating religion as the basis of the nation — is a serious threat; India's answer is a **secular state** (no official religion, freedom of religion, no religious discrimination, but state can intervene to ensure equality, e.g., banning untouchability).
6. A secular constitution is **necessary but not sufficient** to combat communalism.
7. **Caste** is special to India (an extreme, ritual-sanctioned form of social division); it has weakened but not disappeared, and still strongly correlates with economic status (Dalits/Adivasis worst off).
8. **Caste alone does not decide elections** (no constituency has a single-caste majority; "vote bank" ≠ all voters; performance and party loyalty also matter); caste in politics both empowers disadvantaged groups AND can divert attention from real issues.
