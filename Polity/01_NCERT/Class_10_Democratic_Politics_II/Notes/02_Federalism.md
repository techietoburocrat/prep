# Chapter 2: Federalism

## The Big Idea
Federalism is the form of power-sharing where power is divided *vertically* — between a central authority and regional units (states) — with each level drawing its authority from the Constitution. This chapter explains what federalism is in general, what specifically makes India federal, how federalism has actually been strengthened in practice, and how a third tier (local government) was added in 1992.

---

## What is Federalism?
**Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country.** Usually a federation has **two levels of government**:
- One government for the **entire country** (responsible for a few subjects of common national interest).
- Governments at the level of **provinces or states** that look after much of the day-to-day administering of their state.

Both levels enjoy their power **independent of the other.**

**Federalism is contrasted with a unitary system**, where either there is only one level of government, or the sub-units are **subordinate to** the central government. In a unitary system, the central government can pass orders to the provincial/local government. But in a federal system, **the central government cannot order the state government to do something** — the state government has powers of its own for which it is not answerable to the central government. Both are separately answerable to the people.

### Key Features of Federalism (7 features)
1. There are **two or more levels (tiers)** of government.
2. Different tiers govern the **same citizens**, but each tier has its own **jurisdiction** in matters of legislation, taxation, and administration.
3. The jurisdictions of each tier are **specified in the Constitution** — so the existence and authority of each level is *constitutionally guaranteed.*
4. The **fundamental provisions of the constitution cannot be unilaterally changed by one level** of government — such changes require the consent of **both** levels.
5. **Courts** have the power to interpret the constitution and the powers of different levels; the highest court acts as an **umpire** if disputes arise between levels.
6. **Sources of revenue for each level are clearly specified**, to ensure its financial autonomy.
7. The federal system has **dual objectives**: to safeguard and promote the **unity** of the country, while accommodating **regional diversity.** (Two aspects are therefore crucial: *mutual trust* and *agreement to live together*.)

### Two routes to forming a federation
| Type | How it forms | Central vs state power | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coming together federations** | Independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit (pooling sovereignty, retaining identity) | Constituent states usually have **equal power** and are strong vis-à-vis the centre | USA, Switzerland, Australia |
| **Holding together federations** | A large country decides to divide its power between constituent states and the national government | Central government tends to be **more powerful**; different units often have **unequal** powers, some with special powers | **India**, Spain, Belgium |

> **Note:** Only 25 of the world's 193 countries have federal systems, but their citizens make up 40% of the world's population — most large countries are federations.

## What Makes India a Federal Country?
India emerged as an independent nation after a painful and bloody Partition. Soon after Independence, several princely states became part of the country. **The Constitution declared India a "Union of States"** — although it did not actually use the word "federation," the Indian Union is based on the principles of federalism.

The Constitution originally provided for a **two-tier system** (Union Government + State governments). Later, a **third tier** (Panchayats and Municipalities) was added.

### The three-fold distribution of legislative powers (the three Lists)
The Constitution clearly divides legislative powers between the Union and the States through **three lists:**

| List | Contains | Who legislates | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Union List** | Subjects of **national importance** (need uniform policy nationwide) | **Union Government alone** | Defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency |
| **State List** | Subjects of **state and local importance** | **State Governments alone** | Police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigation |
| **Concurrent List** | Subjects of **common interest** to both | **Both** Union and State (if their laws conflict, the **Union law prevails**) | Education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption, succession |

**Residuary powers:** Subjects that don't fall in any list (or that arose *after* the Constitution was made, like computer software) fall under **"residuary" subjects** — and the **Union Government** has the power to legislate on these.

### Not all states have equal power (asymmetric federalism)
- **Union Territories** (like Chandigarh, Lakshadweep, or Delhi) are areas too small to become independent states and could not be merged with existing states. They do **not** have the powers of a state; the **Central Government has special powers** in running them.
- Some states enjoy **special status** under **Article 371** (e.g., Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram) due to their peculiar social and historical circumstances — especially to protect the land rights, culture, and employment of indigenous peoples. For example, people who are not permanent residents of some of these states cannot buy land or house there.

### How the power-sharing is protected
- Changing this Union–State power-sharing is **not easy**: any change must be **passed by both Houses of Parliament with at least two-thirds majority**, and then **ratified by the legislatures of at least half the total states.**
- The **judiciary** oversees implementation and settles Centre–State disputes (via the High Courts and Supreme Court).
- Both Union and State governments have the power to **raise resources by levying taxes** for their respective responsibilities.

## How is Federalism Practised?
Constitutional provisions are **necessary but not sufficient** for federalism's success. India's real success is attributed to the **nature of its democratic politics**, which made *respect for diversity* and *desire for living together* into shared ideals. Three major ways this happened:

### 1. Linguistic States
The creation of linguistic states was the **first and major test** for Indian democracy.
- In **1947**, boundaries of several old states were changed to create new states, so that **people speaking the same language lived in the same state.**
- Some states were created **not** on the basis of language but to recognise differences in culture, ethnicity, or geography — e.g., **Nagaland, Uttarakhand, and Jharkhand.**
- When the demand for linguistic states was first raised, some national leaders **feared it would lead to disintegration.** The Central Government resisted it for a while — but experience has shown the **opposite: linguistic states actually made the country more united**, and made administration easier.

### 2. Language Policy
- The Constitution did **not** give the status of *national* language to any language. **Hindi was identified as the official language** — but Hindi is the mother tongue of only about **40%** of Indians, so there are safeguards to protect other languages.
- Besides Hindi, there are **21 other languages** recognised as **Scheduled Languages** by the Constitution (i.e., **22 Scheduled Languages** in total, in the Eighth Schedule). A candidate for a central government examination may opt to take it in any of these languages. States have their own **official languages** too, and much government work happens in the state's official language.
- Unlike Sri Lanka, India's leaders adopted a **cautious attitude** in spreading Hindi. According to the Constitution, the use of **English for official purposes was to stop in 1965**, but many non-Hindi states demanded English continue. In **Tamil Nadu, this movement took a violent form.** The Central Government agreed to **continue the use of English along with Hindi.**
- **Promotion of Hindi** continues to be official policy, but *promotion* does not mean the Centre can *impose* Hindi on states where people speak a different language. This flexibility helped India avoid a Sri Lanka-type situation.

### 3. Centre–State Relations
How constitutional power-sharing works *in reality* depends heavily on how ruling parties and leaders follow these arrangements.
- **Before 1990:** The same party often ruled both at the Centre and in most states, so state governments did not exercise their rights as autonomous federal units. When a *different* party ruled a state, the party at the Centre often tried to **undermine the state's power** — and the Central Government would sometimes **misuse the Constitution to dismiss state governments** controlled by rival parties. This **undermined the spirit of federalism.**
- **After 1990 — a major shift:** This period saw the **rise of regional political parties** and the beginning of the **era of coalition governments** at the Centre (since no single party got a clear Lok Sabha majority, national parties had to ally with regional parties). This led to a new culture of **power-sharing and respect for the autonomy of State Governments.**
- This trend was supported by a **major Supreme Court judgment that made it difficult for the Central Government to dismiss state governments in an arbitrary manner.** As a result, **federal power-sharing is more effective today** than it was in the early years after the Constitution came into force.

## Decentralisation in India (the Third Tier)
A vast country like India cannot be run by just two tiers — states themselves are as large as independent European countries (Uttar Pradesh is bigger than Russia in population; Maharashtra is about as big as Germany). Since these states are internally very diverse, there is a need for power-sharing *within* states too. This is the rationale for **decentralisation.**

**Decentralisation** = when power is taken away from Central and State governments and given to **local government.** The basic idea: a large number of problems are **best settled at the local level**, where people have better knowledge and can directly participate — realising the principle of **local self-government.**

**Before 1992:** Local governments existed but were directly under state government control, had no powers or resources of their own, and elections to them were **not held regularly** — so there was very little *effective* decentralisation.

### The major step: the 1992 Constitutional Amendment
The Constitution was amended in **1992** to make the third tier of democracy more powerful and effective. Key mandatory provisions:
- It became **constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections** to local government bodies.
- **Seats are reserved** in elected bodies and executive head positions for **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes (OBCs).**
- **At least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.**
- An independent **State Election Commission** was created in each state to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
- State governments are required to **share some powers and revenue** with local bodies (the nature of sharing varies from state to state).

### Structure of local government
**Rural (Panchayati Raj):**
- **Gram Panchayat** — for a village or group of villages; a council of ward members (*panch*) and a president (*sarpanch*), directly elected by all adult villagers. Works under the supervision of the **Gram Sabha** (all voters of the village), which must meet at least twice or thrice a year to approve the annual budget and review performance.
- **Panchayat Samiti / Block / Mandal** — a few gram panchayats grouped together; members elected by panchayat members.
- **Zilla (District) Parishad** — all panchayat samitis/mandals in a district together; most members elected, plus MPs and MLAs of that district; the **Zilla Parishad chairperson** is its political head.

**Urban:**
- **Municipalities** in towns; big cities have **Municipal Corporations.** Both are controlled by elected bodies. The **municipal chairperson** is the political head; in a corporation, an officer called the **mayor** is the head.

### Assessment of decentralisation
This is the **largest experiment in democracy conducted anywhere in the world** — there are now about **36 lakh elected representatives** in panchayats and municipalities, more than the population of many countries. It has deepened democracy and, notably, **increased women's representation and voice.** But difficulties remain:
- **Gram Sabhas are not held regularly.**
- Most **state governments have not transferred significant powers** to local governments, nor given adequate resources.
- So India is **still a long way from realising the ideal of self-government.**

*(The chapter also notes an experiment in Brazil — **Porto Alegre** — combining decentralisation with participative democracy, where ordinary citizens directly help make the city's budget, ensuring money is spent for the benefit of poorer colonies.)*

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Federalism | A system of government where power is divided between a central authority and constituent units (states) |
| Unitary system | A system with only one level of government, or where sub-units are subordinate to the central government |
| Jurisdiction | The area over which someone has legal authority (defined by geography or by subjects) |
| Coming together federation | Independent states voluntarily uniting to form a federation (e.g., USA); constituent states have equal power |
| Holding together federation | A large country dividing its power between the centre and states (e.g., India); centre is stronger, units may have unequal power |
| Residuary powers | Power to legislate on subjects not mentioned in any of the three lists; belongs to the Union in India |
| Coalition government | A government formed by two or more political parties coming together |
| Decentralisation | Taking power away from Central and State governments and giving it to local government |

## Quick Recap (8 points to remember)
1. **Federalism** divides power between a central authority and constituent units, each drawing authority independently from the Constitution — contrasted with a **unitary** system where sub-units are subordinate.
2. India is a **"holding together" federation** (a large country dividing power, with a stronger centre and some states having special powers) — declared a **"Union of States,"** though the word "federation" isn't used.
3. Legislative powers are divided via three lists: **Union List** (Union alone), **State List** (States alone), and **Concurrent List** (both; Union law prevails on conflict); **residuary powers** belong to the Union.
4. India has **asymmetric federalism** — Union Territories have less power, while states like Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, and Mizoram have **special status under Article 371.**
5. Federalism was strengthened in practice through **linguistic states** (which united rather than divided the country), a **cautious language policy** (Hindi as official but not imposed; 22 Scheduled Languages; English retained alongside Hindi), and healthier **Centre–State relations after 1990** (rise of regional parties, coalition era, and a Supreme Court check on arbitrary dismissal of state governments).
6. The **1992 Constitutional Amendment** created a powerful third tier: mandatory regular local elections, reservation for SC/ST/OBC and at least one-third for women, State Election Commissions, and revenue-sharing with local bodies.
7. Local government structure: **Gram Panchayat → Panchayat Samiti → Zilla Parishad** (rural, under Gram Sabha supervision); **Municipality / Municipal Corporation** (urban).
8. Decentralisation is the **world's largest democratic experiment** (~36 lakh elected reps) and has boosted women's representation, but is limited by irregular Gram Sabhas and states not transferring enough power/resources.
