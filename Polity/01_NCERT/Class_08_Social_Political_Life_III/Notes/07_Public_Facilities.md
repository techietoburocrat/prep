# Chapter 7: Public Facilities

> **Note:** The PDF file in this folder is named `07_Understanding_Marginalisation.pdf`, following an older edition's title. In the current (2024–25) NCERT edition, this chapter is actually titled **"Public Facilities."** (This edition has 8 chapters in total, organised into 5 Units — Understanding Marginalisation is Chapter 5, covered in `05_Understanding_Marginalisation.md` in this folder.)

## The Big Idea
Certain facilities — like water, healthcare, education, electricity, and public transport — are essential for a decent life, and once provided, their benefits can be shared by many people at once. Because private companies chase profit and will only serve people who can pay, it is the **government's responsibility** to ensure these **public facilities** reach everyone, especially the poor, at a price they can afford.

---

## Water and the People of Chennai: four contrasting stories
The chapter opens with Amu and Kumar travelling by bus through Chennai, observing water access in four different neighbourhoods:

| Person | Area | Water situation |
|---|---|---|
| **Mr Ramagopal** (senior govt official) | Anna Nagar | Lawns kept green by generous water spraying; tap water for most of the day; if supply is short, he simply calls a senior contact at the municipal water board and a tanker is arranged easily |
| **Subramanian** | Mylapore (apartments) | Municipal water once every two days; a private borewell gives brackish water (used only for toilets/washing); spends ₹500–600/month buying water from tankers; installs water purifiers for drinking water |
| **Siva** | Madipakkam (rents a room) | Gets water once every four days; buys bottled water for drinking; water shortage is a major reason he hasn't brought his family to live with him in Chennai |
| **Padma** (domestic worker) | Saidapet slum | Pays ₹650 rent for a hutment with no bathroom or tap; 30 hutments share one common tap, fed by a borewell for just 20 minutes, twice a day; a family can fill a maximum of 3 buckets in that time; in summer the flow reduces to a trickle, so one family's water comes "at the cost of another" |

> **The pattern is unmistakable:** the wealthier and more powerful you are, the easier and more reliable your water access — while the poorest, who can least afford alternatives, face the greatest hardship and expense in simply getting water.

## Water as Part of the Fundamental Right to Life
Water is essential not just for daily needs but also for good health — unsafe water causes diseases like **diarrhoea, dysentery, and cholera.** Over **1,600 Indians, mostly children under five, reportedly die every day** because of water-related diseases — deaths that are entirely preventable with access to safe drinking water.

**The Constitution's protection:** The **Right to Water is recognised as part of the Right to Life under Article 21** of the Constitution. This means every person — rich or poor — has the right to sufficient water to meet daily needs, at a price they can afford. This principle is called **universal access** — everyone should have both **physical access** (a tap nearby) and be able to **afford** it.

**Courts have repeatedly upheld this right.** In 2007, the **Andhra Pradesh High Court** heard a case based on a letter from a villager in Mahbubnagar district, complaining that a textile company was discharging poisonous chemicals into a stream, contaminating the groundwater used for irrigation and drinking. The Court restated that the right to safe drinking water is a Fundamental Right, and directed the district collector to supply **25 litres of water to each person** in the affected village.

## What Are Public Facilities?
Like water, other essential facilities need to be provided to everyone — **healthcare, sanitation, electricity, public transport, schools, and colleges.** These are collectively called **public facilities.**

**Key characteristic:** once a public facility is provided, its benefits can be **shared by many people at once.** For instance, a village school benefits many children's education; electricity to an area lets farmers run pump sets for irrigation, lets people open small electricity-run workshops, and benefits the whole village in different ways.

---

## The Government's Role: why can't private companies just handle it?
Since public facilities are so important, **someone** must take responsibility for providing them to everyone — and that "someone" is the **government.** Here's the reasoning:

- **Private companies operate for profit.** For many public facilities, there simply is **no profit to be made** — for example, what profit could a company earn from keeping drains clean, or running an anti-malaria campaign? Private companies have little interest in this kind of work.
- For facilities like schools, hospitals, and water supply — where profit **is** possible — private companies often do get involved, especially in big cities. But then they provide the facility **only at a price**, meaning **only some people can afford it.** If we let facilities go only to those who can pay, then people who cannot afford to pay would be **deprived of the opportunity to live a decent life.**
- Since public facilities relate to people's **basic needs**, and the Constitution's **Right to Life** applies to *every* person in the country, **the responsibility to provide public facilities must be the government's.**

### Where does the government get the money?
The government's yearly **budget**, presented in Parliament, is an account of past spending and planned future spending. The government's main source of revenue is **taxes** collected from the people, which it is empowered to use for public programmes. For water specifically, the government incurs costs in pumping, transporting over long distances, laying pipes, treating water for impurities, and finally collecting and treating wastewater — costs met partly through taxes and partly by charging a price for water, kept low enough that most people can afford a basic minimum amount daily.

**A dialogue worth remembering — Amu and Kumar debate whose responsibility public facilities are:**
> Kumar suggests public facilities are meant for "those who live in proper houses in colonies... the people who pay taxes." Amu pushes back: "Slum dwellers are also citizens and they have rights too." When Kumar worries the government will "go bankrupt," Amu responds that the government "has to find a way," since the Constitution recognises public facilities like water as part of the Right to Life, which must be protected for everyone to live a decent life.

## Water Supply to Chennai: Is It Available to All?
Despite the ideal that public facilities should reach everyone, in reality there is a **great shortage**, and the burden of that shortage **falls mostly on the poor.**
- Chennai's municipal water meets only about **half** the city's needs on average; areas near storage points get more water, colonies further away get less.
- The **middle class copes** through private means — digging borewells, buying water from tankers, using bottled water.
- Access to *safe* drinking water is also unequal: the wealthy benefit from a booming market in bottled water and water purifiers, while the poor are left out — meaning in reality, **it is mostly people with money who effectively have the "right to water,"** a situation far removed from the ideal of universal access.

**A troubling side effect — "Taking water from farmers":** Private companies now supply water to cities by buying it from nearby towns and villages, using fleets of over 13,000 water tankers in Chennai alone. Water dealers pay farmers in advance for the right to extract water from their land — water taken not just from agriculture, but from the drinking water supplies of villagers themselves. As a result, **groundwater levels have dropped drastically** in these towns and villages.

---

## In Search of Alternatives: should private companies take over water supply?
Some argue that since government water departments often run at a loss and can't meet demand, **private companies should take over** water supply, claiming they would perform better. The chapter examines this claim against several facts:

1. **Throughout the world, water supply is overwhelmingly a government function** — there are very few real-world examples of successful private water supply.
2. **Porto Alegre, Brazil** — a rare success story where the *government's own* water department achieved **universal access** to safe water, kept average prices low, charged the poor half the basic rate, and ran transparently, with citizens directly voting on the department's priorities in public meetings. This helped Porto Alegre achieve a far lower infant death rate than most other cities in the world.
3. In cases where water supply **was** handed over to private companies, prices rose steeply, becoming unaffordable for many — leading to huge protests and riots (e.g., in **Bolivia**), which forced governments to take the service back from private hands.
4. **Within India, there are limited success stories in government-run water supply:** Mumbai's water department raises enough money through charges to cover its costs; Hyderabad has improved coverage and revenue collection; Chennai has taken initiatives for rainwater harvesting to boost groundwater, while still keeping private companies "on contract" — meaning the government department sets the rates and gives permission, rather than fully privatising the service.

> **The chapter's implicit conclusion:** Even where private players are involved, the **government must retain overall responsibility and control** to ensure public facilities remain affordable and reach everyone.

## Extending Sanitation Facilities
Sanitation (safe disposal of human waste, preventing water-borne diseases) is just as important as safe water, but coverage in India lags even further behind: as of 2011, about **87% of households** had access to drinking water, but only about **53%** had access to sanitation (toilets within their premises) — again, it is largely **the poor, in both rural and urban areas**, who lack access.

**A historical echo:** Mahatma Gandhi, recounting untouchables' grievances at the Rajkot Sanitation Committee in 1896, quoted them saying: *"'Latrines for us!' they exclaimed in astonishment. 'We go and perform our functions out in the open.' Latrines are for you big people."* This shows how access to basic sanitation has long been denied to the poor and marginalised.

**Sulabh** is a non-government organisation that has worked for nearly five decades to address sanitation problems facing low-caste, low-income people in India — it has built more than **8,500 community toilet blocks and 1.5 million household toilets**, giving sanitation access to **20 million people**, mostly the poor working class. Sulabh partners with municipalities (which provide land and government funds) and sometimes charges small user fees (e.g., ₹2 per use in cities) to cover maintenance.

*(India's rural household electrification stood at 96% in 2022, per the National Family Health Survey — showing real, though incomplete, progress on another key public facility.)*

## Conclusion
Public facilities relate to our most basic needs, and the Indian Constitution recognises the right to water, health, and education as part of the **Right to Life.** Ensuring adequate public facilities for everyone is therefore one of government's core responsibilities.

However, **progress has been far from satisfactory:** there is an overall shortage in supply, and stark inequalities in distribution — towns and villages remain under-provided compared to metros and large cities, and poorer localities remain under-serviced compared to wealthy ones. **Handing these facilities over entirely to private companies is not the answer** — since every citizen has an equal right to these facilities, any real solution must ensure they are provided to all people in an **equitable** manner.

| Company | A form of business set up by individuals/groups (a **private company**, e.g., Tata Steel) or by the government (e.g., Indian Oil) |
| Universal access | Achieved when everyone has both physical access to a good/facility, and can also afford it |
| Basic needs | Primary requirements necessary for survival: food, water, shelter, sanitation, healthcare, and education |

## Quick Recap (6 points to remember)
1. **Public facilities** (water, healthcare, education, electricity, transport) benefit many people once provided, and are essential to people's **basic needs.**
2. The **Right to Water is part of the Right to Life (Article 21)**; courts have repeatedly enforced this, as in the 2007 Andhra Pradesh High Court case on contaminated groundwater.
3. It is the **government's responsibility**, not private companies', to provide public facilities to everyone — because private companies chase profit, leaving those who cannot pay without access to a decent life.
4. Chennai's four contrasting water stories (Ramagopal, Subramanian, Siva, Padma) show that **water access today depends heavily on wealth and power**, not just where you live.
5. Privatising water supply has generally **not worked well globally** (steep price rises, protests in Bolivia) — the rare success (Porto Alegre) came from an efficient, transparent **government** water department, not a private one.
6. **Sanitation coverage in India lags behind water access**, again hitting the poor hardest; organisations like **Sulabh** have worked to fill this gap, but government responsibility for equitable, affordable access remains essential.
