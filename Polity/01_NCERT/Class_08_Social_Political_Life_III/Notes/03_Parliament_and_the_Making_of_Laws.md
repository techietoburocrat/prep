# Chapter 3: Parliament and the Making of Laws

> **Note:** The PDF file in this folder is named `03_Why_Do_We_Need_a_Parliament.pdf`, following an older edition's title. In the current (2024–25) NCERT edition, this chapter is actually titled **"Parliament and the Making of Laws."**

## The Big Idea
Democracy rests on the consent and participation of citizens. In India, this consent is expressed through elected representatives who sit in Parliament — the institution that selects the national government, controls and questions it, and makes the laws that govern the country. But Parliament's work does not happen in isolation: ordinary citizens play a crucial role in shaping which laws get made and how.

---

## Why should people decide? (Setting the historical context)
India became independent on 15 August 1947, after a long freedom struggle in which people from many different backgrounds participated. Under British colonial rule, people lived in fear of the government and had almost no say in decisions that affected them — criticising these decisions could be dangerous. The freedom movement changed this: nationalists began openly criticising British rule and demanding representation.

- As early as **1885**, the Indian National Congress demanded elected members in the legislature with the right to discuss the budget and ask questions.
- The **Government of India Act, 1909** allowed for some elected representation, but these early legislatures under British rule did not allow *all* adults to vote, nor did they allow genuine participation in decision-making.

The experience of colonial rule, combined with the widespread participation of ordinary people in the freedom struggle, convinced India's leaders that in independent India, **all persons should be able to participate in decision-making.** This meant the government could no longer simply do as it pleased — it had to remain sensitive to people's needs and demands. This aspiration was made concrete in the Constitution through the principle of **universal adult franchise**: every adult citizen of the country has the right to vote.

---

## People and their Representatives: the idea of consent
The starting point of any democracy is **consent** — the desire, approval, and participation of the people. It is the decision of the people that creates a democratic government and determines how it functions. The basic idea is that the **individual citizen is the most important person**, and government institutions must have the trust of citizens.

How do individuals give their approval to the government? Primarily through **elections**. People elect their representatives to Parliament; one group from among these elected representatives then forms the government. **Parliament — made up of all elected representatives together — controls and guides the government.** In this way, people, through their chosen representatives, both *form* the government and *control* it.

---

## The Structure of Parliament
The **Parliament of India (Sansad)** is the supreme law-making institution of the country, and it has **two Houses**:

| House | Also called | Membership | Presided over by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lok Sabha** | House of the People | 543 members, directly elected by the people | The Speaker |
| **Rajya Sabha** | Council of States | 233 elected members + 12 nominated by the President = 250 total | The Vice-President of India |

- **Lok Sabha elections** are held once every five years. The country is divided into numerous constituencies, and each constituency elects **one representative** to the Lok Sabha. Candidates usually contest as members of different political parties.
- **Rajya Sabha** functions primarily as the representative of India's states in Parliament. Its members are elected by the elected members of the various State Legislative Assemblies (not directly by the public). While the Rajya Sabha can also initiate legislation, and a bill must pass through it to become law, its important role is **reviewing and altering (if needed) laws that originate in the Lok Sabha.**

---

## Function A: Selecting the National Government
After Lok Sabha elections, a list is prepared showing how many MPs (Members of Parliament) belong to each political party. **For a party to form the government, it needs a majority of elected MPs** — since there are 543 elected Lok Sabha members, a party needs at least **272 (half + 1)** to have a majority.

**All the political parties that are not part of the majority party/coalition together form the Opposition.** The largest of these parties is called the **Opposition party.**

**Example — 17th Lok Sabha Elections (May 2019):**
| Party | MPs won |
|---|---|
| **BJP** | **303** ✅ (clear majority) |
| INC (Congress) | 52 |
| DMK | 24 |
| AITC | 22 |
| YSRCP | 22 |
| *(and many smaller/regional parties)* | — |
| **Total** | **543** |

Since BJP crossed the 272-seat mark on its own, it could form the government without needing a coalition.

**Example — 15th Lok Sabha Elections (May 2009):** Here, INC won 206 seats — a large number, but *not* enough on its own for a majority. So INC had to join together with other political parties who shared similar concerns, forming the **United Progressive Alliance (UPA)**. This kind of arrangement, where multiple parties join forces to form the government because no single party has a majority, is called a **coalition government.**

Once elected, candidates become **MPs (Members of Parliament)**, and together they make up Parliament. One of the Lok Sabha's most important functions is to **select the executive** — the group of people who implement the laws Parliament makes, commonly referred to as "the government."

**How the executive forms:**
1. The **Prime Minister** of India is the leader of the ruling party in the Lok Sabha.
2. From among the MPs of her/his party, the Prime Minister selects **ministers** to work with them.
3. These ministers then take charge of specific areas of government functioning — such as health, education, or finance.

---

## Function B: To Control, Guide, and Inform the Government
Parliament's job doesn't end once the government is formed — Parliament must also keep a close watch on it. It does this mainly through the **Question Hour**.

- The **Question Hour** is the period, held while Parliament is in session, when MPs can ask the government questions about its own functioning.
- Through this process, the government is **alerted to its shortcomings**, and Parliament comes to know the opinions of the people through their elected representatives.
- Asking questions of the government is a **crucial task for every MP**, and the **Opposition parties play a critical role** here — highlighting drawbacks in government policies and programmes, and mobilising public support for alternatives.

**Real example — a question asked in Lok Sabha (15 December 2017):** MP Manoj Rajoria asked the Minister of Women and Child Development whether the government proposes to converge various schemes and policies for children, and to share the details. The Minister of State replied describing the "National Plan of Action for Children 2016," which addresses children's rights under four priority areas: Survival/Health/Nutrition, Education/Development, Protection, and Participation.

This example shows how the Question Hour lets MPs extract detailed, accountable answers directly from ministers — a powerful tool for keeping the government "on its toes." Additionally, on all matters relating to government finances, **Parliament's approval is essential** — another key way Parliament controls, guides, and informs the government.

## Function C: Making New Laws — how a law is actually born
Parliament's role in making laws is central to its purpose, and the process is often set in motion by different groups in society who raise the need for a particular law. Parliament's important role here is to remain **sensitive to the problems people actually face.**

### Case study: The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005
This is a detailed, real example of how ordinary citizens shaped a national law, told through a storyboard involving Kusum and Shazia, two women working at a women's organisation.

**What is domestic violence?** It generally refers to injury or harm — physical, verbal, sexual, or economic — caused by an adult male (usually the husband) against his wife. The 2005 Act extended "domestic" to cover *any* woman living or who has lived in a shared household with the abusive male member.

**The full journey, step by step:**

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| **1991 (and through the 1990s)** | Women came to the women's organisation's office with harrowing stories — being beaten by husbands, verbally abused by in-laws, denied access to their own bank accounts, threatened with eviction from their homes. They didn't want to go to the police (a criminal process); they wanted the violence to stop and to keep their home. But the **existing law was only a criminal law** and didn't offer these options. |
| **Throughout the 1990s** | The need for a **new civil law** was raised at public hearings across the country — testimonies showed women wanted protection against being beaten, the right to continue living in their shared household, and temporary relief. |
| **1999** | **Lawyers Collective** (a group of lawyers, law students, and activists) led nation-wide consultations and drafted the **Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Bill.** This draft was widely circulated, and groups debated details — how broadly to define violence (physical, economic, sexual, verbal, emotional), whether the law should protect women from eviction, and whether it should provide monetary relief. |
| **2002** | The Bill was introduced in Parliament — **but** women's groups felt it didn't reflect what they had been suggesting, and strongly opposed it in its existing form. They held press conferences and even discussed starting an online petition. |
| **2002 (December)** | Several women's organisations and the **National Commission for Women** made submissions to the **Parliamentary Standing Committee**, arguing the definition of domestic violence needed to change and that the law should provide for temporary custody of children. |
| **December 2002** | The Standing Committee submitted its recommendations, accepting **most of the demands of the women's groups.** |
| **2005** | A **new bill was reintroduced** in Parliament, incorporating these changes, and was passed in **both Houses of Parliament.** It was then sent to the **President for assent.** |
| **2006** | **The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act came into effect.** |

**Why this law matters:** It was the **first law to recognise a woman's right to live in a violence-free home**, and it gave a comprehensive definition of domestic violence. As a **civil law**, it aims to provide relief to millions of women — wives, mothers, daughters, and sisters — facing violence in their own homes. Crucially, it recognised women's **right to continue living in a shared household**, allowed them to obtain a **protection order** against further violence, and enabled them to claim **monetary relief** for expenses including medical costs.

> **A note on language:** Women who survive violence or abuse are often labelled simply as "victims." But since women actively struggle, in many different ways, to survive and resist these situations, the chapter points out it is more accurate — and more respectful — to call them **survivors** rather than victims.

**The big lesson:** From establishing the *need* for a new law, all the way through to it being passed, **the voice of ordinary citizens was crucial at every single stage.** This voice reaches Parliament through TV reports, newspaper editorials, radio broadcasts, and local meetings — all of which help make Parliament's work more accessible and transparent to the people. Citizens' role does not end after voting; people must continue watching, questioning, and pushing for the laws they need.

## Unpopular and Controversial Laws
Not every law that Parliament passes is popular. A law can be **constitutionally valid (legal)** and yet remain **unpopular** if people feel its underlying intention is unfair or harmful. In a democracy, citizens can express this unwillingness to accept such laws by criticising them, holding public meetings, writing in newspapers, and reporting to TV news channels. **If enough people feel a law is wrong, this creates pressure on Parliament to change it.**

**Example — hawking and street vending laws:** Many municipal laws ban hawking and street vending within city limits, in order to keep pavements and public spaces walkable. This need is real — but hawkers and vendors also provide essential, affordable, and efficient services to millions of city residents, and hawking is often their **only means of livelihood.** If a law favours one group (people wanting clear pavements) while completely disregarding another (vendors needing to earn a living), it becomes **controversial** and can lead to real conflict. People who believe a law is unfair can approach the **courts**, which have the power to **modify or cancel laws** that don't conform to the Constitution.

**A historical example of protest against unjust laws — Rosa Parks:** As you read about in Class VII, Rosa Parks, an African-American woman, refused to give up her bus seat to a white man on 1 December 1955, protesting a law that segregated public spaces between white and Black Americans. Her refusal was a key event that sparked the **Civil Rights Movement**, ultimately leading to the **Civil Rights Act of 1964**, which prohibited discrimination based on race, religion, or national origin in the USA. This shows how citizens standing up against an unjust law can eventually reshape it.

**A historical Indian example — the Sedition Act of 1870:** Under British colonial rule, the Sedition Act allowed the government to arrest and detain almost anyone it considered to be "stirring up resistance," without needing clear evidence beforehand or offering a proper trial. Indian nationalists protested and criticised this and other arbitrary, repressive colonial laws as part of their broader fight for the **rule of law** — the idea that laws must be fair, clear, and apply equally to everyone, including the government itself.

**The takeaway:** Citizens' involvement doesn't stop once representatives are elected. It is the ongoing **extent, involvement, and enthusiasm** of the people — through protest, criticism, and engagement with the media — that helps Parliament properly perform its representative functions.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Approval | Giving one's consent to and support for something; here, both the formal consent given through elections, and the ongoing trust Parliament must keep earning from the people |
| Coalition | A temporary alliance of political parties formed to reach a majority and form the government, when no single party has enough seats on its own |
| Criticise | To find fault with or disapprove of something — in this chapter, citizens finding fault with government functioning |
| Evolution | The process of development from a simple to a more complex form — here, how the need to protect women from domestic violence developed into an enforceable national law |
| Sedition | Anything the government considers as stirring up resistance or rebellion against it; under the British Sedition Act of 1870, people could be arrested on this basis with very little evidence |
| Repressive | Laws or actions that severely and unfairly control people, often preventing them from exercising Fundamental Rights like free speech and assembly |
| Unresolved | Situations with no easy or immediate solution |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. India adopted **universal adult franchise** because the freedom struggle showed that all citizens should be able to participate in decision-making, unlike under colonial rule.
2. Democracy rests on **consent** — people elect representatives to Parliament, who in turn **form and control** the government.
3. Parliament (Sansad) has two Houses: the **Lok Sabha** (543 directly elected members, presided over by the Speaker) and the **Rajya Sabha** (233 elected + 12 nominated, presided over by the Vice-President; represents the states, reviews laws).
4. Parliament's three main functions: **(A)** selecting the national government (majority = 272+ seats; fewer seats can mean a **coalition government**); **(B)** controlling and questioning the government (mainly through **Question Hour**); **(C)** making laws.
5. The **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (2005)** shows, step by step, how ordinary citizens and civil society groups (Lawyers Collective, women's organisations, the National Commission for Women) shaped a law from a felt need in 1991 to its passage in 2005.
6. A law can be legal but still **unpopular**; citizens use protest, media, and the courts to challenge laws they believe are unfair (hawking bans, Rosa Parks and the US Civil Rights Movement, and the colonial-era Sedition Act of 1870).
7. Citizenship doesn't end with voting — ongoing public involvement (through media, protest, and engagement) keeps Parliament properly representative.
