# Chapter 4: Working of Institutions

## The Big Idea
Big decisions in a democracy are never made by one single person alone — they pass through several institutions (Parliament, the Executive, the Judiciary), each checking and balancing the others. This chapter uses the real story of OBC reservation to show these institutions in action, then examines each one — Parliament, the Political Executive (PM and Council of Ministers), the President, and the Judiciary — in turn.

---

## Opening story: OBC Reservation and the Mandal Commission
In **August 1990**, the government led by Prime Minister **V.P. Singh** decided to implement the recommendations of the **Mandal Commission**, reserving **27% of government jobs for Other Backward Classes (OBCs).** This decision triggered huge protests, especially among urban youth from the upper castes who felt this policy would hurt their opportunities. Some incidents of self-immolation by protesting students were widely reported.

The matter eventually reached the **Supreme Court.** In the landmark **Indra Sawhney case (1992)**, the Supreme Court upheld the government's decision to reserve 27% of seats for OBCs, but added an important qualification — the **"creamy layer"** (the more affluent and socially advanced members of OBCs) should be **excluded** from the benefit of this reservation.

> **Why this story opens the chapter:** It shows how one single decision actually involves multiple institutions working together and checking each other: the **Executive** (V.P. Singh's government) took the original decision; the **Legislature** (Parliament) had earlier passed related laws and represented public opinion; and the **Judiciary** (the Supreme Court) reviewed and modified the decision. This is exactly how "working of institutions" is meant to function in a democracy.

---

## Why Do We Need a Parliament?
In India, as in most democracies, ultimate decision-making power rests with people's elected representatives — collectively, this body is called the **Legislature**, or in India's case, **Parliament** (at the national level) and **State Legislatures** (at the state level).

**Parliament is important for several reasons:**
1. Parliament is the **final authority for making laws** in any country. This lawmaking power is shared by the two Houses of Parliament, though the *final* say rests with the Lok Sabha in most matters.
2. Parliament exercises **control over the money available** to the government — no government can spend without Parliament's approval of its **budget.**
3. Parliament is the highest forum of **discussion and debate** on public issues and national policy.
4. Parliament **controls, guides, and informs the government** — this is done through:
   - **Question Hour**, a time when MPs can ask sharp questions of ministers about government functioning.
   - Various parliamentary committees, where MPs from different parties discuss and evaluate government work in detail.
5. If necessary, Parliament can **remove the government** — the Lok Sabha can pass a **no-confidence motion** against the Council of Ministers; if it passes, the government must resign.

### Two Houses of Parliament
- **Lok Sabha** — the "House of the People," whose members are **directly elected by the people**, through Lok Sabha elections held generally every five years.
- **Rajya Sabha** — the "Council of States," which represents the various states of India. Its members are elected indirectly, by the elected members of the respective State Legislative Assemblies.

While both Houses have equal powers in some respects, in matters of finance and confidence in the government, the **Lok Sabha exercises more power.** Also, only the Lok Sabha can be dissolved before its term (in case no government can be formed with majority support), unlike the Rajya Sabha, which is a permanent House (not subject to dissolution).

---

## Political Executive
The **Executive** is the branch of government responsible for the day-to-day administration and implementation of laws and policies. There are two categories:
- **Political Executive:** elected leaders who take the big, short-term decisions and are politically accountable to the people — e.g., the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers.
- **Permanent Executive:** unelected civil servants (bureaucrats, officers of the IAS/IPS, etc.) who carry out day-to-day administration on a permanent, non-political basis, and remain in position across changes in government.

### Prime Minister and Council of Ministers
- The **Prime Minister** is the *most* important political institution in the country's day-to-day governance. The President appoints the leader of the majority party (or coalition) in the Lok Sabha as Prime Minister.
- The PM then selects other ministers, who together form the **Council of Ministers**, and are formally appointed by the President on the PM's advice.
- The Council of Ministers usually has three ranks: **Cabinet Ministers** (usually senior leaders, in charge of the most important ministries, and part of the Cabinet — the inner, most powerful decision-making circle); **Ministers of State with independent charge**, who look after smaller departments independently; and **Ministers of State**, who assist Cabinet Ministers.
- Since decisions of the government are usually taken through **collective decision-making** in the Cabinet, all ministers, even from different ministries, share collective responsibility for these decisions before Parliament.

---

## President
The **President of India** is the **Head of State** — India's foremost, formal or nominal, head, while the *real* power rests with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.

- The President is elected, **not directly by the people**, but indirectly — by an **electoral college** consisting of all elected members of Parliament (both Houses) and elected members of the various State Legislative Assemblies.
- All governmental decisions are taken in the **name of the President**, and the President has certain powers — for example, appointing the Prime Minister, appointing other constitutional authorities, and even (in rare, disputed situations) exercising more independent judgement, such as when no single party has a clear majority.
- However, by convention and the working of parliamentary democracy, the President normally acts on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers.**

---

## Why Do We Need a Judiciary?
As you studied in Class VIII, the **Judiciary** — the system of courts — plays a vital role in a democracy. Recapping and building on that:

- India has an **independent judiciary**, and an **integrated judicial system**, meaning the decisions made by higher courts are binding on lower courts.
- The judiciary has the power of **judicial review** — it can strike down particular laws passed by the Parliament if it believes these violate the basic structure of the Constitution.
- Ordinary citizens can approach courts if they believe their Fundamental Rights have been violated, or through **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, which makes justice more accessible.
- The judiciary's independence (protected appointments, security of tenure for judges, and separation from the legislature/executive) allows it to act as a genuine check on the power of both other branches of government.

*(The Indra Sawhney case, discussed at the start of this chapter, is itself a powerful example of judicial review in action — the courts examining and modifying a major government policy decision.)*

---

## Why Political Institutions Matter, Even When They Seem Slow or Imperfect
The chapter closes on an important reflection: political institutions like Parliament, the Executive, and the Judiciary can often seem slow, argumentative, or imperfect. But they matter because:
- They ensure power is not concentrated in one person's hands.
- They provide mechanisms of **accountability** — decisions can be questioned, debated, and even reversed through legitimate procedures.
- Even a controversial decision, like reserving seats for OBCs, gets scrutinised, debated in public, and reviewed by courts — exactly the kind of process a non-democratic system would not allow.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mandal Commission | A commission whose recommendation to reserve 27% of government jobs for OBCs became government policy in 1990 |
| Creamy layer | The more affluent, socially advanced members of OBCs, excluded from reservation benefits following the Indra Sawhney judgment (1992) |
| Legislature | The elected law-making body of government; called Parliament at the national level in India |
| No-confidence motion | A motion in the Lok Sabha which, if passed, requires the Council of Ministers to resign |
| Political Executive | Elected leaders (like the PM and ministers) responsible for policy decisions and accountable to the people |
| Permanent Executive | Unelected civil servants who carry out day-to-day administration regardless of which government is in power |
| Electoral College | The specific group of elected representatives (MPs and MLAs) who indirectly elect the President |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. The **Mandal Commission/OBC reservation story** shows Executive, Legislature, and Judiciary all playing a role in one major policy — the **Indra Sawhney case (1992)** upheld 27% OBC reservation while excluding the **"creamy layer."**
2. **Parliament** makes laws, controls government finances (the budget), is the top forum for debate, controls/guides the government (Question Hour, committees), and can remove the government via a **no-confidence motion.**
3. Parliament has two Houses: the directly elected **Lok Sabha** (more powerful in finance/confidence matters) and the indirectly elected **Rajya Sabha** (a permanent House representing the states).
4. The **Political Executive** (PM + Council of Ministers, in three ranks: Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State with independent charge, and Ministers of State) makes day-to-day policy decisions; the **Permanent Executive** (civil servants) implements them regardless of which government is in power.
5. The **President**, elected indirectly through an electoral college of MPs and MLAs, is the ceremonial Head of State, normally acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers.
6. The **Judiciary** is independent, integrated, and has the power of **judicial review**, checking both the Legislature and the Executive.
7. Political institutions matter — even when slow or imperfect — because they prevent concentration of power and keep government decisions accountable and open to challenge.
