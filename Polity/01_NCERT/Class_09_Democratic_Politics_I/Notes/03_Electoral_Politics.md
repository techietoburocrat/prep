# Chapter 3: Electoral Politics

## The Big Idea
Elections are the mechanism through which ordinary citizens choose their representatives and, indirectly, hold power over their government. This chapter explains why elections matter, how India's election system actually works step by step, and what makes Indian elections genuinely democratic despite real flaws.

---

## Opening story: Rao Birendra Singh, Haryana (1967)
In the 1967 Haryana state election, **Rao Birendra Singh** won as a Congress candidate but soon resigned and got re-elected as a candidate of a different party. Within one year, he changed parties (and his position) **three times**, each time becoming a minister in the new coalition government he helped form. This kind of frequent, opportunistic party-switching by elected legislators, purely to gain power, became so common in this period that it earned a famous nickname: **"Aaya Ram Gaya Ram."**

> **Why this story opens the chapter:** It raises a natural question — if this is how some elected leaders behave, why do we hold elections and trust representative democracy at all? The rest of the chapter builds the case for why elections remain essential, and what makes them meaningful.

---

## Why Do We Need Elections?
In any large, modern society, it's simply not possible for every citizen to take part directly in every decision affecting them. **Representative democracy** solves this: people elect their representatives, who then make decisions on their behalf, within a legislature. Elections allow people to:
- **Choose who will make laws** for them.
- **Choose who will form the government** and take major decisions.
- **Choose the ruling party**, whose policies will guide the government and the lawmaking process.
- **Create a Government which people think will be responsive to their needs and demands.**

## What Makes an Election Democratic?
Not every election is genuinely democratic. A **democratic election** requires:
1. Everyone should be able to **choose** — this requires that a *choice* actually exists, offered on a fair basis, to every voter.
2. This choice must be offered at **regular intervals.**
3. **The candidate preferred by the people should get elected.**
4. Elections must be conducted in a **free and fair manner**, where people can genuinely exercise this choice.
5. **Minimum conditions:** everyone should be able to elect representatives with equal value ("one person, one vote, one value").

## What is India's System of Elections?

### Electoral Constituencies
The entire country is divided into different **electoral constituencies** — areas whose voters elect one representative. For Lok Sabha elections, the country is divided into **543 constituencies**; the elected candidate is called a Member of Parliament (MP). Similarly, each state is divided into **Vidhan Sabha (state Assembly) constituencies**, each electing an MLA. Villages have similarly demarcated constituencies for Panchayat elections, and cities for Municipal elections.

### Reserved Constituencies
To ensure fair representation of every social section, some constituencies are **reserved for Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST)** — meaning only a candidate belonging to that community can contest from that constituency, while every citizen in the constituency still gets to vote. As of the current arrangement, the proportion of seats reserved for SCs and STs is equal to their proportion in the population. Seats are also reserved for women in local government bodies (Panchayats and Municipalities), as you read in Class VI.

### Delimitation
The process of drawing the boundary lines of these constituencies (so each has a roughly similar population) is called **delimitation.** This is done periodically to keep constituencies fairly sized.

### Voters' List (Electoral Roll)
Once constituencies are decided, the next step is preparing the list of everyone entitled to vote — this official list is called the **voters' list**, commonly known in India as the **Electoral Roll.** Names can be added if left out, and this list is revised/updated regularly. Every adult citizen (18 years and above) is issued a **Voter ID card**, aimed at making it easier to check bogus voting and voting fraud.

### Nomination of Candidates
Anyone who can be a voter and meets minimum age requirements can generally become a candidate. Political parties nominate their candidates, who receive the party's official symbol and support. Any independent candidate can also contest, usually needing supporters to formally propose their name.

**Important legal requirement (since 2003):** every candidate must file an **affidavit**, giving a sworn statement, providing full details of:
- Any **serious criminal cases** pending against the candidate.
- Details of the **assets and liabilities** of the candidate and their family.
- The **educational qualifications** of the candidate.

This information is made public so voters can make an informed choice.

### Election Campaign
In India, campaigns generally take place over a **two-week period** between the announcement of the final list of candidates and the date the polling actually takes place. During this time, political parties and candidates try to:
- Put forward some **issue**, seek people's support, and criticise other parties' record.
- Mobilise voters through public meetings, rallies, door-to-door canvassing, and now social media.

**The Model Code of Conduct:** All political parties and candidates in an election are supposed to agree to a code of conduct — for instance, no party or candidate can:
- Bribe or threaten voters.
- Appeal in the name of caste or religion.
- Use government resources for election campaigning.
- Spend more than the prescribed limit (currently set) on election expenses.

*(Once the election is announced, ministers cannot announce any new scheme or lay foundation stones of any project, cannot make any big policy decisions, or use official machinery for election work.)*

### Polling Day and Counting of Votes
On the designated day (or days), citizens go to their **polling booth** to cast their vote, done in secret through a ballot to protect voters from any pressure. India today uses **Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs)** instead of paper ballots for this process. Once polling ends, all EVMs are sealed and stored securely, and on a fixed date, all votes are counted, usually at one counting centre for each constituency, under close scrutiny of representatives of the candidates. The candidate who secures the **highest number of votes** from a constituency is declared elected.

## What Makes Elections in India Democratic?

### 1. An Independent Election Commission
The body responsible for conducting elections at every level — from the President of India, to the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies, right down to Panchayats and Municipalities — is the **Election Commission of India (ECI)**, which enjoys enormous *independent* power. It:
- Takes decisions on every aspect of conducting and regulating elections, from announcing dates to counting votes.
- Implements the Model Code of Conduct, and can punish candidates/parties who violate it.
- Can order a **re-poll** in a constituency, or in specific polling booths, if it believes the election there was unfair.
- Can direct the government to transfer certain officials so they cannot influence the election in their favour.

**A well-known example of the ECI's independence:** **T.N. Seshan**, as Chief Election Commissioner in the 1990s, is famously remembered for enforcing electoral discipline strictly, showing how independent and powerful this constitutional body can be, even against powerful ruling parties.

### 2. Popular participation
Despite frequent complaints about politicians and parties, people continue to participate in elections in very large numbers — often, poorer, less-privileged citizens vote *more* actively than wealthier, urban citizens. Popular participation in elections in India has generally either gone up or remained stable, unlike the declining trend seen in some other democracies.

### 3. Acceptance of election outcomes
The ruling party frequently loses elections in India, at both the national and state level. Losing candidates and parties, while they may allege problems in the conduct of a *specific* election, do not generally challenge the legitimacy of elections *as such* — they usually accept the overall outcome and the system that produced it.

### 4. Challenges that remain (a balanced note)
The chapter is honest that Indian elections, despite these strengths, are **not free of problems**: candidates and parties with a lot of money enjoy a big and unfair advantage over smaller parties/candidates; some candidates with criminal backgrounds continue to win elections; family members of existing leaders often have an added advantage; smaller/newer parties may face a real disadvantage compared to large, well-established ones. The chapter's overall conclusion, however, is that Indian elections remain **broadly free and fair**, and are an essential part of what makes India's democracy function.

## Key Words (Glossary)
| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Constituency | A geographical area whose voters elect one representative |
| Delimitation | The process of drawing/redrawing the boundaries of electoral constituencies |
| Electoral Roll (voters' list) | The official list of everyone entitled to vote in an election |
| Affidavit | A sworn, written legal statement, used here to declare a candidate's criminal cases, assets, and education |
| Model Code of Conduct | A set of agreed rules that parties and candidates must follow during an election campaign |
| EVM | Electronic Voting Machine, used in India to record and count votes electronically |
| Election Commission of India | The independent constitutional body responsible for conducting free and fair elections in India |

## Quick Recap (7 points to remember)
1. The "Aaya Ram Gaya Ram" story (Rao Birendra Singh, Haryana, 1967) frames the chapter's question: why do elections and representative democracy still matter?
2. A democratic election requires real choice, regular intervals, free and fair conduct, and equal value for every vote.
3. India's election process runs through several steps: constituencies (with **reserved seats** for SC/ST) → delimitation → electoral roll → nomination (with a mandatory **affidavit** since 2003) → campaign under the **Model Code of Conduct** → polling via **EVMs** → counting.
4. The **Election Commission of India** is an independent, powerful constitutional body that conducts and regulates all elections, and can even order re-polls.
5. **T.N. Seshan**, as CEC in the 1990s, is a well-known example of the Commission's independent authority.
6. Indian elections are considered broadly free and fair because of high popular participation and general acceptance of results — even when the ruling party loses.
7. Real challenges remain: the influence of money and criminal backgrounds in politics, and the advantage enjoyed by established parties/families over smaller ones.
